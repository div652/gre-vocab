# **arresting** *(adj.)* — **uh-RES-ting**

### Means
So striking that it **stops you** — a face, an image, a sentence, a performance that halts you mid-scroll.

The word is admiring but cool-headed. *Beautiful* praises the thing; **arresting** reports what the thing *did to you* — it seized your attention before you consented. That's why critics reach for it when something is compelling without being conventionally pretty: an **arresting** face may be odd, severe, asymmetric. Strength is high (much stronger than *noticeable* or *striking*), but the tone stays composed and slightly analytical — it's a reviewer's word, not a gushing one.

### Trick to lock it in
> An arresting image doesn't **ask** for your attention — it **detains** it.

It's literally *arrest* + *-ing*: your eye is stopped and held, with no say in the matter.

### In sentences
1. Among the gallery's polite landscapes hung one **arresting** portrait, and the room emptied around it.
2. She's got this **arresting** voice — you hear two seconds of it on the radio and you stop chopping onions.

### In the wild
Film-review blurbs live on this word — "an **arresting** debut", "an **arresting** performance" — precisely because it flatters a film without promising it's enjoyable.

### Where it comes from
Latin **restāre** = *"to stop, stand still,"* with *ad-* ("to") → Old French *arester*. To arrest a criminal is just to **stop** him; to arrest the spread of a disease is to halt it.

Same root, one useful oddity: **restive**. It once meant a horse that *refused to move* — and now means the opposite-looking *fidgety, restless, impatient*. Both senses are a creature that won't do what it's told.

# **chastise** *(v.)* — **chas-TEYEZ**

*The noun pulls the stress forward: chastisement = CHAS-tiz-muhnt.*

### Means
To **rebuke severely** — and, in older or biblical use, to **punish physically** for a fault.

Where *criticise* judges the work and *rebuke* just checks the behaviour, **chastise** comes with a moral high horse. The chastiser is above you and says so: a parent, a coach, a bishop, an editorial page. It's a telling-off delivered as correction — *for your own good* — and the older sense of a beating still hangs faintly on it, which is why the word feels heavier than a mere scolding.

⚠️ **chastise ≠ chasten.**
- **chastise** = *deliver* the scolding (active, someone does it to you)
- **chasten** = *humble* someone, usually the after-effect — "a **chastened** manager faced the press"

You chastise a player; the player walks off chastened.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Chastise** hides **chaste** — the punisher thinks he's purifying you, so it always comes down from above.

You chastise a child, a subordinate, or a government you disapprove of — never your boss.

### In sentences
1. The editorial **chastised** the council for approving the development without a single public hearing.
2. Mum **chastised** me for twenty solid minutes about the state of my room.

### In the wild
A newsroom staple: "**chastised**" is what headlines use when one powerful body publicly tells off another — *"IMF chastises government over spending."* If you see it, someone is being scolded from a height.

### Where it comes from
Latin **castus** = *"pure"* → **castigāre**, *"to make pure, to correct"* — punishment as purification.

Same root gives you **castigate** (to rip into someone verbally), **chasten**, **chaste**, **chastity** — and **incest**, literally *in-castus*, "not chaste."

# **cumbersome** *(adj.)* — **KUM-ber-suhm**

### Means
**Hard to handle** because it's too large, heavy, or awkwardly built — and by extension, any system so clunky and over-complicated that it slows everything down.

The complaint is about **friction, not pain**. Something *onerous* imposes real hardship; something **cumbersome** merely makes you struggle with it — the suitcase that won't fit through the door, the eight-step approval form that eventually works. It's mild, practical, faintly exasperated: the word of someone who got there in the end, but wishes they hadn't had to.

On the GRE it usually shows up in the figurative sense — cumbersome *machinery*, *bureaucracy*, *prose*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **burdensome, troublesome, tiresome, cumbersome** — the *-some* family all name a nuisance; cumbersome's nuisance is **bulk**.

Park it beside its rhymes and keep the distinguishing bit: the trouble is that the thing is too big or clunky to manage smoothly, not that it's painful or wrong.

### In sentences
1. The committee's **cumbersome** review procedure required four signatures before a single sentence could be published.
2. This laptop's fine, but the charger's so **cumbersome** I just leave it at home.

### In the wild
This is a workplace staple: *a cumbersome approval process*, *a cumbersome UI*, *cumbersome paperwork*. It's the standard polite complaint for something that technically works but makes you work for it.

### Where it comes from
From **cumber**, "to hinder, block up" (Old French *combrer*, from Late Latin *cumbrus*, a barrier or heap of rubble) + **-some**, "full of."

The useful payoff is the family: **encumber** (to weigh down), **encumbrance** (a burden — in law, a claim against property), and the GRE favourite **unencumbered** — free of baggage, financial or otherwise.

# **economy** *(n.)* — **ih-KON-uh-mee**

### Means
Beyond "the nation's finances," the sense the GRE tests is **sparing, efficient use of any resource** — words, movement, effort, materials. *An economy of gesture. Economy of style.*

Used this way it's almost always **praise**: it means nothing is wasted, not that little is spent. A stingy person practises *parsimony*; a good writer practises *economy* — same reduction, opposite verdict. It implies skill and control, the sense that everything left in is doing work.

⚠️ **economic ≠ economical.**
- **economic** = to do with the economy (*economic policy, economic growth*)
- **economical** = thrifty, sparing (*an economical little car*)

An economic decision affects GDP; an economical decision saves you money.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Economy isn't *less* — it's *no waste*.**

That distinction is why "economy of prose" is a compliment while "cheap prose" is an insult.

### In sentences
1. Vermeer's late paintings achieve their power through sheer **economy**: a single window, one figure, nothing else in the room.
2. Watch her cook — total **economy** of movement, not one wasted step between the fridge and the pan.

### In the wild
**Economy class** on a plane: the seat designed so that not one cubic inch is spent on anything you don't strictly need. Fly it once and the meaning of the word is in your knees.

### Where it comes from
Greek **oikos** (*house*) + **nomos** (*law, management*) → *oikonomia*, "household management" — running the home without waste. That's why the thrift sense is the original one and the national-finances sense came later.

Both halves pay off elsewhere: **oikos** gives **ecology** and **ecumenical**; **nomos** gives **autonomy**, **astronomy**, **taxonomy**, **gastronomy** — all "the rules governing X."

# **elementary** *(adj.)* — **el-uh-MEN-tuh-ree**

*Usually squeezed to el-uh-MEN-tree in fast speech.*

### Means
Concerned with the **first and simplest parts** of a subject — introductory, rudimentary, requiring no advanced knowledge.

Applied to *material*, it's flatly neutral: an elementary textbook, elementary arithmetic. Applied to *a person's mistake or question*, it turns barbed — "an elementary error" means one so basic you should be embarrassed to have made it. Same word, and the insult lives entirely in what you attach it to.

⚠️ **elementary ≠ elemental.**
- **elementary** = *basic, beginner-level* — an elementary mistake
- **elemental** = *raw, primal, of the fundamental forces* — elemental rage, elemental fury of the storm

Elementary is small and simple. Elemental is huge and primitive.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Elementary school** comes before everything else — and calling a grown-up's question *elementary* is telling them that's where they belong.

The school sense fixes the meaning (the very first level) and the sneer fixes the tone.

### In sentences
1. A grasp of **elementary** statistics is assumed before the seminar begins.
2. Mate, you forgot to plug it in — that's an **elementary** mistake.

### In the wild
**"Elementary, my dear Watson"** — Holmes's catchphrase in the films (not, in fact, in Conan Doyle's stories in that exact form), and the title of the Sherlock series *Elementary*. The tone is the point: he means *obvious to anyone paying attention*, which is why it stings.

### Where it comes from
Latin **elementum** = *a first principle, a rudiment* — and, in the plural, **the letters of the alphabet**, the very first thing a Roman child was taught. Elementary knowledge is literally your ABCs.

# **embellish** *(v.)* — **em-BEL-ish**

### Means
**1.** To make something prettier by adding decorative detail — beads on a dress, gold leaf on a manuscript, a flourish on a melody.
**2.** To make a story more interesting by adding details that aren't true.

Sense 2 is the GRE one, and its charge is mild. **Fabricate** invents the whole thing; **embellish** starts from something real and dresses it up. That's why it's the word people use when they're half-forgiving someone — a storyteller embellishes, a fraudster lies. Say a witness *embellished* her account and you've cast doubt without calling her a liar.

### Trick to lock it in
> **em-BELL-ish** — you put a **belle** on it: prettier, not truer.

The word hides *belle*, "beautiful woman," and that's exactly what embellishing does — improves the surface while leaving the truth underneath untouched.

### In sentences
1. The monks **embellished** the margins with gilt vines and small, improbable beasts.
2. He tells the story every Christmas and **embellishes** it a bit more each year — there were two robbers last time, now there are four.

### In the wild
Shop for a party dress online and half the listings say **"embellished"** — retail code for sequins, beading, or appliqué stitched onto a plain base.

### Where it comes from
Old French **embellir**, from **bel / beau** = *"beautiful"* — the same root behind **belle**, **beau**, **beauty**, **belladonna**, and Italian *bellissimo*.

🚫 **Not Latin *bellum*, "war."** Identical-looking root, opposite world: *bellicose, belligerent, antebellum, rebel*. One makes things pretty, the other starts fights.

# **euphoric** *(adj.)* — **yoo-FOR-ik**

### Means
Feeling **intense, soaring elation** — happiness dialled far past ordinary happiness.

Scale matters: *pleased* < *delighted* < *elated* < **euphoric**. This is the top of the ladder, and it's a *state*, not a temperament — nobody is euphoric by nature, only for an hour. The word also carries a faint clinical shadow: doctors use it for the high of morphine or mania, and journalists use it for the mood of a market right before it crashes. So calling someone euphoric can gently suggest the feeling is chemical, disproportionate, or about to end.

⚠️ **euphoric ≠ euphonic.**
- **euphoric** = feeling wonderful
- **euphonic** = *sounding* wonderful (pleasing to the ear — *euphony*)

A euphoric crowd; a euphonic choir.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-phor** is the *carry* ending — a **meta·phor** carries meaning across, eu·**phor**·ia carries *you* off the ground.

Anything carried has to be set down again, which is exactly why euphoria is a high that comes with a landing.

### In sentences
1. The delegates emerged **euphoric**, convinced that a treaty two decades in the making had finally been secured.
2. I was **euphoric** for about ten minutes after the results came out, then remembered I still had to move house.

### In the wild
HBO's **Euphoria** (Zendaya) — a series named for exactly this sense of the word: highs that are chemical, enormous, and never last.

### Where it comes from
Greek **eu** = *"good"* + **pherein** = *"to carry, to bear"* — literally *"carrying well."*

Both halves pay off across the GRE. **eu-**: *eulogy* (good words), *euphemism* (good speech), *euphony* (good sound), *euthanasia* (good death). **-phor/-pher**: *metaphor* (carry across), *periphery* (carried around), *phosphorus* (light-bearer).

# **exonerate** *(v.)* — **ig-ZON-uh-rayt**

*The x is a "gz" buzz, not "ks" — ig-ZON, never ek-SON.*

### Means
To **officially clear someone of blame** for something they were accused of.

The force of it is affirmative: an acquittal only says guilt wasn't proved, but an exoneration says the person **didn't do it**. It's the strong word — courts, inquiries, internal investigations, DNA labs. And it's about *blame*, not sin: a priest absolves you, an investigation exonerates you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nobody exonerates themselves.** A court, a report, or a lab test does it *for* you.

That's why the word lives in the passive — "was fully exonerated" — and why protesting your innocence never counts as exoneration.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal's final report **exonerated** the two officers, attributing the failure to a defect in the equipment itself.
2. Turns out the cat knocked the vase over, so I'm **exonerated** — you owe me an apology.

### In the wild
The **Innocence Project** headlines you see every few months — "man exonerated after 27 years by DNA evidence" — are the word's natural habitat, and they show the full sense: not released on a technicality, but proven innocent.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** (*off*) + **onus, oneris** (*burden*) — literally *to take the load off*. The blame was weight on your shoulders; exoneration lifts it.

Same root gives you **onerous** (*burdensome, heavy going*) and **onus** (*the burden of proof*) — both GRE regulars.

# **extrapolate** *(v.)* — **ik-STRAP-uh-layt**

### Means
To take what you know — a trend, a sample, a few data points — and **project it outward** into territory you have no data for. Also, loosely: to infer a general conclusion from a narrow piece of evidence.

The word always carries a whiff of risk. A calculation is certain; an extrapolation is a *bet* that the pattern holds where you can't see. In technical writing it's neutral bookkeeping ("extrapolate the curve to t=0"); in argument it's often a quiet accusation — "you're extrapolating from a sample of one." Assume, guess, and extrapolate all leap beyond the evidence, but only *extrapolate* claims a method for the leap.

⚠️ **extrapolate ≠ interpolate.**
- **interpolate** = estimate *between* two known points (inside the data)
- **extrapolate** = estimate *beyond* the known points (outside the data)

You interpolate Tuesday's temperature from Monday's and Wednesday's. You extrapolate next August's.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Extra** — you're drawing *extra* line past where the dots stop.

The data ends, the pencil keeps going — which is exactly why an extrapolation can be confidently, elegantly wrong.

### In sentences
1. From three fragmentary inscriptions the historian **extrapolated** an entire system of provincial taxation — persuasive, but resting on almost nothing.
2. Don't **extrapolate** from one good week and start telling people we've turned the business around.

### In the wild
Election night: with 8% of votes counted, the networks "project" a winner. That projection is pure extrapolation — and the famous misfires are what happens when the sample doesn't behave like the rest.

### Where it comes from
A modern coinage (1870s), built on **extra-** = *"outside, beyond"* — the same prefix in **extraordinary** (beyond the ordinary), **extraneous**, **extracurricular**, **extraterrestrial**.

The **-polate** half was simply borrowed from **interpolate** to make a matching opposite, so the prefix carries the whole meaning: *inter-* = between the data, *extra-* = beyond it.

# **falter** *(v. / n.)* — **FOL-ter**

*Rhymes with *alter*; the **l** is fully pronounced.*

### Means
To **lose strength, steadiness or momentum** part-way through something — to stumble in step, waver in resolve, or break and tremble in voice. As a noun, that break itself: *a falter in her voice*.

The key is that it's **involuntary and mid-motion**. You *hesitate* because you're weighing something; you *waver* because two options are pulling at you; you **falter** because the thing keeping you going — legs, nerve, funding, breath — is giving out. It also implies something was already underway and moving: an economy, a march, a speech, a career. And it's a wobble, not a stop — the faltering runner may still finish.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Falt**er = one letter from **fault** and one sound from **fall** — the step that faults, the voice about to fall apart.

Nobody chooses to falter; something structural gives way underneath you.

### In sentences
1. Halfway through the eulogy his voice **faltered**, and he gripped the lectern until he could go on.
2. She didn't **falter** for a second — walked straight in and asked for double.

### In the wild
Business and sports headlines run on this word: *talks faltered*, *the recovery faltered*, *United faltered in the second half*. It's the standard verb for something that was going well and started losing steam.

# **fervent** *(adj.)* — **FER-vuhnt**

### Means
Burning with feeling — intensely sincere, wholehearted, unembarrassed by its own heat.

The word almost always **approves**. A fervent believer, a fervent hope, a fervent supporter: the emotion is deep and genuine, and the speaker respects it. That's what separates it from *zealous* (which hints at excess) or *fanatical* (which condemns outright). Fervent heat is warmth, not danger — you'd happily call your own convictions fervent, which you'd never do with *rabid*. It also pairs habitually with the quiet, inward emotions: hope, prayer, wish, belief, admiration.

**fervent vs. fervid.** Same root, different temperature. **Fervent** = warmly sincere (approving). **Fervid** = overheated, feverish, slightly too much (often disapproving). A fervent plea moves you; a fervid one makes you back away.

### Trick to lock it in
> **FER-vent** = a **fever** with a **vent** — real heat, but let out safely.

Fervent feeling runs hot yet stays sincere and controlled, which is why the word praises rather than warns.

### In sentences
1. Her **fervent** defence of the accused clerk lasted an hour, and the committee, though unmoved, did not interrupt.
2. He's a **fervent** Arsenal fan — he's cried at more matches than at weddings.

### In the wild
Lincoln, Second Inaugural Address: *"Fondly do we hope, **fervently** do we pray, that this mighty scourge of war may speedily pass away."* Hope and prayer — the word's two natural companions, in one line.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fervēre** = *"to boil, to glow."* The heat is literal at the root and metaphorical in every descendant: **fervour** (boiling passion), **fervid** (overheated), **effervescent** (*ef-* out + boil — bubbling over), **ferment** (what boiling yeast does to sugar, and what unrest does to a country).

# **foment** *(v.)* — **foh-MENT**

### Means
To **stir up** trouble — unrest, rebellion, discord, resentment — and keep it going.

*Incite* is a spark: one speech, one moment, and the crowd moves. **Foment** is slow heat applied over time, usually from off-stage — the agitator working the room for months, the foreign power funding both sides. It carries blame: nobody foments by accident, and the object is always something bad. You can foment a mutiny, a strike, a panic; you cannot foment goodwill.

⚠️ **foment ≠ ferment.**
- **foment** (verb) = *deliberately stir up* trouble — someone is doing it
- **ferment** = to brew/bubble; as a noun, a state of agitation ("the country was in a **ferment**") — no agent required

Writing "ferment a rebellion" is a classic slip; the rebellion ferments on its own, but a person **foments** it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Foment rhymes with torment — and keeps the same bad company.**

Both are second-syllable verbs for inflicting something unpleasant on purpose, so if the object isn't ugly, the word is wrong.

### In sentences
1. Colonial administrators accused the pamphleteers of **fomenting** sedition among the garrison troops.
2. He wasn't in the argument — he just sat there **fomenting** it, feeding each side quotes from the other.

### In the wild
Standard diplomatic-accusation vocabulary: governments and news wires routinely charge rival states with "**fomenting** unrest" or "**fomenting** instability" — a phrase you'll meet in almost any foreign-affairs report.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fovēre** = *"to warm."* A **fomentum** was a **warm compress** laid on the skin — and *fomentation* is still the medical term for one. That's the whole image: you don't light the fire, you hold warmth against the spot until it inflames.

# **gaffe** *(n.)* — **GAF**

### Means
An embarrassing blunder — almost always something **said**, in public, in front of exactly the people who shouldn't have heard it.

Scale is the whole point. A *blunder* can lose a battle; a **gaffe** loses you a news cycle. The tone is wince, not condemnation — journalists reach for the word when they're half-enjoying the spectacle. And it's usually a slip of *tact*, not of manners: a **faux pas** means you breached etiquette, a **gaffe** means you let something tactless or inconveniently true escape your mouth.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Gaffe** rhymes with **laugh** — the room laughs, nobody dies.

That rhyme fixes the scale of the word: a gaffe costs you dignity, never the outcome.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador's **gaffe** — congratulating the minister on a promotion he had not yet received — was replayed on every evening bulletin.
2. I called his wife by his ex's name. Total **gaffe**. I'm never going to a dinner party again.

### In the wild
Journalist Michael Kinsley's famous line, now a standard bit of political shorthand: *"A gaffe is when a politician accidentally tells the truth."*

# **heterodox** *(adj.)* — **HET-uh-ruh-doks**

### Means
Holding beliefs that depart from the accepted or official doctrine — of a church, a discipline, a party.

The word lives in the world of **ideas**, not behaviour: you have heterodox *views*, not a heterodox haircut (that's *unorthodox*). And it stops short of **heretical** — a heterodox thinker is still inside the tent, arguing with the creed rather than renouncing it. Because of that, it carries a faint respectability: mainstream economists say "heterodox" about rivals they must take seriously, and dissenters happily claim the label for themselves.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ortho**dox keeps the *straight* docs; **hetero**dox reads the *other* docs — same religion, different book.

"Dox" is doctrine, and the prefix tells you whether you're on the official line or a rival one — not off the map altogether.

### In sentences
1. His **heterodox** reading of the treaty was dismissed by the faculty for a decade, then quietly adopted into the syllabus.
2. She's the **heterodox** one at the family dinner — still votes, still goes to church, just never the way anyone expects.

### In the wild
**Heterodox economics** is the standard name for the schools kept outside the mainstream — Marxian, Austrian, post-Keynesian. It's the label a whole profession uses for "not the official doctrine, but still economics."

### Where it comes from
Greek **heteros** = *"other"* + **doxa** = *"opinion, belief."* Literally: the other opinion.

*Doxa* is the payoff root: **orthodox** (*orthos*, straight — the correct belief), **paradox** (*para*, contrary — a belief against belief), **doxology** (words of praise). *Heteros* gives you **heterogeneous** — of other kinds, mixed.

# **histrionic** *(adj. (n. pl. histrionics))* — **his-tree-ON-ik**

### Means
Wildly theatrical; over-acted; emotion pitched for an audience rather than felt.

Calling behaviour **histrionic** is always a judgement, and the judgement is *insincerity*. You are saying the grief, the rage, the fainting fit was **staged** — there is a performer, and there is a crowd being played to. Contrast **dramatic** (neutral, can be a compliment) and **melodramatic** (overblown, but the feeling may be real). Histrionic strips the emotion of credibility.

The noun **histrionics** is the more common form: *"spare me the histrionics."*

⚠️ **histrionic ≠ hysterical.**
- **hysterical** = emotion genuinely **out of control**
- **histrionic** = emotion deliberately **put on for show**

The hysterical person can't stop; the histrionic one could stop the moment the audience left.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Histrionic = "his theatrics."**

The sound gives you the meaning and the sneer at once — you are attributing the drama to *him*, as a performance you are not buying.

### In sentences
1. The defence counsel's **histrionic** appeal to the jury — the trembling voice, the pause at the window — persuaded no one who had read the file.
2. He slammed the door, then opened it to slam it again; the whole **histrionic** routine lasted about ten minutes.

### In the wild
**Histrionic personality disorder** is a real DSM diagnosis — the clinical name for a pattern of attention-seeking theatricality and shallow, rapidly shifting emotion. Psychiatry picked the word precisely because *acting* is the core of it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **histrio** = *"actor"*. That's the whole word: histrionic behaviour is literally actor-behaviour.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *history*.** That's Greek *historia*, "inquiry" — a lookalike, unrelated.

# **implicit** *(adj.)* — **im-PLIS-it**

### Means
**1.** Understood without being said — implied, contained in something rather than stated by it. *An implicit criticism; an implicit assumption.*
**2.** (of trust, faith, obedience) **Absolute, unquestioning** — held so completely that no reservation is even voiced.

Sense 1 is the opposite of *explicit*: the meaning is there, but you have to draw it out. It's not vagueness — an implicit claim is definitely being made, just never put on the record, which is exactly why it's the useful word for arguments people won't admit to making.

Sense 2 is the GRE's favourite ambush. *Implicit trust* is not partial or unspoken trust; it's total trust. Same logic underneath — nothing is held back *separately*, so nothing qualifies it.

⚠️ **implicit ≠ complicit.**
- **implicit** = *implied*, unstated
- **complicit** = *involved* in the wrongdoing

A silence can be implicit agreement; a silence that helps the crime makes you complicit.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Im**plicit is folded **in** — you unfold it; **ex**plicit is laid **out** — spelled *ex*-actly.

Same root *plicāre* (to fold), and the prefix tells you whether the meaning is tucked inside or already on the table.

### In sentences
1. The report never accuses the ministry outright, but the charge of negligence is **implicit** in every paragraph.
2. She never actually said yes — but leaving her keys on the table was pretty **implicit**.'

### In the wild
**Implicit bias** — the standard term in every corporate training deck and hiring policy: prejudice nobody states, that shows up in the decisions anyway.

### Where it comes from
Latin **implicāre** = *"to fold in, entangle"* (**in-** + **plicāre**, *to fold*).

*Plicāre* is a workhorse root: **explicit** (folded out), **implicate** (fold someone in), **complicated** (folded together), **replicate** (folded back), **pliable**, **ply**, **apply**, **deploy**, **perplex**.

# **inviolate** *(adj.)* — **in-VEYE-uh-luht**

*The middle is "VEYE", as in violate — not "vee".*

### Means
Free from injury, breach, or desecration — **kept whole and untouched**, usually because something sacred or solemn protects it.

This is not the same as *intact*, which is merely physical and neutral. An **inviolate** thing has survived untouched because touching it was *forbidden*: a vow, a sanctuary, a border, a secret, a right. The word carries reverence — the speaker approves of the thing being untouched. It's high-register and slightly ceremonial; you meet it in constitutions, treaties, and elegies far more than in speech.

**inviolate vs. inviolable.**
- **inviolate** = *has not been* violated (a fact about the present state)
- **inviolable** = *cannot or must not be* violated (a claim about its protection)

A treaty can be **inviolable** in principle and still not remain **inviolate** in practice.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-ate** tells you the st**ate** it's in; **-able** tells you what it's **able** to withstand.

The suffix does all the work — *inviolate* reports that nothing has touched it, *inviolable* insists that nothing may.

### In sentences
1. For forty years the monastery library remained **inviolate**, its manuscripts unread by any hand outside the order.
2. We had one **inviolate** rule in that house: nobody talks about work at the dinner table.

### In the wild
Legal boilerplate you can find in a great many American state constitutions: *"the right of trial by jury shall remain **inviolate**."* Whenever a right is described as untouchable, this is the word the drafters reach for.

### Where it comes from
Latin **violāre** = *"to treat with force, to dishonour,"* from **vis** = *"force."* Add the negative *in-* and you get *"not forced open."* Same root: **violate, violence, violent, violation, inviolable**.

🚫 **Not *violet*.** The flower is Latin *viola*, an unrelated word — pure spelling coincidence.

---

# **liability** *(n.)* — **lye-uh-BIL-uh-tee**

### Means
**1.** A **legal or financial obligation** — a debt, a claim someone can enforce against you. (Plural on a balance sheet: **liabilities**, the money you owe.)
**2.** A **person or thing that costs you more than it gives** — the opposite of an asset.

Sense 2 is the GRE one, and it's colder than *burden* or *nuisance*. A nuisance annoys you; a liability **exposes** you — it creates risk you'll have to pay for. Calling a teammate a liability isn't quite an insult about character; it's an accounting verdict: on net, they're in the debit column. That borrowed-from-the-ledger flavour is what gives the word its clinical sting.

**liability ≠ libel.** *Libel* (LYE-bul) is defamation in **writing**. You can, of course, incur *liability* for *libel* — which is exactly why the two get tangled.

### Trick to lock it in
> **li-a-BIL-ity** — say it slowly and you say **a bill**.

A liability is whatever hands you a bill: a debt you owe, or a person whose mistakes you end up paying for.

### In sentences
1. The auditors found undisclosed **liabilities** buried in the subsidiary's accounts, wiping out the reported profit.
2. He's fast, sure, but he can't defend — at this level he's a **liability**.

### In the wild
Every **Ltd.** and **LLC** on a company name is short for *limited liability* — the whole point of the structure is that if the business goes under, creditors can take the company's money but not the founders' house.

### Where it comes from
From **liable**, most likely via Old French *lier* ← Latin **ligāre** = *"to bind."* A liability is what you are **bound** to — legally tied, unable to walk away.

Same root: **ligament** (binds bone to bone), **oblige** (bind toward), **liaison** (a binding-together), **ally**, **league**.

# **obstinate** *(adj.)* — **OB-stuh-nuht**

*Never ob-STY-nayt — the stress is right at the front and the ending mumbles away.*

### Means
Refusing to change your mind, your course, or your behaviour — and refusing past the point where anyone thinks it's reasonable.

The word carries a verdict. *Determined*, *resolute* and *steadfast* describe the same immovability with approval; **obstinate** is what the people you're blocking call it. It implies the resistance has stopped serving any purpose — you're holding out from sheer will, possibly against your own interests. It's also a notch more formal and colder than *stubborn*, which can be affectionate ("stubborn old mule"); obstinate rarely is.

It works on things too: an **obstinate** stain, an **obstinate** cough, an **obstinate** lid — anything that won't yield to repeated effort.

**obstinate ≠ obstreperous.** Both start *obst-*, both describe a difficult person — but **obstinate** is silently immovable, **obstreperous** (ub-STREP-er-uhs) is *noisily* unruly. The obstinate witness says nothing; the obstreperous one won't stop shouting.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's an **obstacle** hiding in **obstinate** — and it's a person.

Same *obst-*, same idea: this isn't admirable firmness, it's something standing in the way that you've given up trying to move.

### In sentences
1. Despite three days of testimony and a mountain of documents, the minister remained **obstinate** in his denial.
2. He's so **obstinate** — the restaurant's been closed for a year and he still won't admit I was right about the reservation.

### In the wild
Lady Catherine de Bourgh's parting shot at Elizabeth Bennet in *Pride and Prejudice*: **"Obstinate, headstrong girl!"** — kept word-for-word in the 2005 film. Note who says it: the person being defied, furious that she can't win.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob-** (*against*) + **stāre** (*to stand*) — literally *to stand against*. The obstinate man plants his feet.

**stāre** is one of the great GRE roots: **stable**, **status**, **stationary**, **constant**, **circumstance**, **obstacle** — everything that stands, stays, or gets in the way.

# **painstaking** *(adj.)* — **PAYNZ-tay-king**

*Parse it pains-taking, not pain-staking — there's a z sound, and no "stake" in it.*

### Means
Done with **extreme care and thoroughness**, attending to every small detail; of a person, willing to put in that grinding, unglamorous effort.

This is a compliment, and a slightly awed one — it credits *patience and stamina*, not talent. **Meticulous** describes the standard of the result; **painstaking** describes the labour behind it, the hours nobody saw. It attaches naturally to slow, fiddly work: a painstaking translation, a painstaking reconstruction, painstaking research. Call a five-minute job painstaking and it sounds sarcastic.

⚠️ **painstaking ≠ painful.** Painstaking work is *hard on the worker's patience* but praised; painful work simply hurts. The pain in *painstaking* is taken voluntarily.

### Trick to lock it in
> You **take pains** — you don't feel them. The pains are the price of the care.

Split the word at the right seam — *pains-taking* — and it stops meaning "agonising" and starts meaning "willing to go to trouble."

### In sentences
1. The catalogue is the product of forty years of **painstaking** archival work, each attribution traced to a surviving receipt.
2. She spent the whole weekend **painstakingly** peeling the old labels off, one corner at a time.

### In the wild
The phrase film studios and museums live by: *"painstakingly restored"* — the label on every 4K remaster and every cleaned-up old painting, doing the work of telling you how many quiet hours went into it.

### Where it comes from
From the old idiom **to take pains** = *to go to trouble*, where **pains** means effort, not hurt — the same sense in *"he was at pains to explain."*

Behind it is Latin **poena** = *penalty, price paid*, which also gives **penalty, penal, penitent, subpoena, punish** — and *pine* (to pine away). Effort as the price you pay is the thread.

# **phlegmatic** *(adj.)* — **fleg-MAT-ik**

*In phlegm the g is silent (FLEM), but in phlegmatic it comes back to life.*

### Means
Calm, unexcitable, hard to stir — sluggishly even-tempered by nature.

This is temperament, not achievement. *Stoic* means you endure pain without flinching; *serene* means an inner peace worth having. **Phlegmatic** means the alarm simply never went off — the person is constitutionally slow to feel anything much. That gives it a double edge: British writers use it admiringly ("a phlegmatic captain") for the man who doesn't panic, but it can just as easily mean torpid, unresponsive, a bit cold-blooded. Whether it's praise depends entirely on whether the situation deserved a reaction.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Phlegm** is thick, slow, sluggish stuff — and so is a **phlegmatic** person.

The word you already know for the goo gives you the tempo: nothing moves it quickly.

### In sentences
1. Shelling resumed at dawn, and the sergeant received the news with the same **phlegmatic** shrug he gave the weather.
2. I told him the flight was cancelled and he just kept eating — he's the most **phlegmatic** person I've ever met.

### Where it comes from
Greek **phlegma** = *"inflammation, clammy humour."* Medieval medicine held that your personality came from whichever of the four bodily humours you had most of — and each one is still an English word:

- **blood** → **sanguine** (cheerful, optimistic)
- **yellow bile** → **choleric** (hot-tempered)
- **black bile** → **melancholic** (gloomy)
- **phlegm** → **phlegmatic** (cool, sluggish, unmoved)

Learn the set once and you get four GRE words from one theory.

# **prodigious** *(adj.)* — **pruh-DIJ-uhs**

*Second syllable is a soft "j" — not "prod-ig-ee-us".*

### Means
Enormous in size, amount, or degree — and enormous in a way that **causes amazement**.

Big is a measurement; **prodigious** is a reaction. It carries the stare. A prodigious appetite isn't just large, it's large enough that people comment on it; a prodigious output means a body of work whose sheer volume seems barely human. It leans admiring even when the thing itself is unwelcome (*a prodigious hangover*), and it sits high in register — you'll meet it in essays, obituaries and match reports, rarely in casual speech.

**prodigious ≠ prodigal.**
- **prodigious** = astonishingly *large*
- **prodigal** = recklessly *wasteful* with money (the Prodigal Son blew his inheritance)

Spend prodigious sums and you're impressive; spend prodigally and you're broke.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Prodigy** is hiding inside **prodigious** — a prodigy is a child so far past normal that people gawk, and that's the size a prodigious thing is.

Both words demand an audience reaction, so if nobody would raise an eyebrow, it isn't prodigious.

### In sentences
1. Balzac's **prodigious** output — some ninety novels fuelled by fifty cups of coffee a day — killed him before he turned fifty.
2. He put away a **prodigious** amount of biryani for someone who claimed he wasn't hungry.

### In the wild
Sports commentary lives on this word: a batsman with **prodigious power**, a bowler getting **prodigious turn**, a golfer with a **prodigious** drive. Whenever the ball goes further than it has any right to, the commentator reaches for *prodigious*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prodigium** = *an omen, a portent, an unnatural sign* — something that made Romans stop and look up. That's why the word still means "huge enough to astonish" rather than plain "huge", and it links straight to **prodigy**.

🚫 **Not the root of *prodigal*.** That's *prodigere*, "to drive forth, squander" — same first four letters, opposite idea.

# **propensity** *(n.)* — **pruh-PEN-suh-tee**

### Means
A **built-in leaning** toward a particular behaviour — an inclination you keep acting on, usually without choosing to.

A *tendency* is a plain observation; a *penchant* is a taste you enjoy indulging (a penchant for red wine). A **propensity** is diagnostic — it sounds like a trait someone has been assessed and found to have, which is why lawyers, psychologists and statisticians reach for it. And because it's the word used for things people can't help, it tilts toward faults: propensities are for violence, for exaggeration, for spending, far more often than for kindness.

⚠️ **propensity ≠ propinquity.**
- **propensity** = a leaning *toward a behaviour*
- **propinquity** = nearness in *space or kinship* (the propinquity effect: you befriend whoever sits closest)

### Trick to lock it in
> Listen for what follows it: *a propensity for violence, for lying, for overspending* — the word walks in ahead of a flaw.

The collocation carries the connotation: if you can't imagine the phrase as a compliment, you've got the register right.

### In sentences
1. The report noted the firm's **propensity** to under-price risk in precisely the quarters when regulators were not looking.
2. He's got a **propensity** for texting his ex at 2am, and no amount of advice has ever fixed it.

### In the wild
Courts fight constantly over **propensity evidence** — proof that a defendant has done this sort of thing before. It's generally *inadmissible*, precisely because a jury hearing "he has a propensity for theft" will convict on the character rather than the facts. That's the word's whole flavour in one rule.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pro-** (*forward*) + **pendere** (*to hang, to weigh*) → *propendere*, "to lean toward." You are already hanging in that direction before the situation arises.

*pendere* is a workhorse root: **pendulum**, **pending**, **suspend**, **append**, **pensive** (weighing things in your mind), and **ponder**.

# **qualm** *(n.)* — **KWAHM**

*The L is silent, exactly as in calm and palm; you'll also hear KWAWM.*

### Means
A **twinge of doubt or unease** about whether what you're doing is right — a flicker of conscience that makes you hesitate.

Size and direction are what define it. A qualm is small and internal: not guilt (which comes *after*), not remorse (heavier, longer), not an objection (which you'd say out loud). It's the private wobble *before* you act. And it lives mostly in the negative — **"no qualms about"** is the phrase you'll meet nine times out of ten, meaning someone did the thing without so much as a flinch.

A second, older sense survives: a sudden wave of faintness or nausea.

### Trick to lock it in
> **qualm** rhymes with **calm** — and a qualm is the little ripple that breaks it.

The rhyme fixes the silent L *and* the scale: a qualm disturbs your calm, it doesn't destroy it.

### In sentences
1. The committee approved the merger without a **qualm**, untroubled by the four hundred jobs it would quietly erase.
2. I had no **qualms** about eating the last slice — nobody had labelled it.

### In the wild
Boardroom and news English runs on the negative: a lawyer, a hiring manager or a pundit will say a company **"had no qualms about"** doing something ruthless. Hear the phrase and you know the speaker disapproves.

### Where it comes from
Old English **cwealm** = *plague, torment, death* — and the word's first modern use was a sudden fit of physical sickness. The moral sense grew straight out of that: a qualm is **nausea of the conscience**.

# **renege** *(v.)* — **ri-NEG**

*Also heard as ri-NEEG; both are standard. Never "REN-eg."*

### Means
To **go back on a promise, deal, or commitment** you already made. It almost always takes *on*: you renege **on** a pledge, a contract, an agreement.

The word needs a prior commitment — you can't renege on something you never promised. That's what separates it from simply refusing or failing: the other side had your word and arranged their affairs around it. Milder and more procedural than *betray*, cooler than *double-cross*, but it still carries a charge of bad faith — it's the verb of choice for governments abandoning treaties and buyers walking away from signed offers.

### Trick to lock it in
> Re-**neg**-e = you **negate** what you already said.

The **neg** sitting in the middle is the same *neg* as *negative* and *negate* — the promise existed, and you cancelled it.

### In sentences
1. The government **reneged** on the treaty within a year of signing it, leaving the smaller states with no recourse.
2. He said he'd cover my shift and then **reneged** the night before.

### In the wild
**"Renegades"** by X Ambassadors — a *renegade* is literally one who has reneged: someone who denied their oath and walked out on their side.

### Where it comes from
Latin **re-** ("back") + **negāre** ("to deny") — so, *to deny back*, to un-say your yes.

*negāre* is a workhorse root: **negate, negative, deny, abnegate, renegade**.

# **stinting** *(adj. / v. (pres. part. of *stint*))* — **STIN-ting**

### Means
Giving **sparingly**, holding back, rationing out less than is wanted or deserved. To **stint** on something is to skimp on it; to **stint** someone is to keep them short.

This isn't thrift you admire — it's withholding something that ought to have flowed freely: praise, effort, food, money, time. A budget airline is *economical*; a host who serves one sandwich to twelve guests is **stinting**. Note where you'll actually meet it: nine times out of ten in the negative — *without stinting*, *never stinted*, **unstinting** support. So when the bare positive form shows up on the exam, read it as grudging rationing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Stint** and **stunt** are the same word wearing different vowels — both cut something short before it reaches full size.

A stunted tree never got its full growth; a stinting host never gave you your full share.

### In sentences
1. The committee was **unstinting** in its praise, devoting three full pages to her contribution.
2. Don't **stint** on the garlic — nobody's ever complained about too much.

### In the wild
You already use the noun: *"I did a two-year **stint** at Deloitte."* A stint is a rationed, measured-out spell of time — the same idea of a portion cut to size.

### Where it comes from
Old English **styntan** = *"to blunt, to make dull, to cut short."* Blunt it → shorten it → give less of it. The same root surfaces as **stunt** (to check something's growth), which is why the two feel related — they are.

# **temper** *(v. / n.)* — **TEM-per**

### Means
**Verb (the GRE sense):** to **moderate something strong by mixing in its opposite** — justice tempered with mercy, ambition tempered by caution.
**Verb (technical):** to **toughen** metal or glass by controlled heating and cooling.
**Noun:** your habitual state of mind (*an even temper*), or the loss of it (*lost his temper*).

To temper is not to cancel. Nothing is deleted — a counterweight is added until the thing sits in proportion, so the result is *balanced*, not *diluted*. That's why the metal sense isn't a contradiction: tempered steel isn't softer steel, it's steel brought into balance and made harder to shatter. And note the pull of the verb's usual objects — you temper enthusiasm, expectations, criticism, zeal: always something running hot.

⚠️ **temper ≠ tamper.**
- **temper** = moderate it, bring it into balance
- **tamper (with)** = meddle with it, interfere improperly

A jury's verdict can be tempered by mercy; a juror can be tampered with.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Temper** hides **temp**erature — you don't kill the heat, you *regulate* it.

Both senses run through that one idea: the smith tempers the blade by controlling the heat rather than removing it, and you temper a claim by dialling it down, not dropping it.

### In sentences
1. The judge **tempered** the severity of the sentence with an acknowledgment of the defendant's youth.
2. Great news, but let's **temper** expectations — one good quarter isn't a turnaround.

### In the wild
The **tempered glass** screen protector on your phone — same word, and it shows the sense the definition hides: tempering made that glass *tougher*, not weaker.

### Where it comes from
Latin **temperāre** = *"to mix in due proportion."* That proportion-mixing idea fans out into a family worth owning: **temperature** (the degree of heat), **temperate** (a mild climate, a moderate person), **temperance** (restraint in drink), **temperament** (your personal mix), **distemper** (a bad mix — disorder, disease).

# **tentative** *(adj.)* — **TEN-tuh-tiv**

### Means
Two linked senses:

**1. Provisional** — arranged but not settled, and everyone knows it may change: *a tentative agreement, a tentative date, tentative conclusions.*
**2. Hesitant** — done without confidence, as if testing whether the ground holds: *a tentative step, a tentative knock, a tentative smile.*

Sense 1 is neutral and businesslike — a placeholder, pencil rather than pen, and no criticism is implied. Sense 2 is about manner, and there the word quietly reports timidity: someone who sounds tentative in a meeting has lost the room. Note the difference from *reluctant*: a tentative person isn't unwilling, just unsure — they're going ahead, only lightly.

⚠️ **tentative ≠ tenuous.**
- **tentative** = *not final* — it may still change
- **tenuous** = *not strong* — thin, flimsy, barely holding (*a tenuous grip, a tenuous connection*)

A tentative alliance may be rock solid once signed; a tenuous one is already fraying.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A tent isn't a house.** You pitch it, and you can un-pitch it tomorrow.

*Tentative* opens with **tent** — anything tentative is pitched rather than built, standing only until someone decides otherwise.

### In sentences
1. The two governments announced a **tentative** settlement, subject to ratification by both parliaments.
2. He gave the door a **tentative** knock, half hoping nobody was in.

### In the wild
Every Outlook or Google Calendar invite gives you three buttons: **Accept**, **Decline**, and **Tentative** — the "probably, don't hold me to it" answer.

### Where it comes from
Latin **temptāre / tentāre** = *"to try, to test, to feel out."*

Same root gives **attempt** (to try) and **temptation** (something testing you) — and **tentacle**, literally a *feeler*. Picture how an octopus arm moves and you have sense 2 exactly: reaching out to test before committing.

# **unprecedented** *(adj.)* — **un-PRES-uh-den-tid**

*Stress lands on PRES, like precedent — never "un-pre-SEED-ented", even though it comes from precede.*

### Means
Never done, known, or seen before — there is **no earlier case of it on record**.

Strictly, it's a claim about the historical record, not about size or drama: a thing can be tiny and still unprecedented. News and marketing have worn it down into a vague intensifier meaning "huge," but the GRE will use it literally — *no prior instance*. Note also that it says nothing about likelihood: an event can be fully predicted and still unprecedented, because prediction looks forward and precedent looks back.

⚠️ **unprecedented ≠ unpredicted.** *Unprecedented* = it has never happened before. *Unpredicted* = nobody saw it coming. Epidemiologists predicted a pandemic for years; when it came, it was still unprecedented in living memory.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Un-PRECEDENT-ed** — a judge with no precedent has no case to cite.

The whole word is just *precedent* wrapped in negatives, so it points at the empty archive, not at how big or shocking the thing is.

### In sentences
1. The court's ruling was **unprecedented**: no bench had ever extended the doctrine to private contracts.
2. Three sick days in one week? For him that's **unprecedented** — the man came in with a broken wrist once.

### In the wild
2020 made it a punchline: every brand email on earth opened with "in these **unprecedented** times," until the phrase became a joke about corporate filler.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae-** (*before*) + **cedere** (*to go*) — a precedent is literally the thing that "went before."

That root is a GRE goldmine: **precede, recede, secede, concede, accede, antecedent, cede, procedure**.

# **vivacious** *(adj.)* — **vih-VAY-shus**

### Means
Lively and high-spirited in an attractive, sparkling way — full of animation in company.

The energy here is **social**, not physical: a vivacious person talks, laughs, lights up a room. An athlete is *energetic*; a hostess is *vivacious*. It's warmer than **lively** and less exhausting than **exuberant**, and it carries a whiff of charm — you enjoy being near a vivacious person. Note the usage skew: it's applied to women far more often than men, which is why it can read as slightly dated or faintly patronising depending on who says it.

⚠️ **vivacious ≠ voracious.**
- **vivacious** = *bubbling with life* (viv- = live)
- **voracious** = *ravenously hungry* — for food, or for books

A vivacious reader is fun at parties; a voracious reader gets through four books a week.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Viva**-cious — she *is* the "viva!" in the room.

The word literally opens with a cheer, which is the right register: it's sparkle and charm, not stamina.

### In sentences
1. Her letters are as **vivacious** as her conversation — every page seems to be talking over the last one.
2. He's fine one-on-one, but put him next to his **vivacious** sister and he vanishes.

### In the wild
Coldplay's **"Viva la Vida"** — *"long live life."* Same **viv-** root; a vivacious person is that title in human form.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vīvere** = *"to live"* → **vīvax**, "lively, long-lived."

One of the best-value GRE roots: **vivid** (life-like), **revive**, **survive**, **vital**, **convivial** (living *together* = good company), **vivisection** (cutting the living).
