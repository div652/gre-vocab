# **abreast** *(adv. / adj.)* — **uh-BREST**

*Rhymes with rest, not beast.*

### Means
**Literally:** side by side and facing the same way, in a single line — *three abreast*, *marching abreast*.

**Figuratively (with *of*):** level with something that keeps moving — *keep abreast of the research, of the law, of your rivals*.

The figurative sense is not simply "informed." It carries the sense of a race you can lose: the subject moves on, and you have to keep pulling level with it. Saying you're *abreast of* developments quietly claims ongoing effort, which is why it belongs to briefings, journalism and annual reviews rather than casual talk — nobody says they're abreast of their friend's gossip.

### Trick to lock it in
> If you're abreast **of** it, it's moving — the news, the field, the rivals.

The word takes *of* only for things that keep advancing, which is exactly why it means "not falling behind" rather than merely "knowing."

### In sentences
1. Compliance officers are expected to keep **abreast of** amendments to the statute as they are gazetted.
2. Walk single file here — you can't go three **abreast** on a path this narrow.

### In the wild
Cyclists "riding **two abreast**" — the phrase every driver, road sign and highway-code argument uses for bikes side by side in one lane.

### Where it comes from
**a-** ("on, in") + **breast** — chests in one line, nobody's ahead. That *a-* prefix is a whole GRE family: **afoot**, **aloft**, **ashore**, **askew**, **awry**.

# **confound** *(v.)* — **kuhn-FOWND**

### Means
Three linked senses, all on the exam:
**1.** to **bewilder** someone completely — confusion so total it stops thought.
**2.** to **defeat or frustrate** something expected — *the result confounded the polls*.
**3.** to **mix up two things** that should be kept apart — *don't confound correlation with cause*.

*Confuse* is ordinary and personal; *confound* is grander and usually has a **winner**. When something confounds you, it hasn't merely muddled you — it has outmatched you. That's why sense 2 works so cleanly: predictions, critics, and doctors get confounded, and the thing doing the confounding comes out on top.

⚠️ **confound ≠ compound.** To **compound** a problem is to *add* to it, make it worse. To **confound** it is to *baffle* or *defeat* it. One letter, opposite direction.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Con-FOUND: the con artist FOUND a way — you're not just puzzled, you're beaten.**

The hidden *found* keeps the winner in the picture, which is what separates *confound* from plain *confuse*.

### In sentences
1. The novel's ending **confounded** every critic who had confidently predicted a tidy reconciliation.
2. He was supposed to lose in straight sets, and he **confounded** the lot of us.

### In the wild
Any paper you read has a **confounding variable** — the lurking third factor that pours cause and effect together so you can't tell which is which. Sense 3, doing daily work in statistics.

### Where it comes from
Latin **con-** (*together*) + **fundere** (*to pour*) — literally *to pour together* until nothing can be told apart.

That root is a goldmine: **confuse** (the same word, worn smooth), **fuse**, **infuse**, **diffuse**, **profuse**, **effusive**, **transfusion**, **refund** (*pour back*).

# **digression** *(n.)* — **dye-GRESH-uhn**

*Also dih-GRESH-uhn; both standard. Verb: digress (dye-GRES).*

### Means
A **temporary departure from the main subject** in speech or writing — you wander off, then (usually) come back.

The word assumes there *is* a main road. That's why it's mild: a digression isn't an error or a failure of logic, just a detour, and it's often affectionate or self-aware — writers announce their own. Compare **tangent** (more casual, and you may never return), **rambling** (pure criticism, no structure implied), and **excursus** (a formal, deliberate side-essay). Calling a lecture "a series of digressions" is a gentle complaint; calling it "rambling" is not gentle at all.

⚠️ **digression ≠ transgression.**
- **digression** = you step *aside* from the topic
- **transgression** = you step *across* a moral or legal line

One derails a lecture; the other gets you fired.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A digression is a *detour*, not a wrong turn — you rejoin the road.**

Both start with *d* and both leave the main route, but a detour is understood to be temporary — which is exactly why the word is forgiving rather than damning.

### In sentences
1. The essay's long **digression** on medieval bookbinding is charming, but it stalls the argument for six full pages.
2. Sorry, that was a total **digression** — where were we?

### In the wild
The stock phrase **"But I digress"** — the little apology writers and speakers drop before steering back to the point. If you've heard someone say it, you already know the word means *I wandered off, and I'm coming back*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **digredi** = *"to step away"* — **di-** (*apart*) + **gradi** (*to step, to walk*).

**gradi** is a GRE workhorse: **progress** (step forward), **regress** (step back), **egress** (step out), **ingress** (step in), **gradual** (step by step), **grade** (a step in a series), **aggressive** (stepping *at* someone).

# **discrepancy** *(n.)* — **dis-KREP-uhn-see**

*Stress on KREP, not on DIS — never "DIS-kreh-pan-see."*

### Means
A **difference between two things that were supposed to match** — two figures, two accounts, two testimonies, the label and the contents.

The word is quietly accusatory without naming a culprit. A *difference* is just a fact; a **discrepancy** is a difference where agreement was expected, so it implies something has gone wrong — sloppiness, error, or a lie — while staying scrupulously neutral about which. That is why auditors, journalists and lawyers love it: it flags the problem and postpones the blame.

⚠️ **discrepancy ≠ disparity.**
- **discrepancy** = two accounts that *should agree* don't (the ledger vs. the receipts)
- **disparity** = an *unequal gap* between two things, no agreement ever expected (wealth disparity, a disparity in size)

### Trick to lock it in
> There is no discrepancy without a **pair that was meant to tally**.

If the two things were never supposed to be identical, what you have is a difference or a disparity — the word only fires when a match was expected.

### In sentences
1. The auditors flagged a **discrepancy** of £40,000 between the reported revenue and the deposits actually received.
2. There's a **discrepancy** between what he told me and what he told you, and I'd like to know which of us he lied to.

### In the wild
It's the standard word on a stock-take or a bank reconciliation: retail and finance teams literally file a **"discrepancy report"** when the count on the shelf doesn't match the count in the system.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dis-** (*apart*) + **crepāre** (*to rattle, creak, make a noise*) → **discrepāre**, "to sound different, to be out of tune." Two accounts that don't ring the same note.

Same root gives you **decrepit** — creaked out, worn to rattling.

# **duplicitous** *(adj.)* — **doo-PLIS-i-tuhs**

### Means
Deceitful in a **two-faced** way: saying one thing while doing or intending another, often to two audiences at once. (Noun: **duplicity** — not "duplicitousness.")

A liar tells one falsehood; a **duplicitous** person runs a sustained double game — the friendly face to you, the knife behind it. It implies system and premeditation, not a slip. The word is formal and openly damning: you don't call someone duplicitous in passing, you call them it when you've decided they're rotten.

⚠️ **duplicitous ≠ duplicative.**
- **duplicitous** = *two-faced, deceitful*
- **duplicative** = *merely repetitive, redundant*

A duplicative report wastes your time. A duplicitous colleague wrote it to cover his tracks.

### Trick to lock it in
> **DUPE** is sitting right at the front — and it takes **two** faces (*dupli-*) to dupe you properly.

The doubling is the point: one mouth for each audience, which is what separates duplicity from an ordinary lie.

### In sentences
1. The memo revealed a **duplicitous** strategy: reassuring regulators in public while lobbying to gut the very rules in private.
2. He was all smiles at lunch and then trashed her in the meeting — completely **duplicitous**.

### In the wild
The 2009 film **Duplicity** — Clive Owen and Julia Roberts as corporate spies who spend the whole movie double-crossing everyone, including each other. The title is the plot.

### Where it comes from
Latin **duplex, duplic-** = *"twofold, double."* Same root gives **duplex**, **duplicate**, **duplicity**.

Zoom out to **-plic-/-plex-** (*plicāre*, "to fold") and you unlock a GRE family: **complicated** (folded together), **implicate** (folded in), **explicit** (folded out, so plain), **replicate**, **perplex**.

# **expedient** *(adj. / n.)* — **ik-SPEE-dee-uhnt**

### Means
**Adjective:** convenient and effective for getting a result right now — whether or not it is fair, honest, or wise.
**Noun:** a makeshift means to an end (*a temporary expedient*).

The word almost always leaves a stain. Calling a decision **prudent** praises the judgement behind it; calling it **expedient** quietly says the person did what worked for *them* and left principle out of it. Note the giveaway collocations — *politically expedient*, *merely expedient*, *it was expedient to* — each one setting up a reason someone should have known better. In the neutral noun sense it just means a stopgap, but even there it admits the fix is second-best.

⚠️ **expedient ≠ expeditious.**
- **expedient** = convenient, self-serving — a judgement on the *ethics*
- **expeditious** = prompt and efficient — a judgement on the *speed*

An expeditious refund is fast. An expedient refund is the one they issued to stop you talking to a journalist.

### Trick to lock it in
> An **expedient** gets your feet out of the trap; it never promises your hands stay clean.

The buried *ped-* ("foot") makes it an escape route, and an escape route is judged by whether it works, not by whether it was honourable.

### In sentences
1. The minister abandoned the pledge the moment it ceased to be **expedient**, and dressed the retreat up as sober realism.
2. We propped the shelf up with a phone book — an ugly **expedient**, but it held.

### In the wild
**"Politically expedient"** is a newsreader's stock phrase, and it is never a compliment — it means the move polls well and costs the country something.

### Where it comes from
Latin **expedīre** = *"to free the feet"* (**ex-** "out" + **pēs, pedis** "foot") — to get someone unstuck.

Its opposite is built the same way: **impede** = *in-* + *pedis*, to shackle the feet. One root, two directions: **expedite, expedient, impede, impediment**, plus **pedestrian, pedal, pedigree**.

# **fabricate** *(v.)* — **FAB-ri-kayt**

### Means
**1.** To manufacture or construct something, especially from prepared parts — *fabricate a steel beam*.
**2.** To invent something false and pass it off as real — a story, an alibi, evidence, a quotation.

The second sense is the GRE one, and it's harsher than *lie*. To lie is to say one false thing; to **fabricate** is to *build* a falsehood — assemble it, give it details, make it hold together under inspection. That's why it attaches to nouns like *evidence*, *testimony*, *sources*, *records*: things that took work to fake. **Forge** is its exact twin in this double life — you forge metal, and you forge a signature.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fabric**ate hides **fabric** — you weave cloth from thread, or you weave a story from nothing.

Both senses are workmanship: one produces an object, the other produces a lie with all its seams sewn shut.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal found that the officer had **fabricated** his contemporaneous notes some weeks after the arrest.
2. He didn't just forget my birthday — he **fabricated** an entire flight delay, with a gate number.

### In the wild
**Prefab** housing — *pre-fabricated*, built in parts off-site — is the same word doing its honest day job, and a useful reminder that sense 1 is the literal one.

### Where it comes from
Latin **faber** = *"craftsman, smith"*, giving **fabrica**, *"workshop, a thing made."*

That one root branches twice into English: **fabric** (the woven thing) and **fabricate** (the making). And *fabrica* went through French to become **forge** — which is why *forge* carries the identical build-it/fake-it split.

# **glum** *(adj.)* — **GLUM**

### Means
Visibly downcast — low-spirited, sullen, and usually **quiet** about it.

Scale matters: *despondent* and *morose* are heavy words for real despair, while **glum** is everyday, mild, and often temporary — the mood of someone whose team lost, whose plans got cancelled, whose Monday is going badly. It's also the most *readable* of the sad words: you can be secretly miserable, but glum shows on the face and flattens the room. Slightly sulky at the edges, so it can carry a faint note of "cheer up."

**glum ≠ grim.** *Glum* is sad and deflated; *grim* is harsh, forbidding, severe. A glum face wants comforting; a grim face wants obeying.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Glum** rhymes with **mum** — the glum one has gone quiet.

The rhyme carries the nuance: glumness is silent, sulky low spirits, not loud grief.

### In sentences
1. The delegates filed out **glum** and silent, the treaty unsigned for a third straight year.
2. He sat **glum** on the sofa with the takeaway menu, still sulking about the cancelled trip.

### Where it comes from
From Middle English **gloumen**, *"to look sullen, to frown."* Same root-cluster as **gloom**, **gloomy**, **glower** (to scowl), and **gloaming** (twilight) — the *gl-* words for darkening skies and darkening faces.

# **harbinger** *(n. (occasionally v.))* — **HAR-bin-jer**

*The g is soft, as in ginger — never "har-BING-er".*

### Means
A person or thing that **goes ahead and signals what is coming** — a forerunner, an advance sign.

A harbinger is never the event itself, only its announcement: the first robin is a harbinger of spring, not spring. And though the dictionary is neutral, usage leans dark — the word's default company is *doom*, *disaster*, *decline*, *recession*. Say something is a harbinger and people brace. Next to it, **omen** is superstitious (the universe is warning you), **herald** is grander and happily positive, and **precursor** is flat and technical. *Harbinger* is the literary, headline-writing choice — elevated enough that you write it far more often than you say it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Harbinger = herald** — both start with "h-er/har-", both arrive first, and neither one *is* the news.

Lock the pair together and you keep the essential point: a harbinger only announces the thing, which is why it works as a warning rather than a fact.

### In sentences
1. The collapse of two regional banks was, in hindsight, a **harbinger** of the crisis that swallowed the decade.
2. Three grey hairs in one week — a **harbinger** of thirty, apparently.

### In the wild
Financial and political journalism runs on it: any week the markets wobble, some headline calls it *a harbinger of recession*. That collocation — **harbinger of** + something bad — is how you'll actually meet the word.

### Where it comes from
Old French **herberge** = *"lodging, shelter."* A medieval **harbinger** was the officer sent ahead of an army or a royal progress to book the lodgings — the man who arrived first so the king could arrive next.

Same root as **harbour/harbor**, the shelter a ship runs to. Once you see the advance man riding ahead, the modern sense is automatic.

# **intrinsic** *(adj.)* — **in-TRIN-zik**

*Also heard as in-TRIN-sik; both are standard.*

### Means
Belonging to a thing **by its own nature** — part of what it *is*, not something attached to it from outside.

The word is defined by its opposite. *Intrinsic* is always doing the work of separating the thing from its wrapping: the intrinsic value of gold vs. its market price, intrinsic motivation vs. a bonus cheque. Where *innate* is about being born with something and *inherent* just says "built in," *intrinsic* implies someone has stripped away the external stuff to see what survives. It's cool, analytical vocabulary — economists, philosophers and psychologists reach for it, and it carries no praise or blame on its own.

⚠️ **intrinsic ≠ extrinsic.** One prefix apart, exact opposites: **intrinsic** = from within the thing, **extrinsic** = from outside it. Money is an extrinsic reward; the joy of the work is intrinsic.

### Trick to lock it in
> **IN**trinsic starts with **IN** — take everything off and what's still *in* there is intrinsic.

The word only matters when something external has been stripped away — the price tag, the prize, the packaging — leaving whatever the thing owns by itself.

### In sentences
1. The court held that the manuscript's **intrinsic** worth as a historical document far exceeded whatever it might fetch at auction.
2. Nobody's paying her to fix that code at midnight — she just finds it **intrinsically** satisfying.

### In the wild
**Intrinsic motivation** is the standard term in psychology and management for doing something because the thing itself is rewarding — as opposed to the extrinsic bonus, grade or promotion bolted onto it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **intrinsecus** = *"inwardly, on the inside"* (from *intra*, "within"), against **extrinsecus**, *"outwardly."* The whole pair comes over intact: **intra-** gives you *intramural, intravenous, intranet*; **extra-** gives you *extraneous, extracurricular, extraterrestrial*.

# **largesse** *(n.)* — **lar-JESS**

*Also spelled largess; you'll hear LAR-jess too, but lar-JESS is standard.*

### Means
Generous giving — money, gifts, favours — handed out on a grand scale; also the generous spirit behind it.

The word carries a **height difference**. Largesse flows *downward*: from the rich to the dependent, the state to the citizen, the patron to the artist. Plain *generosity* can pass between equals; largesse implies the giver could stop at any time and everyone knows it. That's why it turns sour so easily in print — "living on his father's largesse," "corporate largesse" — half compliment, half sneer at the person doing the receiving.

### Trick to lock it in
> **largesse** rhymes with **noblesse** — and it's exactly what *noblesse oblige* obliges you to do.

The rhyme drags in the nobleman, which is the whole point: largesse is giving from above, not giving between equals.

### In sentences
1. The museum's new wing stands as a monument to one banker's **largesse** — and, engraved above the door, to his name.
2. We only got the good coffee machine because someone in Finance had a rare fit of **largesse**.

### In the wild
Political and business journalism runs on this word: "government largesse," "taxpayer largesse," "corporate largesse" — always with a raised eyebrow at who's footing the bill.

### Where it comes from
Latin **largus** = *"abundant, plentiful"* → French **largesse**. The same root gives you **large**, **enlarge**, and musical **largo** — literally "broad," hence slow and spacious.

# **libertine** *(n. / adj.)* — **LIB-er-teen**

### Means
**Noun:** someone who lives without moral restraint, especially in sex and drink — a debauchee.
**Adjective:** characterised by that unrestraint (*libertine habits, a libertine court*).

The word wears a costume. *Pervert* is clinical and disgusted, *womaniser* is small and modern, *sleaze* is grubby — but a **libertine** has style: wit, money, poetry, a philosophy of pleasure that he'd happily defend over wine. There's often a flicker of admiration in it, or at least amusement. Calling a man a libertine places him in a candlelit drawing room, not a courtroom.

⚠️ **libertine ≠ libertarian.** A **libertarian** wants the *state* off his back (politics). A **libertine** wants *morality* off his back (bedroom). Same root, very different dinner party.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A libertine takes *liberties*.**

The word "liberty" is sitting right inside it, and the freedom he claims is always at somebody else's expense.

### In sentences
1. Rochester's verse scandalised the court precisely because a **libertine** wrote it with such elegance.
2. He talks like a philosopher, but he's just a **libertine** with a good vocabulary.

### In the wild
Johnny Depp played the Earl of Rochester in **The Libertine** (2004) — the Restoration poet who drank and whored his way to death at 33. The film opens with him saying *"You will not like me."*

### Where it comes from
Latin **liber** = *"free."* A Roman **libertinus** was a *freedman* — an ex-slave, free but never quite respectable. English first used *libertine* for a **freethinker in religion**, then the freedom slid from belief to behaviour.

That root is everywhere: **liberty, liberal, liberate, libertarian**, and — via French — **deliver** (*de-liberare*, to set free).

# **malfeasance** *(n.)* — **mal-FEE-zuhns**

### Means
Wrongdoing — specifically **an illegal or dishonest act committed by someone holding public office or a position of trust**: bribe-taking, embezzlement, rigging a contract.

This is courtroom-and-boardroom vocabulary, and it carries the weight of a *breach of duty*, not just bad behaviour. A private citizen who steals is a thief; a treasurer who steals is guilty of malfeasance. The word implies there was an office, a duty attached to it, and a deliberate betrayal of it — which is why it reads as heavier and colder than *misconduct*.

⚠️ **malfeasance ≠ misfeasance.**
- **malfeasance** = doing something **wrongful in itself** (the clerk took a bribe)
- **misfeasance** = doing a **lawful** act **badly or improperly** (the clerk filed the permit wrong)

Third of the set: **nonfeasance** = failing to act at all.

### Trick to lock it in
> **mal-FEE-zance** — the official took a **fee** he shouldn't have.

The stressed syllable is literally *fee*, and the bribe-taking official is the archetypal malfeasant — someone paid to do a job who instead got paid to betray it.

### In sentences
1. The inquiry found no evidence of criminal **malfeasance**, but it did find a commissioner who had signed contracts he never read.
2. Losing the client wasn't **malfeasance** — he was sloppy, not crooked.

### In the wild
**"Corporate malfeasance"** is the stock phrase business journalism reaches for whenever an Enron- or FTX-style scandal breaks — executives looting the company they were trusted to run.

### Where it comes from
Latin **male** (*badly*) + **facere** (*to do*), through French *faisance*, "doing." Literally **bad-doing**.

*Facere* is one of the great GRE roots: **feasible** (do-able), **factory**, **benefactor**, **malefactor**. And *male-* runs through **malevolent**, **malign**, **malady** — anything that starts *mal-* is bad news.

# **manifest** *(adj. / v. / n.)* — **MAN-i-fest**

### Means
**Adj.:** plain to see, needing no proof — **evident to the senses**, not inferred.
**Verb:** to show something plainly, to make it appear ("the illness *manifested* as a rash"; "*manifest* itself").
**Noun:** the official list of cargo or passengers on a ship or plane.

*Obvious* is casual and can be a bit dismissive; **manifest** is formal and carries weight — it's the word of judges, doctors and diplomats. It implies the thing is displayed so openly that arguing about it would be embarrassing: a *manifest error*, a *manifest injustice*, *manifest incompetence*. Note the direction of travel in the verb: something hidden — a disease, a talent, a grudge — comes **out into view**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **MAN + FIST** — if you could close your fist around it, it isn't a matter of opinion.

The hidden word is also the real root, and it captures the strength of the adjective: manifest means so plainly there you could practically grab it.

### In sentences
1. The court found a **manifest** error of law and ordered a retrial.
2. He says he's fine, but his stress **manifests** as him reorganising the entire kitchen at 2 a.m.

### In the wild
The whole online **"manifesting"** trend — journalling your dream job into existence — is exactly this verb: making a thing come *out into visible reality*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **manifestus** = *manus* ("hand") + *-festus* ("struck, seized") — literally **"caught by the hand," red-handed**. What's caught in your hand can't be denied.

*manus* is a high-yield GRE root: **manual, manipulate, manuscript, manacle, emancipate** (*e-* out + *manus* + *capere* — released from the hand of a master).

# **minute** *(adj.)* — **my-NOOT**

*Also my-NYOOT. The 60-seconds noun is MIN-it — same spelling, different stress, different word. On the GRE, assume the my-NOOT one.*

### Means
**1.** Exceedingly small — small to the point of being hard to detect. **2.** Of an examination or account: **going into the tiniest particulars**; painstakingly detailed.

*Tiny* is casual and *minuscule* just insists on smallness, but *minute* implies an **observer straining to see** — it's the word for cracks, traces, discrepancies, quantities under a microscope. That's why it slides so naturally into sense 2: if you're noticing things that small, you're being exhaustively thorough. "A minute inspection" isn't a short one — it's one that missed nothing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **my-NEWT** — small as a newt, and you'd need a close look to spot one.

The correct pronunciation hands you the meaning: newt-sized, and only visible to someone actually looking hard.

### In sentences
1. Forensic analysis turned up **minute** traces of accelerant on the doorframe, invisible to the naked eye.
2. He gave me a **minute** account of his flight delay — every gate change, in order.

### In the wild
The **minutes** of a meeting are the same word: medieval Latin *minuta scriptura*, "small writing" — the record of the small details. Take the minutes (MIN-its) by writing down the minute (my-NOOT) points.

### Where it comes from
Latin **minuere** = *"to lessen"*, past participle **minutus** = *"made small."* That root is a GRE goldmine: **minus, minor, diminish, diminutive, minutiae, minimal, mince** (to cut small).

The clock word comes from the same place — *pars minuta prima*, the "first small part" of an hour. The second is *pars minuta secunda*, the second small part.

# **modish** *(adj.)* — **MOH-dish**

*Long "oh" — like *mode*, not like *modest*.*

### Means
Fashionable, stylish, in step with what's current.

But it almost never means *elegant*. **Modish** points at the calendar, not at taste: it's fashionable *right now*, which quietly implies it won't be next year. Call a jacket **stylish** and you've praised it; call it **modish** and you've suggested it's chasing something. Writers use it for ideas as much as clothes — a *modish* theory is one that's trendy in seminar rooms, and the speaker is not impressed.

### Trick to lock it in
> **modish = mode + -ish** — in the current *mode*, with the **-ish** doing the sneering.

That *-ish* suffix hedges everywhere it appears (*newish, youngish, girlish*), and here it downgrades "fashionable" into "merely fashionable."

### In sentences
1. Critics dismissed the novel's fragmented structure as **modish** — a technique borrowed from whoever was being praised that season.
2. He turned up in some **modish** little collarless jacket that'll be embarrassing by Christmas.

### In the wild
**Pie à la mode.** French *à la mode* = "in the fashion" — ice cream on pie was simply the fashionable way to serve it, and the phrase stuck to the dessert. Same *mode* sitting inside *modish*.

### Where it comes from
French **mode** = *"fashion, manner,"* from Latin **modus** = *"measure, manner."* One root, a big family: **mode, model, modern, moderate, modify, à la mode, modus operandi** — and **outmoded**, which is exactly what everything modish eventually becomes.

# **nascent** *(adj.)* — **NAY-suhnt**

*Also commonly NASS-uhnt — both are standard.*

### Means
Just coming into existence; **newly born and still forming**, not yet developed enough to judge.

The word points at a thing's **earliest stage, with the future left open** — a nascent industry, a nascent democracy, a nascent talent. It carries quiet promise and quiet fragility at once: something is genuinely there, but it could still die in the crib. Register is formal-journalistic — you meet it in essays, analyst reports and op-eds, almost never in speech. Note that it attaches to *abstractions and movements* far more than to people or objects; you'd say a nascent friendship, not a nascent baby.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nascent = "nay-scent"** — the first faint scent of something, before you can name the smell.

It's detectable but not yet defined — exactly the stage the word describes.

### In sentences
1. The treaty gave the **nascent** republic a border but not yet an economy.
2. She spotted the trend while it was still **nascent** — three shops in one city — and bought in early.

### In the wild
It's the default adjective of business and tech coverage: *the nascent AI market*, *the nascent EV sector*. If a journalist wants to say "this is real but too early to call," this is the word they reach for.

### Where it comes from
Latin **nasci** = *"to be born"* (present participle *nascens*). Learn this root once and you unlock a crowd: **native, natal, prenatal, nativity, nation, innate** (*born in*), **cognate** (*born together*), and **Renaissance** — literally a *re-birth*.

# **perennial** *(adj. / n.)* — **puh-REN-ee-uhl**

### Means
**Adj.:** lasting through the years, or — more precisely — **coming back again and again**. **Noun:** a plant that lives on and reflowers each year (as opposed to an annual).

The word carries **recurrence**, not just duration: something that fades and returns on schedule, so reliably you stop being surprised. That's the difference from *perpetual* (never stops) and *eternal* (never ends). Tone flips with the object: a **perennial favourite** is affectionate — it keeps coming back because people love it; a **perennial problem** or **perennial complaint** is weary — it keeps coming back because nobody ever fixed it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A perennial comes back. A perpetual never left.**

Perennial things go away and return on a cycle, which is why it fits favourites, candidates and unfixed problems rather than things that simply never stop.

### In sentences
1. Funding for the arts is a **perennial** casualty of the budget: cut, restored, and cut again.
2. Dad's **perennial** joke about the sat-nav gets wheeled out every single road trip.

### In the wild
The plant tags at any garden centre: **annuals** die off and you rebuy them next spring; **perennials** cost more because they come back on their own, year after year.

### Where it comes from
Latin **per** (*through*) + **annus** (*year*) = "through the year."

**annus** is a workhorse root: **annual**, **anniversary**, **annuity**, **biennial** (every two years), **millennium** (*mille* + *annus*), **superannuated** (over-yeared, i.e. obsolete or pensioned off).

# **pious** *(adj.)* — **PEYE-uhs**

*Noun form is *piety* (PEYE-uh-tee), not "piousness".*

### Means
Devoutly religious; faithful in observance and duty — and, by extension, high-mindedly virtuous.

Two settings, and they pull opposite ways. Said of a believer or a ritual, it's straight description: *a pious woman*, *a pious custom*. But drop it next to an abstract noun and it turns acid — a **pious hope** is one nobody expects to come true, a **pious platitude** is virtue on display costing the speaker nothing. *Devout* stays warm; *sanctimonious* is openly hostile; **pious** sits in between and lets context decide, which is exactly why writers reach for it when they want to sneer without being caught sneering.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's an **IOU** hiding in p-**IOU**-s: piety is a debt of duty — to God, to parents, to country.

That's the Roman sense precisely, and it explains the modern sneer: a *pious hope* is someone making a show of paying a debt that costs them nothing.

### In sentences
1. The abbey's founder was a **pious** man who gave away two-thirds of his estate and kept a stone cell for himself.
2. Everyone nodded along to his **pious** little speech about teamwork, then went straight back to hoarding their own data.

### In the wild
Twelve popes have taken the name **Pius** — the Latin adjective itself, chosen precisely because it advertises dutiful devotion.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pius** = *"dutiful, devoted"*, noun **pietās**. That noun split into two English words with very different jobs: **piety** (duty owed upward, to God) and **pity** (compassion owed downward, to the wretched) — same root, opposite directions.

Also from it: **expiate** (pay off a wrong), **impious**, and **pittance** — originally a small charitable donation left in a will, which is why it now means a miserably small amount.

# **providential** *(adj.)* — **prov-ih-DEN-shuhl**

*First syllable is PROV (as in *provocative*), not "pro-".*

### Means
Happening at exactly the right moment, as though arranged by a benevolent higher power; luckily timed, opportune.

*Lucky* is blind chance. **Providential** implies the good fortune looks **designed** — the rain stops the fire, the letter arrives the morning before the deadline. Even in fully secular use the word keeps a faint theological glow: someone or something was looking after you. It's warm, formal, and always positive, and it attaches to **timing** above all — a providential delay, a providential encounter.

⚠️ **providential ≠ provident.**
- **providential** = *fortunately timed*, as if by divine arrangement (about luck)
- **provident** = *prudent, saving for the future* (about foresight — a provident aunt who bought insurance)

A provident man plans for the storm; a providential wind blows it away.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Providential** = something **provided** it — and provided it *just in time*.

The hidden word *provide* is the whole meaning: the help was supplied, not stumbled upon, and the point is the timing.

### In sentences
1. The fog that settled over the harbour that night proved **providential**, screening the retreat from the guns on the far shore.
2. My phone died right before I could send that text — honestly, **providential**.

### In the wild
**Providence, Rhode Island** — Roger Williams, banished into the winter wilderness in 1636, named the place he survived at for God's providence. A whole city named after lucky-looking-like-design.

### Where it comes from
Latin **providēre** = *"to see ahead"* (**pro-** "before" + **vidēre** "to see"). Divine **providence** is God's foresight arranging events; what falls out of that arrangement is *providential*.

The root pays rent across the exam: **provide, provision, purvey, prudent** (a squashed form of *providens*), and on the *vidēre* side **evident, vision, video, visage**.

# **prowess** *(n.)* — **PROW-iss**

### Means
Exceptional skill or ability in some field — and, in older or heroic use, **bravery in battle**.

Unlike *skill* or *competence*, this word admires. You don't have prowess at loading the dishwasher; prowess is displayed, and someone is impressed. It also almost never stands alone — it arrives bolted to a domain: *military prowess, athletic prowess, technical prowess, prowess in the courtroom*. And it's faintly grand, so claiming it about yourself sounds like boasting; it's a word other people use about you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pro**-wess — what a **pro** possesses, and never without a field to be pro *at*.

The hidden "pro" gives you both halves of the word: expert-level ability, always in a named domain.

### In sentences
1. The chronicle dwells less on the king's statecraft than on his **prowess** in the field, where he is said to have unhorsed three men in a single charge.
2. Everyone found out about his **prowess** at the barbecue when he produced ribs that shut the entire party up.

### In the wild
Live sports commentary keeps this word alive: *"his prowess in front of goal," "her prowess from the baseline."* If a pundit is praising someone's specialist ability, this is the noun they reach for.

### Where it comes from
Old French **proesce** = *"bravery, valour,"* from **prou** = *"brave, worthy."*

Same root gives you **proud** — and, oddly, **prude**, from *prudefemme*, "a fine, upstanding woman," which soured over the centuries into "prim." Bravery → self-regard → primness, all from one stem.

🚫 **Not the *prow* of a ship.** That's Latin *prora*, "front end." Pure spelling coincidence.

# **schism** *(n.)* — **SIZ-uhm**

*Traditionally SIZ-uhm; SKIZ-uhm is now widely accepted. The "ch" is never "ch" as in chair.*

### Means
A **formal split of a group into opposing factions** — especially a church, party, or institution — usually over doctrine or principle. Also the resulting division itself.

A *rift* is emotional and may heal; a *dispute* is an event. A schism is **structural and permanent**: one body becomes two, each claiming to be the true one. The word carries an ecclesiastical shadow even in secular use, so calling a party split a schism implies a war over *orthodoxy*, not tactics — heretics on one side, the faithful on the other.

### Trick to lock it in
> **sch-ISM** — a split over an **-ism**.

The word hides *ism* precisely because a schism is never about personalities — it's a rupture over doctrine, and it ends with two organisations where there was one.

### In sentences
1. The reform vote produced a lasting **schism**, and within a year the dissenting bishops had consecrated a rival hierarchy.
2. There's a proper **schism** in the group chat now — half of them won't book the same flight as the other half.

### In the wild
**The Great Schism of 1054**, when Christianity split into Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox — the textbook case, and the reason the word still smells of churches.

### Where it comes from
Greek **skhizein** = *"to split, to cleave"* → **skhisma**, "a cleft."

Same root gives **schizophrenia** (literally "split mind") and **schist**, the rock that flakes apart in sheets. Hear *schizo-* inside *schism* and the meaning is free.

# **slander** *(n. / v.)* — **SLAN-der**

### Means
**Noun:** a **false spoken statement** that damages someone's reputation. **Verb:** to make such a statement about someone.

Three things have to be true for slander, and the GRE tests all three: the claim is **spoken** (not written), **false** (not just unkind), and **presented as fact** (not opinion). Calling your boss an idiot is an insult; saying he embezzled money is slander. That factual pretence is what gives the word its legal teeth — *badmouth* and *trash-talk* are social, *slander* is actionable, the kind of thing a lawyer's letter follows.

⚠️ **slander ≠ libel.** Same crime, different medium: **slander** is *spoken*, **libel** is *written or published*. A rumour whispered at a party is slander; the same rumour tweeted is libel.

### Trick to lock it in
> **S**lander is **S**poken; **L**ibel is **L**etters.

The initial letter tells you the medium, which is the only thing separating the two words.

### In sentences
1. The former minister sued for **slander**, alleging that the remarks made on air had cost him his seat on the board.
2. Don't go **slandering** him at the pub — you don't actually know he took the money.

### In the wild
Online, "that's slander" has become a stock joke reply to any unflattering claim about you — mock-legal outrage over something trivially small.

### Where it comes from
Latin **scandalum** = *"a stumbling block, a trap"* (from Greek *skandalon*). It came into English through Old French *esclandre*, where the **-nd-** wobbled into an **l**.

So **slander** and **scandal** are literally the same word, split in two: the scandal is the disgrace, the slander is the lie that manufactures it.

# **stalwart** *(adj. / n.)* — **STAWL-wert**

*The -wart ending mumbles to "wert" — nothing to do with a wart.*

### Means
**Adj.:** loyal, dependable and hard to shift — steadfast in support, and (older sense) physically sturdy and robust.
**Noun:** the long-serving reliable one: *a stalwart of the party / of the club*.

This is a word of **praise for endurance**. *Stubborn* says you won't move and that's a flaw; *stalwart* says you won't move and thank God for it. It carries a hint of physical solidity and of **time served** — a stalwart has been there for years, through the bad seasons, doing the unglamorous work. You don't call a brilliant newcomer a stalwart.

### Trick to lock it in
> **STAL-** as in **stall**: a stalwart holds his stall and is *worth* keeping in it.

The word literally packages "standing-place" + "worth", which is exactly its flavour — not just immovable, but admirably so.

### In sentences
1. Two **stalwarts** of the movement, both past eighty, were still handing out leaflets in the rain.
2. He's not flashy, but he's been a **stalwart** — twelve years and he's never once let the team down.

### In the wild
Sports commentary lives on this word: the "club **stalwart**" is the defender with 400 appearances, no glory, and no intention of leaving.

### Where it comes from
Old English **stælwierþe**, generally traced to *staþol* ("foundation, standing place") + *weorþ* ("worth") — literally **"worth its place."** Same *sta-* family as **stall**, **stand**, and **steadfast**: all of them about not moving.

# **supplicate** *(v.)* — **SUP-luh-kayt**

*Noun forms: supplication (sup-luh-KAY-shun), supplicant (SUP-luh-kunt).*

### Means
To beg humbly and earnestly for something — to entreat someone who has power over you.

The word carries **posture**. To *request* is horizontal, between equals; to *plead* is urgent; to **supplicate** is to ask from below, kneeling, hands out, all dignity deliberately surrendered. It belongs to prayer, to petitioning kings, to the defeated asking mercy. Used of ordinary life it is faintly withering — "he supplicated for a raise" says the man abased himself.

⚠️ **supplicate ≠ supplant.** *Supplicate* = beg on your knees. *Supplant* = oust someone and take their place. The supplicant is under you; the supplanter replaces you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **SUPPLE** is sitting right inside **supplicate** — you make yourself supple, and bend.

Not a coincidence either: both words are the knee folding under you, which is why supplicating always sounds like self-abasement rather than a plain request.

### In sentences
1. The envoys came not to negotiate but to **supplicate**, kneeling on the marble until the emperor deigned to look up.
2. I'm not going to **supplicate** to some ticket bot for concert seats — if it sells out, it sells out.

### In the wild
In Islam, **du'a** — the personal prayer made with open, upturned palms — is standardly rendered in English as **"supplication."** The gesture is the definition.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** ("under") + **plicāre** ("to fold, bend") — *to bend down under someone*.

That *plicāre* is a workhorse root: **complicate** (fold together), **replicate** (fold back), **implicate** (fold in), **application**, **pliable**, and — through French — **deploy** and **employ**.

# **terse** *(adj.)* — **TERS**

### Means
Using **very few words** — brief, clipped, stripped of anything decorative.

But brevity alone isn't the point. *Concise* is a compliment; *laconic* is dry and stylish; **terse** usually comes with a chill. A terse reply is grammatically fine and emotionally shut — the shortness is doing the talking, signalling irritation, reserve, or "this conversation is over." Applied to prose it can be praise (Hemingway is terse), but applied to a person it means they gave you the minimum and nothing more.

⚠️ **terse ≠ tense.** One letter apart, and both fit "a ___ exchange."
- **terse** = short on *words*
- **tense** = short on *ease* — strained, anxious

A terse exchange has few words; a tense one may have plenty.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Terse** rhymes with **curse** and **worse** — the words get cut short, and the mood gets worse.

The rhyme carries the coldness that plain "brief" doesn't have.

### In sentences
1. The minister's **terse** two-line denial only sharpened the reporters' suspicions.
2. I asked if he was okay and got a **terse** "fine" — so, obviously, not fine.

### In the wild
Standard newsroom shorthand: when a company or celebrity is caught out, the coverage says they "issued a **terse** statement" — three sentences, no apology, no questions taken.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tersus** = *"wiped clean, polished"* (from *tergēre*, to wipe). Terse first meant *polished* in style — language buffed until nothing surplus remained — and slid from "nothing wasted" to "nothing offered."

Same root scrubs your dishes: **detergent** = *de-* (away) + *tergēre* (wipe).

# **tirade** *(n.)* — **tye-RAYD**

*Also heard as TYE-rayd, stress on the first syllable — both are standard.*

### Means
A **long, angry, abusive speech** — a sustained verbal attack, usually delivered at volume and at length.

The word passes judgement on the speaker, not the target. Calling something a tirade says the person lost control, went on far too long, and stopped persuading anyone several minutes ago. Nobody describes their own outburst as a tirade; it is what other people call it afterwards. Length is essential — a single cutting remark can never be one.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tirade** begins with **tire** — it rants on until everyone in the room is tired of it.

The hidden word carries the key nuance: a tirade is defined by exhausting length, not just anger.

### In sentences
1. His resignation letter degenerated into a three-page **tirade** against colleagues who had, by then, stopped reading.
2. She launched into a **tirade** about the parking situation and we just stood there holding our coffees.

### In the wild
Newsroom boilerplate: "launched into a foul-mouthed **tirade**" is the standard phrase for a footballer at a referee or a politician caught on a hot mic — you've read the sentence a hundred times.

### Where it comes from
French **tirade**, from Italian **tirata** = *"a pull, a long drawn-out stretch,"* from **tirare**, "to pull." A tirade is speech *drawn out* — the same *tirer* gives English **retire** (to draw back).

# **universal** *(adj.)* — **yoo-nuh-VER-suhl**

### Means
**Applying to every member of a group without a single exception** — and, by extension, found everywhere.

Strength is what separates it from its neighbours. *Widespread* tolerates holdouts, *general* quietly admits exceptions, *common* just means frequent — but **universal** claims totality, so one counterexample destroys it. That absoluteness is exactly why GRE argument passages love the word: when an author calls something universal, the question usually turns on the case that isn't covered.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Universal** has the **universe** inside it — and nothing sits outside the universe.

If even one case escapes the rule, it was never universal, only common.

### In sentences
1. The declaration asserts rights held to be **universal**, binding on every state regardless of custom or creed.
2. Pizza is basically the **universal** food — I've never met anyone who says no to it.

### In the wild
A **universal remote** — the one that works with any brand of TV. That's the meaning in a plastic shell: no exceptions, everything covered.

### Where it comes from
Latin **unus** (*one*) + **vertere / versus** (*to turn*) — "turned into one," rolled into a single whole.

Both halves pay off elsewhere: *unus* gives **unify, unison, unanimous**; *vertere* gives **convert, divert, avert, invert, adverse, versatile**.

# **vanquish** *(v.)* — **VANG-kwish**

*The n comes out as ng, as in sing — VANG-kwish, not VAN-kwish.*

### Means
To defeat someone **completely and finally** — in battle, in competition, or in the case of a fear or a habit, to crush it for good.

*Defeat* is the neutral scoreboard word; *vanquish* is the heroic one. It comes with trumpets — knights, dragons, armies, demons — and it implies the loser is not merely behind but **finished**, with no rematch implied. Because the register is that elevated, using it for small stuff is a deliberate joke: you vanquish an enemy nation, and you "vanquish" your inbox only with a smirk. It also takes abstract objects gracefully — vanquish doubt, grief, temptation — where *beat* would sound flat.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Van-QUISH — squish them so hard they vanish.**

The squish-and-vanish pair carries the totality: not edged out, but flattened out of existence.

### In sentences
1. The young king **vanquished** the rebel lords in a single autumn and never faced a challenge again.
2. She finally **vanquished** her fear of flying — twelve hours to Tokyo, no white knuckles.

### In the wild
The **Aston Martin Vanquish** — the brand's flagship supercar, named to say it doesn't merely beat the competition, it ends it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vincere** = *"to conquer"* (past participle *victum*), reaching English through Old French *vainquir*.

That one root is a GRE goldmine: **victor**, **victory**, **invincible** ("not conquerable"), **convince** (conquer someone's doubts), **evince**, **convict**, **evict**.

🚫 **Not related to *vanish***, despite the sound — that's Latin *evanescere*, "to fade away."

# **woeful** *(adj.)* — **WOH-fuhl**

*First syllable rhymes with go, not goo — WOH, never WOO.*

### Means
Two senses, and the GRE will use either.

**1. Full of sorrow; expressing grief** — a woeful sigh, a woeful expression.
**2. Pitifully bad; lamentably inadequate** — a woeful performance, a woeful lack of preparation.

Sense 2 is the one that trips people, and it is a *judgement*, not a description: calling a defence woeful says it was so incompetent that watching it was painful. Compare **bad** (flat), **dismal** (bleak, joyless), **abysmal** (bottomlessly bad, pure scale) — *woeful* alone carries the sting of grief, as though the failure were a small tragedy. It also runs faintly theatrical: a woeful face is a face wearing its sadness for an audience, which is why *woeful* pairs so naturally with sarcasm.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Woeful defending doesn't *feel* woe — it *causes* it.**

That flip is the whole second sense: the thing isn't sad, it's so bad that it makes *you* despair.

### In sentences
1. The report described a **woeful** neglect of the archive: damp ledgers, unlabelled boxes, whole decades unaccounted for.
2. Three hours of revision and I still bombed it — **woeful**, honestly.

### In the wild
Football commentary and match reports live on this word — "a **woeful** clearance", "**woeful** finishing" — always meaning bad enough to be sad, never merely sad.

### Where it comes from
**Woe** is Old English **wā** — not a noun originally but a *cry*, the sound you make when struck. That's why the word still sounds like a wail.

Same root gives **woebegone**, literally *"beset by woe"* — surrounded by it, hence that hangdog, defeated look.
