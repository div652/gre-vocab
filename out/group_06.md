# **admonish** *(v.)* — **ad-MON-ish**

### Means
To **reprove someone firmly but mildly** — and, by extension, to **urge or warn** them earnestly about what to do next (*he admonished them to keep silent*).

The force is corrective, not punitive. A boss who **castigates** you wants you to feel it; a boss who **admonishes** you wants you to fix it — there's a note of counsel, even care, and usually a moral or authority relationship behind it (parent, judge, teacher, scripture). It's the scolding that comes with advice attached.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ad-MON-ish** — the **monitor** speaks: a warning meant to *correct* you, not crush you.

Same **mon-** as *monitor* and *premonition*, so an admonishment is essentially a heads-up delivered with a frown.

### In sentences
1. The bishop **admonished** his congregation to temper their zeal with charity.
2. Mum **admonished** me for skipping breakfast again — not angry, just disappointed and full of advice.

### In the wild
Courtroom boilerplate: the judge **admonishes** the jury not to discuss the case or read the news. Not a punishment — a warning with instructions, which is the word exactly.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **monēre** (*to warn, remind*). That root is a GRE goldmine: **monitor** (one who warns), **premonition** (a warning beforehand), **monument** (a thing that reminds you), **summon**, **monitory**.

# **aesthetic** *(adj. / n.)* — **es-THET-ik**

*Also spelled esthetic in US English; Brits often stretch the first syllable to ees-THET-ik.*

### Means
**Adj.:** having to do with beauty and the perception of it. **Noun:** a coherent set of principles or a look — *the minimalist aesthetic*.

The word marks a **domain, not a compliment**. "Aesthetic considerations" means *how it looks* — as opposed to moral, practical, or financial considerations. So on the GRE it usually appears as one half of a contrast: the bridge was rebuilt for aesthetic rather than structural reasons. Calling a choice aesthetic can quietly mean it does nothing useful. Only in casual speech ("very aesthetic") has it drifted into meaning *pretty*.

⚠️ **aesthetic ≠ ascetic** (uh-SET-ik).
- **aesthetic** = concerned with beauty
- **ascetic** = severely self-denying — no comfort, no pleasure

An aesthete fills the room with beautiful things; an ascetic empties it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **An-aesthetic** = *no* feeling. Knock off the *an-* and feeling comes back — starting with the eye's kind.

The word is about perceiving and taking pleasure in how a thing looks, never about whether it works.

### In sentences
1. The renovation was condemned as purely **aesthetic**: the façade gleamed, while the wiring behind it remained a hazard.
2. Her flat has a whole **aesthetic** going on — everything beige, everything matching, nothing you're allowed to touch.

### In the wild
Pinterest and TikTok turned it into a noun you scroll past daily — *cottagecore aesthetic*, *clean girl aesthetic*. Each one is exactly what the word means: a consistent set of rules about how things should look.

### Where it comes from
Greek **aisthesthai** = *"to perceive, to feel."* Perception first, beauty second.

That root hands you a family: **anaesthetic** (*an-* = without → no feeling), **aesthete** (one who feels beauty keenly), **synaesthesia** (senses crossing — hearing colour), **kinaesthetic** (feeling your own movement).

🚫 **Not *ascetic*.** That's Greek *askein*, "to train, to exercise" — the same idea behind an athlete's discipline, redirected at the soul.

# **affectation** *(n.)* — **af-ek-TAY-shuhn**

### Means
A bit of behaviour, speech, or dress that is **put on rather than natural** — adopted to impress or to seem a certain kind of person.

Calling something an affectation is always a verdict, not a description: you are saying the pose is fake *and* that you can see through it. A **mannerism** is just a personal quirk, morally neutral; an affectation is a quirk someone chose, and the choice is the crime. Note the scale, too — it's usually small and specific (a drawled vowel, a cigarette holder, spelling *theatre* the British way), not a whole false life. That smallness is what makes it faintly ridiculous rather than sinister.

⚠️ **affectation ≠ affection.**
- **affectation** = a manner you *fake*
- **affection** = fondness you *feel*

One letter-cluster apart, opposite in sincerity.

### Trick to lock it in
> To **affect** an accent is to fake one — an affect**ation** is the fake itself.

The verb *affect* has a sneaky GRE sense — "to feign" (*he affected a limp*) — and the noun is simply that feigning made into a thing.

### In sentences
1. His habit of quoting Latin at dinner was less scholarship than **affectation**, and the table had long since stopped pretending to be impressed.
2. She's been back from London a week and she's already saying "cheers" — total **affectation**.

### In the wild
Madonna's British accent after she moved to London — years of press mockery, and the word used almost every time.

### Where it comes from
Latin **affectāre** = *"to strive after, aspire to"* — and, by an easy slide, *"to pretend to."* An affectation is a quality you reach for because you don't actually have it.

Same family: **affect** (to feign), **affected** (mannered), **disaffected** (no longer attached).

# **alleviate** *(v.)* — **uh-LEE-vee-ayt**

### Means
To make pain, suffering, or a problem **less severe** — to take the edge off.

The word is honest about its limits: you alleviate a symptom, not the disease. *Cure*, *eliminate* and *resolve* claim the problem is gone; **alleviate** only claims the weight is lighter. That's why doctors, aid agencies and policy papers reach for it — it promises relief without promising a fix. Its objects are almost always unpleasant: pain, grief, hunger, poverty, congestion, boredom. You never alleviate a good thing.

### Trick to lock it in
> al-**LEVI**-ate — same **levi** as **levitate**: it lifts weight off you, it doesn't take the load away.

Lightening ≠ removing, which is exactly the difference between *alleviate* and *cure*.

### In sentences
1. Morphine **alleviated** the worst of his pain, though the fracture itself went untreated for two more days.
2. A fan in the window **alleviated** the heat a bit, but honestly, it was still unbearable.

### In the wild
**Poverty alleviation** is the standard phrase in World Bank and NGO reports — chosen precisely because nobody is willing to say *poverty elimination*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **levis** = *"light (in weight)"* → **levāre**, *"to lift, lighten"*; *ad-* + *levāre* = to lighten toward you.

One root, a whole GRE cluster: **elevate** (lift up), **levitate** (float), **levity** (lightness of mood), **relieve/relief** (lift the burden again), **lever** and **leverage** (lifting device), even **leaven** (what lifts the dough).

# **analogous** *(adj.)* — **uh-NAL-uh-guhs**

*Hard g, as in go — never "uh-NAL-uh-jus", despite analogy.*

### Means
Corresponding in **function or relation** to something else, in a way that makes a comparison legitimate — even though the two things are otherwise unalike.

The word is a claim, not an observation. *Similar* notes a resemblance and stops; **analogous** asserts that the parallel runs deep enough to reason from — if it holds for X, expect it to hold for Y. That's why it lives in argument, science and law rather than casual description. And it never means *identical*: the things being compared are understood to differ in substance, matching only in the shape of the relationship. Always paired with **to** (or *with*).

⚠️ **analogous ≠ anomalous.**
- **analogous** *(uh-NAL-uh-guhs)* = parallel, comparable
- **anomalous** *(uh-NOM-uh-luhs)* = abnormal, deviating from the pattern

One says *it fits the pattern*; the other says *it breaks it*. The GRE loves this pair.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ANA-LOG-ous** — same **log**ic, different thing.

What matches is the underlying logic or ratio, not the appearance — which is exactly why an insect's wing is analogous to a bird's.

### In sentences
1. The court held that a text message was **analogous to** a sealed letter, and extended the same protections to it.
2. Running a startup is **analogous to** juggling — drop one thing and you can usually recover; drop two and it's over.

### In the wild
**Analogue** audio: the groove on a vinyl record is a physical wiggle whose shape corresponds, point for point, to the sound wave. Nothing about it *looks* like music — it's analogous to it. That's the whole idea in one object.

### Where it comes from
Greek **ana-** ("according to") + **logos** ("ratio, proportion") → *analogon*, "in proportion." That's why an analogy is written as a ratio: A is to B as C is to D.

**logos** is a monster root — it means both *word* and *reason/ratio*: **logic, dialogue, prologue, catalogue, eulogy**, and every **-ology**.

# **bolster** *(v. / n.)* — **BOHL-ster**

### Means
**Verb:** to prop up, strengthen, or reinforce — usually something abstract: morale, confidence, an argument, a claim, an economy, a defence.
**Noun:** a long firm cushion used as a support.

The word carries an implication about the *object*: whatever you bolster was **sagging, thin, or under pressure**. You don't bolster a case that's already airtight — you bolster the shaky one. Compare *reinforce* (flat, neutral, just adds strength) and *corroborate* (strictly evidence confirming evidence): **bolster** is propping-up work, and it quietly admits the thing needed propping.

⚠️ **bolster ≠ bluster.** One letter apart, opposite jobs: **bolster** = *quietly prop something up*; **bluster** = *loud, empty, threatening talk*. The blusterer makes noise; the bolsterer adds support.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A bolster is the long pillow you shove behind whatever's sagging.**

The noun is the whole verb: you only reach for the cushion when something is drooping — a sinking back, a weak argument, a falling currency.

### In sentences
1. The defence produced three new witnesses to **bolster** an alibi the jury had plainly begun to doubt.
2. One good week won't fix it, but it did **bolster** the team's confidence a bit.

### In the wild
Headline English lives on this verb — *measures to bolster the economy*, *reinforcements sent to bolster the front line*. If you read business or war reporting, you've met it a hundred times, always attached to something in trouble.

### Where it comes from
Old English **bolster** = *"a long pillow, cushion."* The noun never left: a bolster is still that firm cylindrical pillow, and in carpentry it's the block that takes the load. The verb is just that made literal — *to shore up with a cushion.*

# **chauvinistic** *(adj.)* — **shoh-vuh-NIS-tik**

*The ch is sh, not ch — it's a French surname. Never "chaw-".*

### Means
Displaying blind, boastful conviction that **your own group is superior** — your country, your sex, your race, your team — usually with contempt for everyone outside it.

Two flavours live in the word. The original is **fanatical patriotism**; the everyday modern one is **male chauvinism**, the assumption that men are the better sex. Either way it is not mere loyalty — a patriot loves his country, a chauvinist needs yours to be worse. The word is always an accusation, and it paints the person as smug and slightly ridiculous rather than dangerous.

### Trick to lock it in
> **SHOW-vin-**istic — he **shows** off that his side always **wins**.

The showing-off is the point: chauvinism is superiority worn loudly, not quiet allegiance.

### In sentences
1. The editorial's **chauvinistic** insistence that no foreign scientist had contributed anything of note drew ridicule from every university in the country.
2. He made some **chauvinistic** crack about her parking, and the whole table went quiet.

### In the wild
**"Male chauvinist pig"** — the 1970s feminist insult that made the word household English. If you've heard the phrase, you already know the tone: mocking, not fearful.

### Where it comes from
From a man: **Nicolas Chauvin**, a soldier of Napoleon's army so grotesquely devoted to the Emperor that French vaudeville turned him into a stock comic idiot. His name became the word.

That's why the tone is *absurd* rather than sinister — it was born as a punchline.

# **connoisseur** *(n.)* — **kon-uh-SUR**

*Also kon-uh-SOOR. Spelling trap: two n's, two s's, French **-eur** ending.*

### Means
An **expert judge of quality** in something where quality is a matter of taste — art, wine, food, music, cigars.

The key isn't how *much* you know, it's how finely you can **discriminate**. An expert on wine knows the regions; a connoisseur can tell you the vintage from the glass. Against its neighbours: an **aficionado** or **buff** brings enthusiasm, a **dilettante** brings dabbling, a connoisseur brings a trained palate and a verdict. The word flatters — which is why it curdles easily into self-regard, and why "he fancies himself a connoisseur" is an insult.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Amateur** loves it (Latin *amare*); **connoisseur** *knows* it — same French **-eur**, rival claims.

The suffix marks the person, and the stem tells you what qualifies them: feeling versus knowledge.

### In sentences
1. The museum's new curator is a **connoisseur** of early Flemish painting, able to date a panel from its craquelure alone.
2. He's a full-on crisp **connoisseur** — one bite and he'll tell you the batch was fried too long.

### In the wild
Luxury packaging runs on this word: whisky, chocolate and coffee sold as **"for the true connoisseur."** The pitch is pure flattery — buy this and you're the one with the discerning palate.

### Where it comes from
Old French **conoisseor**, from Latin **cognoscere** = *"to get to know."* That root is a GRE goldmine: **recognize, cognition, cognizant, incognito, reconnaissance, cognoscenti** (the in-the-know set).

The modern French spelling is *connaisseur* — English kept the older *-oi-*, which is why the word looks misspelled even when it isn't.

# **dissemble** *(v.)* — **di-SEM-buhl**

*Two syllables in the middle, not three — *di-SEM-buhl*, never *dis-a-SEM-buhl*.*

### Means
To **conceal your real feelings, motives or beliefs behind a false appearance** — to put on a face. (Also transitive: *he dissembled his fury*.)

Lying is about facts; dissembling is about *self-presentation*. You needn't state a single falsehood to dissemble — a composed smile over contempt does it. The word is genteel and faintly literary, which is exactly why journalists and lawyers reach for it: **it lets you accuse someone of deceit without saying "he lied."** The dissembler is a smooth operator, not a caught-out fibber.

**dissemble ≠ disassemble.** *Disassemble* = take a machine apart. *Dissemble* = put a face on. One removes parts, the other adds a mask.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Hiding inside it is *semble* — "to seem."** A dissembler works on how things *seem*, never on what's true.

That's the whole nuance: dissembling manages appearances rather than asserting facts, which is why it feels more like acting than lying.

### In sentences
1. Under cross-examination the minister **dissembled** for an hour, conceding nothing and denying nothing.
2. Don't **dissemble** — I saw your face when she walked in.

### In the wild
Watch any political interview write-up: when a reporter can't prove a lie but won't call the answer honest, the verdict is always **"the candidate dissembled."** It's the polite word for lying, and its natural habitat is the press gallery and the courtroom.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dissimulāre** = *"to make unlike, to disguise"* — from **similis**, "like, similar."

That root is worth banking: **similar, simulate, assimilate, simultaneous, verisimilitude** (the *appearance* of truth), and the stiffer twin **dissimulate**. All of them turn on likeness and seeming.

🚫 **Not *disassemble*.** That's *assemble* (Latin *ad* + *simul*, "together") with *dis-* undoing it — same distant family, entirely different job.

# **dogged** *(adj.)* — **DOG-id**

*Two syllables as an adjective — like rugged, wicked, learned. The verb past tense (the reporters dogged him) is one syllable: dogd.*

### Means
Refusing to give up — grinding on through difficulty, setback, and boredom without letting go.

*Stubborn* is a criticism (you won't move); *dogged* is usually a compliment (you won't stop). It praises endurance rather than brilliance: the dogged detective isn't the clever one, he's the one still knocking on doors in month nine. Note the tone it carries — unglamorous, patient, slightly plodding. **Dogged determination** and **dogged persistence** are near-fixed phrases; if you hear either, someone is being admired for sheer refusal to quit.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A dog with a bone.** It won't let go, and it isn't being clever about it.

That captures both halves of the word — total refusal to release, with no cunning involved.

### In sentences
1. Her **dogged** pursuit of the archive records, over four years and three countries, finally produced the document that broke the case.
2. He's not the smartest guy on the team, but he's **dogged** — he'll still be at that spreadsheet at midnight.

### In the wild
Sports and news writing runs on the verb form: an athlete **dogged by injuries**, a politician **dogged by scandal** — the trouble trails them like a hound that won't be shaken off. Same relentlessness, pointed at you instead of by you.

### Where it comes from
Straight from **dog** — specifically the hound on a scent, which is why the verb *to dog* means to follow and hound someone without let-up.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *dogma* or *dogmatic*.** That's Greek *dogma*, "opinion, decree" (→ *dogmatic, orthodox, paradigm*). A dogged person won't quit; a dogmatic person won't listen.

# **dupe** *(v. / n.)* — **DOOP**

### Means
**Verb:** to trick or deceive someone into believing something false.
**Noun:** the person so tricked — a gull, a sucker, a mark.

The word's weight sits on the **victim, not the trickster**. *Defraud* points at a crime, *swindle* at money, *deceive* at the lie itself — but to be **duped** is to have been credulous, and the word quietly says so. Hence the noun: a *dupe* isn't just someone who was lied to, it's someone who was easy to lie to. It carries a faint condescension, which is why "I was duped" is an admission as much as a complaint.

### Trick to lock it in
> Change one letter and a **dupe** is a **dope** — and a dope is exactly who gets duped.

The near-homophone carries the nuance: the word blames the gullible mark, not the con artist.

### In sentences
1. The pamphlet **duped** thousands of small investors into a company that owned nothing but a leased office and a logo.
2. He spent an hour on the phone before realising he'd been **duped** by a guy reading off a script.

### In the wild
The beauty-world **"dupe"** — a £6 lipstick sold as a dupe for a £40 one. Different sense (that one's short for *duplicate*), but it works the same way: something cheap passing for the real thing.

### Where it comes from
🚫 **Not from *duplicate*.** English took it straight from French **dupe**, "a gull, one easily fooled" — traditionally traced to *de huppe*, "hoopoe," a bird with a reputation for being filthy and stupid.

The look-alike overlap is a coincidence: the *duplicate* sense of "dupe" arrived centuries later, by a completely separate road.

# **empirical** *(adj.)* — **em-PEER-i-kuhl**

*Never stressed on the first syllable — not EM-pirical.*

### Means
Based on **observation, measurement, or experiment** rather than on theory, doctrine, or pure reasoning.

The word labels the *source* of a claim, not its truth. "Empirical" doesn't mean *correct* — it means *this came from going and looking*. Its opposite is **theoretical** or **a priori**, never *false*. In academic and professional writing it carries quiet prestige: **empirical evidence** is data you collected, as against a hunch, a model, or an anecdote. But the older flavour still surfaces on the GRE — an *empiric* was once a physician who worked by trial and error with no theory at all, so the word can shade toward crude, atheoretical rule-of-thumb.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Empirical hides a *pirate*** — and it's the same root. Pirates don't theorise; they go out and try it.

Both words come from Greek *peira*, "a trial, an attempt" — empirical knowledge is what you get by attempting the thing, not by reasoning about it in a chair.

### In sentences
1. The elegance of the model was never in doubt; what it lacked was **empirical** support.
2. Stop arguing about which headline is better — just run the test and get some **empirical** data.

### In the wild
Every A/B test at work is empiricism in miniature: nobody's opinion about the button colour wins, the click data does.

### Where it comes from
Greek **empeiria** = *"experience,"* from **peira** = *"a trial, an attempt."*

That root pays out widely: **experience** and **experiment** (things you try), **expert** (someone who has tried it often), **peril** (a trial that might kill you) — and **pirate**, from *peiratēs*, "one who makes attempts on" ships.

# **engender** *(v.)* — **en-JEN-der**

### Means
To **give rise to**, bring about, cause something to come into being.

The object is almost always **abstract** — trust, resentment, loyalty, debate, a sense of unease. You engender *conditions and feelings*, not objects: a policy can engender confidence, but nothing engenders a bridge. Register is formal and slightly bookish; it belongs to editorials, court judgments and academic prose, where *cause* would sound blunt and *create* too deliberate. It also implies a **side-effect rather than an aim** — the thing arises from the situation, often without anyone intending it.

**engender ≠ endanger.** Same eight letters, nearly the same order — but *engender* brings something into existence, *endanger* puts something at risk. Secrecy may **engender** distrust; it may also **endanger** the deal.

### Trick to lock it in
> You can engender **trust, loyalty, or hatred** — you cannot engender a chair.

The **gen-** buried in the middle means *birth* (as in *genesis*, *generate*), and what this verb gives birth to is always a feeling or a state of affairs, never a physical thing.

### In sentences
1. Decades of unexplained delays had **engendered** a deep public scepticism that no press release could dispel.
2. Turning up unannounced with a lawyer doesn't exactly **engender** goodwill.

### Where it comes from
Latin **gignere / genus** = *"to beget, to be born"*, reaching English through Old French *engendrer*, "to father a child."

That root is one of the best investments on the GRE: **genesis, generate, genre, genus, gene, congenital, progeny, indigenous, genuine, primogeniture**. Whenever you see **gen-**, think *birth or kind*.

# **entitled** *(adj. / v. (past part.))* — **en-TYE-tuhld**

### Means
**1.** Having a **rightful claim** to something: *entitled to a refund, to three weeks' leave, to a fair hearing.*
**2.** Of a person: **assuming** privileges and deference they haven't earned — *"an entitled brat."*
**3.** Given a title: *a novel entitled* Middlemarch.

The grammar tells you which one you're getting. **Entitled *to* something** is cold and bureaucratic — contracts, statutes, HR handbooks; no judgement at all. **Entitled** standing alone in front of a person is a moral verdict, and a sharp one: it says the claim is imaginary and the claimant is insufferable. Sense 1 is what the law grants you; sense 2 is what you grant yourself.

### Trick to lock it in
> **En-TITLE-d** — he thinks he holds a *title*, like a duke, and the world should curtsy.

The insult sense is exactly that: someone behaving as though rank entitles them to service they never earned.

### In sentences
1. Under the terms of the settlement, every claimant is **entitled** to compensation regardless of when the injury occurred.
2. He's so **entitled** he sent the coffee back twice and then asked for the manager's manager.

### In the wild
In US politics, Social Security and Medicare are called **entitlement programs** — benefits you have a legal right to, not favours. Half the country's arguments about them come from hearing the *other* sense of the word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **titulus** = *"title, inscription, label."* To *entitle* is literally to **put a title on** something — hence a book *entitled* X, and hence a person who acts as if he'd been handed a rank.

Same root: **title, subtitle, entitlement**, and the GRE favourite **titular** — holding the title but not the power (*the titular head of state*).

# **pertinacious** *(adj.)* — **per-tuh-NAY-shuhs**

*Not "per-TIN-ay-shus" — the stress jumps to the third syllable, and *-cious* is *-shuhs*, as in gracious.*

### Means
Holding stubbornly and unyieldingly to a purpose, opinion, or course of action; refusing to let go.

**Tenacious** is a compliment — grit, endurance, admirable grip. **Pertinacious** is the same grip held about an hour too long: the persistence has curdled into obstinacy and the speaker is tired of it. It's a formal, slightly bookish word, and it usually carries a sigh — a pertinacious creditor, a pertinacious cough, a pertinacious rumour that will not die. Applied to people, it says *they will not drop this*, and not admiringly.

**pertinacious ≠ pertinent.** Same Latin family, opposite jobs: **pertinent** = *relevant, to the point*; **pertinacious** = *doggedly won't let go*. A pertinent question is worth asking; a pertinacious questioner won't stop asking it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Per-tenacious** — tenacious *per*-severed past the point anyone wanted.

Hear *tenacious* sitting inside the word, then let the *per-* ("thoroughly, all the way through") push the admirable version into the annoying one.

### In sentences
1. Her **pertinacious** pursuit of the buried invoices exhausted three lawyers before the firm finally settled.
2. He's **pertinacious** about that parking fine — four appeals now, over eighteen quid.

### In the wild
Jack Black's band **Tenacious D** — the name is the same *ten-* root worn as a badge: "the D" that never lets go. Pertinacious is that, minus the charm.

### Where it comes from
Latin **per-** (*thoroughly*) + **tenax / tenēre** (*to hold*) — literally *holding on all the way through*.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **tenacious** (gripping), **tenet** (a belief you hold), **tenure** (holding a post), **retain, contain, sustain, abstain** — all holding something, or holding back from it.

# **presumptuous** *(adj.)* — **pri-ZUMP-choo-uhs**

*Four syllables — there is no such word as "presumptious" (pri-ZUMP-shuhs); that misspelling is the commonest error with this word.*

### Means
Overstepping: taking a liberty, a right, or a familiarity that hasn't been granted to you.

The charge isn't that you were wrong — it's that you **assumed standing you don't have**. Correcting your professor's Latin, calling the CEO "Dave" on day one, ordering for someone else at dinner: presumptuous. *Arrogant* describes a permanent inflation of the self; *presumptuous* describes a single act of reaching past your place, and can be levelled at an otherwise modest person. It also has a curious polite use — English speakers pre-emptively accuse *themselves* of it ("it would be presumptuous of me to advise you") precisely to prove they aren't.

⚠️ **presumptuous ≠ presumptive.**
- **presumptuous** = rudely overstepping
- **presumptive** = presumed, assumed-for-now — the *heir presumptive*, the *presumptive nominee*

Calling someone the presumptive winner is neutral reporting. Calling them presumptuous is an insult.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pre-SUMP-tuous** = **pre** (before) + **sume** (take) — he *took* it before it was offered.

The whole offence lives in that *pre-*: not the taking, but the not-waiting.

### In sentences
1. It would be **presumptuous** of the committee to declare a verdict before the evidence has been heard.
2. Bit **presumptuous** of him to bring a girlfriend to my wedding when he RSVP'd for one.

### In the wild
Its natural habitat is the hedging work email: *"Apologies if this is presumptuous, but I've gone ahead and drafted it."* You use the word to apologise for the very thing you're doing anyway.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sumere** = *"to take"* — a root that pays out everywhere: **as**sume (take on), **con**sume (take up entirely), **re**sume (take up again), **sub**sume (take under), **pre**sume (take beforehand). Even **sumptuous** belongs — Latin *sumptus*, "expense," what's been *taken out* of your purse.

# **probity** *(n.)* — **PROH-bih-tee**

*Long "oh", despite the short o in probable — PROB-ih-tee is a common slip.*

### Means
Complete honesty and moral uprightness — especially incorruptibility in handling money, office, or public trust.

*Integrity* is warm and broad; you can have integrity as an artist or a friend. **Probity** is cold and official. It's the word for a treasurer who never touched the fund, a judge who couldn't be bought — honesty of the kind that survives an audit, not honesty of the heart. It lives in obituaries, judicial citations and procurement rules, and almost never in conversation.

**probity ≠ propriety.** *Probity* = you are genuinely honest. *Propriety* = you observe the accepted forms of decent behaviour. A charming embezzler can have flawless propriety and zero probity.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nobody praises your **probity** until money has passed through your hands.

The word surfaces only where funds, contracts or public office are involved, which pins it to incorruptibility rather than general niceness.

### In sentences
1. The bench was chosen for probity as much as for learning; a single questionable payment would have ended any of their careers.
2. Twelve years as club treasurer and not a penny out of place — the man's probity was boring, and that was the point.

### In the wild
In UK and Australian government tendering, big contracts require an appointed **probity adviser** or **probity auditor** — a real job title whose whole function is to certify that nobody was bought.

### Where it comes from
Latin **probus** = *"good, upright"*, via **probāre** = *"to test, to prove."* Probity is honesty that has been **tested and passed**.

That root spreads wide: **prove**, **approve**, **probe**, **probation** (a testing period), **probable** (what tests out as likely), and **reprobate** — literally *re-tested and rejected*, hence a scoundrel.

---

# **proliferate** *(v.)* — **pruh-LIF-uh-rayt**

### Means
To **multiply rapidly** — to increase in number by producing more copies of itself. Also transitive: to *cause* something to spread that way (*to proliferate weapons*).

Things don't proliferate by being added one at a time; they proliferate the way cells and rumours do — each one spawning the next, faster than anyone is tracking. That's why the word so often arrives with a faint note of alarm: *conspiracy theories proliferated*, *nuclear weapons proliferated*, *bubble-tea shops proliferated on one street*. It can be perfectly neutral in biology, but in ordinary use the implication is usually **out of hand**. *Increase* is a measurement; *proliferate* is a warning.

### Trick to lock it in
> **PRO-LIFE-rate** — the rate at which life makes more life.

The word literally hides *life* multiplying at a *rate*, which is exactly the runaway, self-copying growth it describes.

### In sentences
1. Unregulated by the state, private militias **proliferated** across the border provinces within a single decade.
2. Ever since he got the label printer, little stickers have **proliferated** on every drawer in the kitchen.

### In the wild
The **Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty** — the whole international project of stopping nuclear weapons from *spreading to more and more countries*. If you know the treaty, you already know the word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prōlēs** = *"offspring"* + **ferre** = *"to bear."* To proliferate is literally **to bear offspring** — hence the sense of self-copying rather than mere adding.

*prōlēs* also gives **prolific** (producing lots) and **proletariat** (in Rome, the class so poor their only contribution to the state was children). *ferre* is everywhere: **transfer, confer, refer, fertile**.

# **specious** *(adj.)* — **SPEE-shuhs**

*First syllable is "spee-", not "spesh-".*

### Means
Superficially convincing but actually **false** — an argument, claim, or excuse that passes a quick look and collapses under a second one.

The word is aimed almost always at **reasoning**, not at objects or people. And it carries a specific insult: the thing is *dressed up*. A liar is crude; a specious argument is well-tailored — plausible structure, respectable vocabulary, rotten logic. Calling something specious says you inspected it and found the shine was the whole product.

**specious ≠ spurious.**
- **specious** = *sounds* right, reasoning is bad
- **spurious** = *isn't* genuine, origin is fake

A specious argument is really an argument, just a bad one. A spurious quotation was never said at all.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Specious** is one letter off **spacious** — a spacious argument: impressive room, nothing in it.

Both words promise something grand on first look, and the specious one turns out to be empty inside.

### In sentences
1. The defence rested on a **specious** distinction between advising a crime and ordering one — the jury saw through it in an hour.
2. He gave me some **specious** line about the traffic, which would've worked if I hadn't watched him leave the pub.

### Where it comes from
Latin **speciōsus** = *"good-looking, showy,"* from **species** (*appearance*) ← **specere**, *"to look at."* The word originally meant *handsome* — and then slid, as flattering words do, into *only* handsome.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **species, specimen, spectacle, inspect, conspicuous, perspective, suspicion** — all about looking. *Specious* is the one where looking is all you get.

---

# **spurious** *(adj.)* — **SPYUUR-ee-uhs**

*The first syllable is "spyoo", not "spur" — never SPUR-ee-uhs.*

### Means
Not genuine; lacking any legitimate basis or origin. A **spurious** document is a forgery, a **spurious** claim rests on nothing, a **spurious** correlation is a pattern that isn't really there.

It's the vocabulary of dismissal. Calling an argument *false* engages with it; calling it **spurious** refuses to — you're saying it never had standing in the first place. And unlike *fraudulent* or *phony*, it doesn't require a liar: statistics throw up spurious results with nobody deceiving anyone. Cool, formal, faintly contemptuous — the word of judges, editors and scientists.

⚠️ **spurious ≠ specious.**
- **spurious** = *not genuine* — no real basis, whether or not it looks convincing
- **specious** = *deceptively convincing* — it does look good, and that's the problem

A specious argument seduces you. A spurious one just doesn't hold up.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Spur**ious claims deserve to be **spur**ned.

Same opening four letters, and it captures the tone — you don't rebut something spurious, you brush it aside.

### In sentences
1. The court dismissed the petition as **spurious**, finding no evidence that the signatory had ever existed.
2. He gave some **spurious** excuse about traffic, but we'd all seen his car in the lot since nine.

### In the wild
**Spurious correlation** is the standard term in stats and data science — and Tyler Vigen's *Spurious Correlations* charts (US cheese consumption vs. deaths by bedsheet entanglement) made it a running internet joke.

### Where it comes from
Latin **spurius** = *"illegitimate, of unknown father."* A spurious child had no acknowledged parentage — and that's still exactly the accusation: a spurious quote, gospel or manuscript is one whose claimed origin is a lie.

---

# **subjective** *(adj.)* — **suhb-JEK-tiv**

### Means
Belonging to the observer rather than the observed — shaped by personal feeling, taste, or point of view. Also, in a stricter philosophical sense: **existing in the mind**, not out in the world.

Note that it is not a polite word for *biased*. Bias is a distortion you could in principle correct; subjectivity is simply the fact that the judgment lives inside a particular head — pain, beauty, and "too spicy" are subjective without anyone being at fault. The negative charge only appears when someone deploys it as a verdict ("that's purely subjective"), meaning: *unverifiable, so it doesn't count as evidence.*

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ob**-ject = thrown *in front of you*, out there. **Sub**-ject = thrown *underneath* — the self doing the looking.

Objective facts sit outside where anyone can check them; subjective ones sit under your own skull where nobody else can get at them.

### In sentences
1. Aesthetic judgment, Kant argued, is **subjective** and yet somehow demands the agreement of others.
2. Scoring interviews out of ten is hopelessly **subjective** — one panellist's "confident" is another's "arrogant".

### In the wild
Doctors' notes are literally built on the split: the **SOAP** note opens with **S — Subjective** (what the patient reports: "it burns at night") before **O — Objective** (what the thermometer and the blood panel say).

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** (*under*) + **jacere** (*to throw*) — the subject is what's "thrown under": a king's *subjects* live under his power, and the *subject* of a sentence sits under the action.

**jacere** is a top-tier GRE root — *project* (throw forward), *eject*, *inject*, *reject* (throw back), *dejected* (thrown down), *conjecture* (throw ideas together), *trajectory* (the path of a throw), *abject* (thrown away).

# **subvert** *(v.)* — **suhb-VERT**

### Means
To **undermine and overturn** something established — a government, an authority, a rule, a convention — usually by working from *inside* or *underneath* it rather than attacking it head-on.

The method is what marks the word: an army *overthrows* a regime, but a mole *subverts* it. Subversion is quiet, internal, corrosive — it hollows the thing out until it collapses. Applied to power it's an accusation (*subversive elements*, *subverting democracy*); applied to art, genre, or stereotype it flips positive — a film that **subverts expectations** is being praised for cleverness, not condemned.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **sub**marine attacks from *below the surface* — and *vert* is a **turn**. Subvert = turn it over from underneath, unseen.

The whole point of the word is that the damage comes from below and out of sight, not from a frontal assault.

### In sentences
1. The pamphlets were banned on the grounds that they sought to **subvert** lawful authority.
2. She kept smiling and agreeing in meetings while quietly **subverting** every decision he made.

### In the wild
"**Subverting expectations**" is now standard film-criticism vocabulary — the phrase the internet argued about for years over *Star Wars: The Last Jedi*, where a twist that overturns what the audience assumed is either brilliant or infuriating depending on who's talking.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** (*under*) + **vertere** (*to turn*) = "to turn from below, overturn."

*Vertere* is a GRE workhorse: **invert** (turn in), **avert** (turn away), **divert** (turn aside), **revert** (turn back), **convert** (turn with), **versatile** (easily turned), **vertigo** (the spinning), **controversy** (turned against each other).

# **timorous** *(adj.)* — **TIM-er-uhs**

*Three syllables, stress on the first — not "tim-OR-us."*

### Means
Fearful and nervously hesitant; lacking the nerve to act.

*Timid* is plain and can be affectionate — a timid child, a timid smile. **Timorous** is bookish and faintly withering: it paints someone shrinking, hedging, trembling at a decision they ought to make. You'd call a shy toddler timid; you'd call a committee that won't commit to anything **timorous**. It describes a settled disposition, not a passing flutter of nerves.

**timorous ≠ temerity.** They look like cousins and mean opposites: **timorous** = shrinking with fear; **temerity** (and *temerarious*) = reckless nerve. The timorous clerk lacked the temerity to speak up.

### Trick to lock it in
> **TIM-er-uhs** — say it fast and it's *tremulous*: the trembling kind of scared.

The near-homophone carries the nuance — not merely quiet, but visibly quivering at the thought of acting.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador's **timorous** reply satisfied no one: it conceded nothing and defended nothing.
2. Don't be so **timorous** — just ask her for the raise.

### In the wild
Robert Burns, *To a Mouse*: **"Wee, sleekit, cow'rin, tim'rous beastie"** — the small creature bolting from the plough is the picture of the word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **timēre** = *"to fear."* It gives you **timid**, **timorous**, and **intimidate** (literally "to put fear *into*").

# **tortuous** *(adj.)* — **TOR-choo-uhs**

*Three syllables. *Torturous* has four: TOR-cher-uhs.*

### Means
**Literally:** full of bends and turns — a tortuous road, a tortuous mountain river.
**Figuratively:** so twisting that it's hard to follow — tortuous reasoning, a tortuous negotiation, tortuous prose.

The complaint is about the *path*, not the pain. A tortuous explanation isn't painful, it's serpentine: it goes there eventually, by way of everywhere else. *Circuitous* is neutrally roundabout and *convoluted* stresses tangle, but *tortuous* carries a faint whiff of evasion — call someone's logic tortuous and you're hinting they twisted it that way on purpose.

**tortuous ≠ torturous.**
- **tortuous** = full of twists (a road, an argument)
- **torturous** = causing agony (a torturous three-hour wait)

The extra **r** is the one on the **r**ack.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tor-tu-ous** = a **tour** with too many turns.

You get there in the end, but by a route nobody would design on purpose.

### In sentences
1. The treaty emerged only after eighteen months of **tortuous** negotiation, each clause bought with a concession elsewhere.
2. His excuse was so **tortuous** that by the end I'd forgotten what he was apologising for.

### Where it comes from
Latin **torquēre** = *"to twist."* It powers a huge GRE family: **torque** (twisting force), **contort**, **distort**, **extort** (twist money out of someone), **retort** (a reply twisted back at you), **torsion** — and **tortilla**, the little twisted-and-rolled flatbread.

**Torture** comes from the same root — the rack twisted the *body*. So both words twist; the question is only what's being twisted. Road → **tortuous**. Victim → **torturous**.

# **tractable** *(adj.)* — **TRAK-tuh-buhl**

### Means
Easy to manage, control, or deal with. Of a **person or animal**: obedient, easily led. Of a **problem**: solvable, workable with the tools you have.

Applied to people it is faintly condescending — praise from the person holding the reins. A tractable employee isn't *enthusiastic*, just **compliant**; *docile* stresses gentleness, *pliable* stresses being reshaped, but *tractable* stresses being **steerable by someone else**. Applied to problems it loses all that and turns purely technical: a tractable equation is one that yields to method rather than defeating it. Its negative twin **intractable** — a stubborn person, an unsolvable problem, a chronic pain that won't respond to treatment — shows up on the GRE more often than the positive form.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tractable = tract*or*-able** — you can tow it wherever you want.

The credit belongs to the driver, not the thing being pulled, which is exactly why calling a person tractable is a backhanded compliment.

### In sentences
1. The new recruits proved unusually **tractable**, absorbing the drill without a murmur of complaint.
2. Break the whole thing into small chunks and suddenly it's a **tractable** problem.

### In the wild
Standard shop-talk in tech and research: a problem is **"computationally tractable"** if it can actually be solved in reasonable time — and **"intractable"** if the compute blows up. If you've sat in an engineering review, you've heard it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **trahere, tractus** = *"to pull, to drag."* Tractable = pullable.

That root drags a whole GRE family behind it: **tractor** (pulls), **traction** (grip that pulls), **attract** (pull toward), **retract** (pull back), **extract** (pull out), **subtract** (pull away), **protract** (drag out), **contract** (pull together), even **treaty** and **trace**.

# **transient** *(adj. / n.)* — **TRAN-zee-uhnt**

*Also heard as TRAN-shuhnt or TRAN-see-uhnt; all three are standard.*

### Means
**Adj.:** lasting only a short time; passing through rather than staying.
**Noun:** a person who stays somewhere briefly — a short-stay guest, or (in American news and police usage) a drifter with no fixed address.

Of all the "short-lived" words, this is the cold one. *Ephemeral* and *evanescent* are for sunsets and beauty; *fleeting* has a sigh in it. **Transient** is the word an engineer, a doctor, or a hotel manager uses — a transient voltage spike, a transient population, a transient fever. It reports impermanence as a fact, with no wistfulness attached, which is exactly what makes the noun sense sound so clinical when it's applied to a human being.

⚠️ **transient ≠ intransigent.** *Transient* = passing through. *Intransigent* = refusing to budge an inch. They share a look, not a root — and they're near-opposites in spirit.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Trans-i-ent = in *transit*.** Anyone in transit is on the way somewhere else.

Same root, same idea — the thing is only passing through, which is a statement of logistics, not a lament.

### In sentences
1. The town's population is largely **transient**: oil crews arrive in March and the streets are empty again by autumn.
2. Don't panic about the error message — it's **transient**, it clears itself after a reboot.

### In the wild
A **TIA** — *transient ischaemic attack*, the "mini-stroke" — is named for exactly this word: the blockage passes, the symptoms clear within minutes, and that's precisely why people ignore it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **trans** (*across*) + **īre** (*to go*) → *transīre*, "to pass through." The present participle *transiēns* is literally **"going across."**

**īre** is a workhorse root worth banking: **transit**, **transition**, **exit** (*ex-it* — "he goes out"), **itinerant**, **circuit**, **perish** (*per-īre*, "to go through to the end").

# **ubiquitous** *(adj.)* — **yoo-BIK-wih-tuhs**

*Four syllables — not "you-bick-wit-ous."*

### Means
Present **everywhere at once**; found in every place you look.

Stronger than *common* or *widespread*. *Common* is a statement of frequency; **ubiquitous** is a statement of inescapability — the thing has saturated the environment so completely you stop noticing it. It also carries a faint weariness when applied to fads, logos, or slogans: *ubiquitous* often means "and frankly, enough." Applied to infrastructure (wifi, sensors), it's purely neutral and slightly admiring.

**ubiquitous ≠ iniquitous.**
- **ubiquitous** = everywhere
- **iniquitous** = grossly unjust, wicked (*iniquity* = sin)

An iniquitous regime; a ubiquitous advert.

### Trick to lock it in
> You can't **quit** it — it's already everywhere. (ubi-**QUIT**-ous)

The hidden *quit* holds the nuance: a ubiquitous thing isn't merely frequent, it's something you can't get away from.

### In sentences
1. By the late 1990s the mobile phone had become **ubiquitous**, altering the texture of public life before anyone thought to object.
2. That song was **ubiquitous** last summer — supermarket, taxi, gym, wedding, my own head at 3am.

### In the wild
**Ubiquitous computing** is a real, named field — Mark Weiser's 1988 term at Xerox PARC for computers dissolving into everyday objects. That's the world you now live in, and it's why the word turns up constantly in tech writing.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ubīque** = *"everywhere,"* from **ubi** = *"where."*

Same root gives **ubiquity**, and **ubiquitin** — the protein was literally named for being found in every cell they looked in.

# **underscore** *(v. / n.)* — **UN-der-skor**

### Means
**Verb:** to emphasize; to make a point impossible to miss.
**Noun:** the line drawn under text — and the `_` key.

On the GRE it's almost always the verb, and the giveaway is *who* does the underscoring: usually not a person but an **event, a fact, a statistic**. A crash *underscores* the need for regulation. The word carries a quiet implication — the importance was already there; the event merely made it visible. That's what separates it from *stress* or *insist* (a speaker pushing) and from *exaggerate* (inflating). Underscoring adds no volume, just a pointing finger. Neutral, slightly formal, at home in editorials and reports.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **score** is a scratch — you *scratch a line under* the sentence you don't want skipped.

The mark itself does nothing to the sentence; it just makes the reader's eye stop there, which is exactly what the verb means.

### In sentences
1. The committee's own data **underscored** the fragility of the system it had been defending for a decade.
2. Losing my keys twice in one week really **underscores** the fact that I need one place to put them.

### In the wild
It's the house verb of serious journalism — every news report ends with some version of *\"the incident underscores growing concerns about…\"*. Spot that sentence once and the word is yours.

### Where it comes from
**Score** comes from Old Norse *skor* = *"a notch, a cut."* Debts and points were once tallied by cutting notches into a stick — hence *keep score*, *settle a score*, and *scoreline*. **Under + score** = cut a line underneath.

# **venal** *(adj.)* — **VEE-nuhl**

### Means
Open to bribery; capable of being bought. Used of officials, judges, police, journalists — and of the systems they rot.

Narrower and nastier than **corrupt**. *Corrupt* covers any kind of rottenness — cronyism, cover-ups, abuse of power. *Venal* names one specific mechanism: **there is a price, and you can pay it.** And unlike *mercenary*, which merely means money-motivated (a mercenary lawyer is legal, just cold), *venal* means the person's duty itself is on sale. It is a contemptuous word — you accuse someone of venality, you don't confess to it.

⚠️ **venal ≠ venial.**
- **venal** = *bribable, for sale*
- **venial** (VEE-nee-uhl) = a **minor, forgivable** sin — the opposite of mortal

A venial fault gets you a shrug. A venal one gets you indicted.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ven**al like a **vend**ing machine — insert money, the official dispenses the verdict you want.

Same root as *vend*: the venal man isn't merely dishonest, he's stocked and priced.

### In sentences
1. The republic did not fall to invaders; it was hollowed out by a **venal** senate that auctioned its provinces to the highest bidder.
2. Every permit took three months unless you knew which **venal** clerk to slip an envelope to.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vēnum** = *"thing for sale"* → **vēnālis**, "on sale." Same source as **vend**, **vendor**, **vending machine** — literally the goods-for-money family.

🚫 **Not *venial*.** That's Latin **venia**, *"forgiveness, indulgence"* — a venial sin is one the Church will pardon. Different root, opposite moral weight.

# **venerate** *(v.)* — **VEN-uh-rayt**

### Means
To regard with **deep, reverential respect** — the kind owed to saints, elders, sacred texts, and the honoured dead.

*Admire* is about qualities; *respect* is about standing; **venerate** is one notch below worship. It carries incense on it: age, holiness, tradition, a figure placed above ordinary criticism. That's also why it turns sour so easily — say a nation **venerates** its founders and you're hinting the reverence has stopped being examined.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ven-ERATE** — you don't just *rate* them highly, you rate them as **divine**.

The hidden *rate* fixes it as an act of ranking someone, and "divine" marks how far above the scale veneration puts them.

### In sentences
1. The village still **venerates** the hermit's grave, leaving bread and coins on the stone each spring.
2. My grandad **venerates** that car — you're not allowed to eat in it, sit in it, or breathe near it.

### In the wild
The Catholic Church draws a formal line between **veneration** of saints and relics and **worship**, which is reserved for God alone — the distinction that gives the word its exact altitude: as high as you can go without crossing into worship.

### Where it comes from
Latin **venerārī** = *"to revere,"* from **Venus, veneris** — the goddess, and before that "love, desire, charm."

🚫 The false friend that isn't: **venereal** comes from the *same* root. Venus split into the sacred branch (**venerate, venerable, veneration**) and the carnal one (**venereal**). Same goddess, opposite ends of her portfolio.
