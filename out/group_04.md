# **abstain** *(v.)* — **ab-STAYN**

### Means
To **deliberately hold back** from doing something you could perfectly well do — drinking, eating, speaking, voting.

The key is that it's chosen and usually sustained. You don't abstain because you can't; you abstain because you've decided not to, often on principle, health, or religious grounds. *Refrain* is a single moment of restraint ("he refrained from replying"); *abstain* is a standing policy ("she abstained for a year"). It also has a precise institutional sense: to formally decline to vote either way — present, counted, but neither yes nor no.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ab-STAIN** — you stay *away* so it can't *stain* you. The stain was available; you declined it.

The hidden word "stain" locks in the flavour of self-denial by choice, not by inability.

### In sentences
1. Three members voted in favour, two against, and the chairman **abstained**, unwilling to commit the board either way.
2. He's **abstaining** this month — no beer, no wine, nothing, and he's insufferable about it.

### In the wild
Any committee, parliament, or UN vote has three buttons: **Yes**, **No**, and **Abstain** — the official way to be present and still refuse to pick a side.

### Where it comes from
Latin **abs-** (*away*) + **tenēre** (*to hold*) — literally *to hold yourself away from*.

That **tenēre** is a workhorse root: **contain, retain, detain, sustain, tenacious, tenet, tenure, tenable, abstinence**. Anything that grips or holds.

# **approbation** *(n.)* — **ap-ruh-BAY-shuhn**

### Means
Formal or official **approval; praise given from a position to bestow it** — a nod that counts.

Approval is what a friend gives you; approbation is what an institution, a critic, or a superior gives you. It carries weight and audience: it's the word for a board's blessing, a public's acclaim, a mentor's endorsement. Slightly stiff-collared and old-fashioned — nobody says it about a sandwich.

⚠️ **approbation ≠ opprobrium.** Same *prob-* root, opposite poles: **approbation** = public praise; **opprobrium** = public disgrace.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Approbation = approval + ovation.**

It's approval delivered publicly and formally, with something of an audience clapping behind it.

### In sentences
1. The proposal was adopted only after it won the **approbation** of the standing committee.
2. He kept glancing at his dad after the recital, fishing for a bit of **approbation**.

### In the wild
Jane Austen's characters live on this word — in *Pride and Prejudice* people are forever seeking or withholding one another's **approbation**, which tells you the register exactly: drawing-room, formal, socially official approval.

### Where it comes from
Latin **probare** = *"to test, to prove good"* (from *probus*, "upright, honest"), plus *ad-* "toward" — so *approve after testing*.

That root pays out across the exam: **probity** (proven integrity), **probation** (a testing period), **probe**, **probable**, **reprobate** (tested and found *bad*), **opprobrium**.

# **cherish** *(v.)* — **CHER-ish**

### Means
To hold something **dear** and care for it tenderly — over time, not in a flash.

Sitting between *like* and *love*, it adds two things neither has: **protectiveness** and **duration**. You *treasure* a watch; you *cherish* the grandmother who gave it to you. It also takes abstractions — you can cherish a hope, a memory, a belief — and there the word turns faintly wistful, sometimes ironic: to *cherish an illusion* is to keep hugging something you should have let go of.

### Trick to lock it in
> Look at what it takes as an object: memories, moments, people, hopes — never things you could simply buy again.

The collocations give away the nuance — cherishing is reserved for the irreplaceable, which is why it always carries a trace of fear of loss.

### In sentences
1. He **cherished** the letters long after the hand that wrote them had stilled, rereading them each winter.
2. Look, just **cherish** this — you'll never all be in the same room again.

### In the wild
The wedding vow: *"to have and to hold… to love and to **cherish**, till death do us part."* Note that it's the *slow* verb in the line — not passion, but a promise of ongoing care.

### Where it comes from
Old French **cher** = *"dear"* (as in *ma chérie*), from Latin **cārus**, *"dear, costly."*

Same root: **charity** (*cāritas*, dearness → love for others) and **caress**. If something is *cher*, it is both beloved and expensive — the word never quite forgets the price of losing it.

# **corroborate** *(v.)* — **kuh-ROB-uh-rayt**

### Means
To **support a claim with independent evidence** — a second witness, a document, a lab result that backs up what was already asserted.

It is a *supporting* word, not a *proving* word. Corroboration always arrives second: there must be a story on the table first, and the corroborating fact strengthens it without being the whole case. And it must come from elsewhere — your own repetition corroborates nothing. Register is formal: courtrooms, journalism, science. In plain speech people say *back up*.

⚠️ **corroborate ≠ collaborate.**
- **corroborate** = *confirm* someone's account
- **collaborate** = *work together* on something

Near-identical sound, opposite jobs — the GRE loves this pair.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Co-**rroborate: the *co-* means it takes someone **else** — you can never corroborate your own story.

That prefix carries the whole nuance: corroboration is by definition second-hand support, so a witness repeating himself adds nothing.

### In sentences
1. Two independent laboratories **corroborated** the finding before the journal would print it.
2. Nice alibi — got anyone who can **corroborate** that?

### In the wild
Every police procedural and courtroom drama runs on the line *"Can anyone corroborate your story?"* — which is also exactly how lawyers and journalists use it at work: one source is a rumour, two is a fact.

### Where it comes from
Latin **robur** = *"oak, hardwood, strength."* To corroborate is literally to **strengthen together** — you put an oak beam under someone's claim.

Same root gives **robust** (oak-strong) and **roborant** (a strengthening tonic). 🚫 The *-labor-* in **collaborate** is a different root entirely (*labor*, "work") — that's why the two words mean unrelated things.

# **disparate** *(adj.)* — **DIS-puh-ruht**

*Also heard as dis-PA-ruht; the first-syllable stress is standard.*

### Means
Different in **kind**, not just in degree — so unlike that the things resist being compared or lumped together.

Two shirts of different colours are *different*; a shirt, a tax code and a whale are *disparate*. The word is usually doing one of two jobs: insisting that a comparison is illegitimate ("these are disparate cases"), or flagging that a collection is a grab-bag of mismatched parts ("**disparate** sources", "**disparate** systems"). Formal and analytical — it's the vocabulary of reports and arguments, not of conversation.

⚠️ **disparate ≠ desperate.** *Desperate* (DES-puh-ruht) is hopeless, frantic, willing to do anything. Nearly the same sound, opposite half of the dictionary — the GRE will happily exploit the eye-slip.

### Trick to lock it in
> **dis-PAR-ate = not on *par*.** Different things you can still rank; disparate things you can't even score on the same card.

The hidden **par** ("equal") is the whole point: disparate items aren't merely unequal, they're not on comparable terms at all.

### In sentences
1. The committee's report stitched together **disparate** strands of evidence — census data, coroners' records, a century of parish registers — into a single argument.
2. Our team is five people from five **disparate** backgrounds, and somehow the standups still work.

### In the wild
**"Disparate impact"** — the standard term in employment and housing law for a neutral-looking rule that lands unequally on different groups. If you've read a hiring-policy or compliance document, you've met the word there.

### Where it comes from
Sits in the family of Latin **par** = *"equal"* — the **par** of *parity*, *par for the course*, *peer*, and *disparity* (from **dispar**, "unequal"). Lock that root in and you get several GRE words at once.

# **emulate** *(v.)* — **EM-yuh-layt**

### Means
To imitate someone **in order to match or surpass them** — copying with ambition behind it.

The word carries admiration and rivalry at once. *Imitate* is neutral and can be mindless; *mimic* and *ape* are faintly insulting, suggesting a copy with nothing behind it. **Emulate** always points upward: you emulate a mentor, a champion, a predecessor — someone whose level you want to reach. Praise the copier, not mock him.

(Computing has borrowed it flatly: one machine **emulating** another just means reproducing its behaviour, no ambition involved.)

⚠️ **emulate ≠ simulate.**
- **emulate** = actually *do what the model does*, to match it
- **simulate** = *fake* it, or model it for study

A flight simulator pretends to be a plane. An emulator genuinely runs the other machine's software.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Emulate** rhymes with **elevate** — you copy someone to rise to their level.

The rhyme locks in the direction of the word: emulation is imitation aimed upward, at someone better than you.

### In sentences
1. Successive Mughal emperors sought to **emulate** Akbar, and each fell short in a different way.
2. He started waking at five and reading two books a week, trying to **emulate** his older sister.

### In the wild
Playing old Nintendo or PlayStation games on a laptop means running an **emulator** — software pretending to be the original console, well enough to run its actual cartridges.

### Where it comes from
Latin **aemulus** = *"rival."* Not "copier" — **rival**. Emulation was born competitive: you match your rival's feat in order to beat it. That buried rivalry is exactly what separates *emulate* from *imitate* on the exam.

# **enervate** *(v.)* — **EN-er-vayt**

*Stress the first syllable — not "ee-NER-vayt".*

### Means
To **drain of energy, vitality or force**; to leave someone weak and listless.

This isn't ordinary tiredness. *Tire* is what a run does to you; **enervate** is what a swampy climate, a long illness, or years of soft living do to you — a slow sapping that leaves you limp and without will. It carries a hint of moral softening as well as physical weakness, and it's a formal, written word: heat, luxury, and bureaucracy enervate; a gym session just tires you out.

⚠️ **enervate ≠ innervate.**
- **enervate** = take the nerve *out* → weaken
- **innervate** = supply nerves *in* → stimulate, activate

One letter, opposite directions. (And despite the look of it, **enervate** is the opposite of *energize*, not a synonym.)

### Trick to lock it in
> **Enervate** wears *energize*'s face — but that **E** is for **empty**.

The word looks like a boost and delivers the reverse: a slow draining that leaves you limp rather than merely tired.

### In sentences
1. Months of tropical damp had **enervated** the whole garrison; by August the men could barely shoulder their rifles.
2. That three-hour status meeting **enervated** me — I got home and just stared at the wall.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ē-** (*out*) + **nervus** (*sinew, tendon, nerve*) — literally *"to cut the sinews out."* A hamstrung animal is the picture: still alive, no strength left.

Same root: **nerve, nervous, unnerve, innervate** — and note that *nervus* first meant **sinew**, which is why "he has some nerve" means gall, not neurons.

# **ephemeral** *(adj.)* — **uh-FEM-uh-ruhl**

### Means
Lasting a very short time; here and then gone.

Compare the neighbours: **temporary** is administrative (a temporary badge), **transient** is clinical or technical (a transient fault), **fleeting** is emotional (a fleeting glance). **Ephemeral** is the literary one, and it usually carries a hint of admiration — the shortness is part of what makes the thing lovely. Cherry blossom, a sunset, a mood, internet fame. It also has a hard technical life in engineering (ephemeral storage, ephemeral keys, ephemeral containers), where it means *short-lived by design, don't rely on it*.

⚠️ **ephemeral ≠ ethereal.**
- **ephemeral** = short-lived (about **time**)
- **ethereal** = delicate, airy, otherworldly (about **texture**)

A voice can be ethereal for three hours. That performance was ethereal, not ephemeral.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ephemeral doesn't complain about the brevity — it admires it.**

You call a rival's fame ephemeral to belittle it, but you call blossom ephemeral to praise it — the word leans wistful, not dismissive.

### In sentences
1. The exhibition collected the **ephemeral** matter of a city — tickets, handbills, receipts — none of it meant to survive the week it was printed.
2. Don't get attached to that job title, mate, it's **ephemeral** — they reorg every six months.

### In the wild
Instagram and Snapchat **Stories** vanish after 24 hours — the tech press literally calls it *ephemeral content*, and the Greek root means "lasting a day." The product and the etymology agree to the hour.

### Where it comes from
Greek **epi** (*for*) + **hēmera** (*day*) = **ephēmeros**, "lasting one day."

Same root elsewhere: **ephemera** (tickets, flyers — printed matter never meant to be kept) and **ephemeris** (the day-by-day table of where the planets will be). And the mayfly's order is **Ephemeroptera** — the insect that famously gets one day of adult life.

# **fervid** *(adj.)* — **FUR-vid**

### Means
Burning with feeling — passionate, ardent, intensely emotional.

The heat is literal in the root and audible in the word. **Fervent** admiration is warm and sincere and you'd be glad to receive it; **fervid** admiration runs a degree hotter, and a writer who picks it is often hinting the feeling has boiled past good sense — a fervid nationalist, a fervid fan, a fervid denunciation. It's a literary, slightly disapproving word: intensity observed from the outside, usually by someone cooler than the person being described.

**fervid vs. fervent.** Same root, different temperature. **Fervent** = sincerely, warmly passionate (approving). **Fervid** = feverishly passionate (often too much). A fervent prayer; a fervid mob.

### Trick to lock it in
> **FER-vid = fever + vivid** — feeling hot enough to be a symptom.

Fervent is body-warm; fervid is running a temperature.

### In sentences
1. His pamphlets were **fervid** to the point of incoherence, each page hotter and less persuasive than the last.
2. She's a **fervid** Arsenal fan — don't bring up the referee unless you've got an hour.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fervēre** = *"to boil, to seethe."* Literal boiling, then metaphorical.

One root, a free family: **fervent** (glowing), **fervour** (passion), **ferment** (bubbling — in a vat or in a country), and **effervescent** — *boiling out*, which is exactly what fizzy water and bubbly people do.

# **garrulous** *(adj.)* — **GAR-uh-luhs**

### Means
Excessively talkative — especially in a rambling, aimless way, about things that don't matter.

The judgement is built in. **Talkative** is neutral, **loquacious** is a compliment in a suit (fluent, articulate), **voluble** is speech that flows fast and easily — but *garrulous* is the listener's complaint. It implies you can't get away: the trivia, the digressions, the story about the neighbour's fence. Classically attached to old men on porches and strangers on long train journeys.

⚠️ **garrulous ≠ querulous.**
- **garrulous** = talks too much
- **querulous** = *complains* too much (whining, peevish)

Same *-ulous* ending, opposite failing: one is a chatterbox, the other is a grumbler.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Garrulous** starts like **gargle** — a noise from the throat that just keeps going.

Gargling isn't speech and it isn't content, which is exactly the accusation: sound where conversation should be.

### In sentences
1. The memoir is charming for eighty pages and then turns **garrulous**, every anecdote spawning three more.
2. Our cab driver was so **garrulous** I learned his entire divorce before we hit the motorway.

### Where it comes from
Latin **garrīre** = *"to chatter"* — an imitative word, the babble of birds. The Eurasian jay, a bird that will not shut up, is still classed as **Garrulus** today.

# **incendiary** *(adj. / n.)* — **in-SEN-dee-air-ee**

### Means
**Literal:** designed to set things on fire — an *incendiary bomb*, *incendiary device*. As a noun: an arsonist, or the bomb itself.
**Figurative — the GRE sense:** deliberately calculated to inflame anger, hatred, or violence.

The key is **intent**. *Controversial* just means people disagree; *provocative* can be playful; *inflammatory* is close but describes the effect. **Incendiary** points at the arsonist — it says someone lit this on purpose and knew what would catch. It's the word journalists reach for when a speech is one match away from a riot, and it is never a compliment.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Incendiary** hides **incense** — and to *incense* someone is to set them burning.

Same root, same fire: incendiary words are built to incense the people who hear them.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal found that his **incendiary** broadcasts had directly precipitated the massacres that followed.
2. Don't put that in the group chat — it's **incendiary** and you know it.

### In the wild
Headline vocabulary you've read a hundred times: a politician's **"incendiary remarks"** — never a neutral description, always the press saying *he lit this fire deliberately*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **incendere** = *"to set alight,"* from **candēre**, *"to glow, to burn white."*

That inner root pays off across the GRE: **incandescent** (glowing white-hot), **candle**, **candid** (shining, unclouded — hence honest), **candidate** (Romans campaigned in a bleached-white *toga candida*), and **incense** — both the smoke and the verb *to enrage*.

# **inimical** *(adj.)* — **ih-NIM-ih-kuhl**

### Means
**Harmful to; working against.** Also, more rarely, **openly hostile** (*an inimical stare*).

On the GRE it almost always arrives in the first sense and almost always with **to**: *conditions inimical to growth, a climate inimical to free speech*. That matters, because the harm is usually **structural, not personal** — a policy, a temperature, a bureaucracy. Nothing is angry at you; the conditions simply make thriving impossible. Compare *hostile*, which needs a hater, and *detrimental*, which is bloodlessly neutral about damage. **Inimical** sits between: no hater, but a real sense of opposition — the environment is set *against* the thing.

⚠️ **inimical ≠ inimitable.**
- **inimical** = *harmful to* — an insult to whatever it touches
- **inimitable** (*ih-NIM-ih-tuh-buhl*) = *impossible to copy* — high praise

One syllable apart, opposite verdicts: *his inimitable style* is a compliment; *a style inimical to clarity* is not.

### Trick to lock it in
> Strip the **in-** and you're left with **amical** — *friendly*. Inimical = **un-friendly conditions**, not an unfriendly person.

The prefix flips *amicable* into its opposite, and the "conditions" half is what keeps you from mixing it up with plain personal hostility.

### In sentences
1. The committee found the merger **inimical** to competition in every market it examined.
2. Open-plan offices are pretty **inimical** to getting any actual thinking done.

### In the wild
A regulator's stock phrase: US securities and insurance statutes repeatedly bar conduct **"inimical to the public interest."** If you ever read a licence revocation, you'll meet the word in its natural habitat.

### Where it comes from
Latin **inimīcus** = *in-* ("not") + *amīcus* ("friend") — literally **"un-friend."**

That one root pays out across the exam: **enemy** (same word, worn down through French), **amicable**, **amity**, **amiable**. Spot *amic-* and think *friend*; spot *in-amic-* and think *the opposite of friend*.

# **intimate** *(v. / adj. / n.)* — **IN-tuh-mut (adj.) / IN-tuh-mayt (v.)**

*The part of speech changes the last syllable: you are IN-tuh-mut with a friend, but you IN-tuh-MAYT a suspicion.*

### Means
**Verb (the GRE sense):** to make something known **indirectly** — to hint, to suggest without stating.
**Adjective:** closely personal, private, deeply familiar; also *detailed* ("an intimate knowledge of the tax code").

The verb is genteel and deliberate. *Imply* is the neutral logical relation; *insinuate* is sly and usually nasty; *hint* is casual. To **intimate** something is to place it delicately in the air so the other person picks it up — and so that you can deny ever having said it. It belongs to diplomats, board meetings and polite letters: the chairman *intimated* that he might not seek another term.

### Trick to lock it in
> **St**ate it and you're on record; **intim**ate it and you can still deny it.

Same *-ate* ending, half the volume — the intimated message was delivered without ever being said.

### In sentences
1. In his resignation letter he **intimated**, without once naming the minister, that the decision had been taken above his head.
2. She never actually asked me to leave — she just **intimated** it, about six times, until I got the hint.

### In the wild
Department stores file lingerie under **\"Intimates\"** — the clothes worn innermost, closest to the skin. That is the root, literally.

### Where it comes from
Latin **intimus** = *"innermost,"* the superlative of **intus** (*within*) — so an intimate friend is the one furthest inside the circle.

The verb comes from **intimāre**, *"to put inside, to make known"* — you get the thing inside the other person's head without putting it out loud.

Same *within* root: **interior, internal, intrinsic, intestine**.

# **invigorate** *(v.)* — **in-VIG-uh-rayt**

### Means
To fill with fresh energy and life — to make someone (or something) feel strong, alert and awake again.

The energy comes **from outside**: a cold swim, a hard frost, a new manager, a cash injection. You rarely invigorate yourself; something does it to you. Compare *encourage*, which lifts your morale, and *stimulate*, which merely gets a reaction — **invigorate** is bodily and bracing, a physical jolt of vitality. It stretches easily to institutions and economies (*reinvigorate the campaign*), where it means the same thing: something limp has been given its pulse back.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in-VIG-orate** — pour **vigour** *in*.

The *in-* is doing real work: vigour is being poured into you by something else, which is why the word suits cold water and new leadership rather than sheer willpower.

### In sentences
1. The mountain air, thin and freezing, **invigorated** the party more thoroughly than any breakfast could have.
2. Honestly, one strong coffee and I'm **invigorated** — ask me again at four o'clock.

### In the wild
The word lives on shower-gel and shampoo bottles — *"invigorating mint and tea tree"*. Ad copy chose it because it promises a physical jolt, not a mood.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vigēre** = *"to be lively, to thrive"* → **vigor**. Straight to **vigour, vigorous**, and — same root family — **vigil, vigilant** (lively = wakeful).

The bonus: **vegetable** belongs here too, from *vegetus*, "full of life." Eat your vegetables, be invigorated.

# **mitigate** *(v.)* — **MIT-uh-gayt**

### Means
To make something bad **less severe** — to soften the force of a harm, penalty, or risk without removing it.

The damage still happens; you are only turning the dial down. That built-in limitation is the whole point: a seawall mitigates flooding, it doesn't prevent it. Compare **alleviate**, which relieves suffering (pain, hunger, a burden) and is warmer and more human — *mitigate* is cooler and more institutional, the verb of insurers, engineers, judges and climate reports: mitigate risk, mitigate damages, mitigate emissions. Applied to a person's conduct it means *makes the offence look less bad* rather than *excuses it*.

⚠️ **mitigate ≠ militate.**
- **mitigate** *X* = soften X
- **militate against** *X* = count as strong evidence against X (military root — the facts are *fighting* your case)

"Mitigate against" is the classic error: you mitigate a problem; evidence militates against a conclusion.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **mitten** doesn't stop the punch — it just takes the sting out.

*Mit*-igate is padding, not armour: the blow still lands, only softer.

### In sentences
1. The court accepted that his youth and cooperation **mitigated** the offence, and reduced the sentence accordingly.
2. We can't stop the outage, but rolling back that one service should **mitigate** the damage.

### In the wild
Any courtroom drama's sentencing scene: the defence stands up to plead **mitigating circumstances**. Guilt is already settled — all that's left to argue is how hard the punishment lands.

### Where it comes from
Latin **mitis** = *"mild, soft"* + **agere** = *"to make, drive"* — literally *to make mild*.

That **-igate** ending is *agere* in disguise, and it recurs: **fumigate** (make smoke), **navigate** (drive a ship), **litigate**, **castigate**, **instigate** — plus **agent, agenda, agile, act**.

# **obsolete** *(adj. (also v.))* — **ob-suh-LEET**

*Also heard as OB-suh-leet; both are standard.*

### Means
No longer in use or no longer useful, because something better has taken its place — fax machines, floppy disks, a skill the industry has moved past.

The word carries **replacement**, not just age. A vintage watch is old; a pager is obsolete — the world routed around it. That's why it stings when applied to people or jobs: *outdated* says you've fallen behind, *obsolete* says there's no longer a role for you at all. As a verb (**to obsolete** something) it's business-speak: a company obsoletes its own product line by launching the successor.

⚠️ **obsolete ≠ obsolescent.**
- **obsolete** = already dead, superseded
- **obsolescent** = *on the way out*, going obsolete but still in use

DVDs were obsolescent for years before becoming obsolete.

### Trick to lock it in
> Obso-**lete** ends like de**lete** — not merely old, but deleted from use.

The ending marks the difference between something dated and something whose function has been erased by a replacement.

### In sentences
1. The committee's procedural handbook, drafted in 1974, had been rendered **obsolete** by a single line of the new statute.
2. My charger's **obsolete** — they changed the port again.

### In the wild
**Planned obsolescence** — the reason your two-year-old phone suddenly can't take the update. Products designed to go obsolete on schedule.

### Where it comes from
Latin **obsolescere**, *"to fall into disuse"* — from **solere**, *"to be accustomed."* Something obsolete is what people are no longer accustomed to using.

Same root gives **insolent**: *in- + solere*, "not customary" — behaviour outside what's accustomed, hence rude.

# **opaque** *(adj.)* — **oh-PAYK**

*Two syllables, and the -que is just a hard "k". The noun shifts both stress and vowel: opacity = oh-PASS-uh-tee.*

### Means
**Literally:** not letting light through — frosted glass, thick paint, milk.

**On the GRE, almost always figurative:** impossible to see into or understand. Opaque prose, an opaque ruling, an opaque procurement process.

The word implies a **total barrier, not a dim one**. *Obscure* means hard to make out — you squint and eventually get there. *Opaque* means the surface gives you nothing back. And because *transparency* is the civic virtue, calling an institution opaque is close to an accusation: the blockage looks chosen, not accidental.

⚠️ **opaque ≠ obtuse.** *Opaque* is the thing that can't be seen into; *obtuse* is the person who can't see. An opaque explanation defeats everyone; an obtuse listener misses a clear one.

### Trick to lock it in
> **op-*aque* = op-*packed*** — packed so solid that no light, and no meaning, gets through.

The "packed" sound carries the key nuance: the block is complete, so nothing leaks out to help you.

### In sentences
1. The committee's funding criteria remained **opaque**, and applicants were left to infer the rules from who had been rejected.
2. Sorry, I read your text four times and it's still completely **opaque** to me — what time are we meeting?

### In the wild
The **opacity** slider in Instagram, Photoshop or Figma: drag it to 100% and the layer is opaque — you can't see a thing behind it.

# **paradigmatic** *(adj.)* — **pa-ruh-dig-MAT-ik**

*The g that stays silent in paradigm (PA-ruh-dyme) is fully pronounced here.*

### Means
Serving as **the model or defining case** of something — the example that sets the pattern everything else is compared against.

It sounds like praise but usually isn't. **Exemplary** commends; **paradigmatic** merely classifies — it says *this is the standard instance of the type*, and the type can be a disaster. A paradigmatic bureaucratic failure is still a failure. Register is academic: historians, critics and scientists reach for it when they want "textbook case" without sounding casual.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Exemplary** = the best example. **Paradigmatic** = *the* example.

One word hands out praise, the other just points at the case the whole pattern is built on.

### In sentences
1. The Dreyfus affair remains the **paradigmatic** instance of a state closing ranks against inconvenient evidence.
2. That three-hour meeting with no decision was **paradigmatic** of this whole company.

### In the wild
**"Paradigm shift"** — Thomas Kuhn's phrase from *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, now thoroughly colonised by corporate slide decks. The paradigmatic case is whatever the current paradigm treats as normal.

### Where it comes from
Greek **para-** ("beside") + **deiknynai** ("to show") → **paradeigma**, a thing *shown alongside* as the pattern to copy — exactly what a model does.

That **para-** runs through *parallel, paraphrase, parody, paramedic*; **deiknynai** is cousin to Latin *dicere*/*index*, the pointing-and-showing family behind *indicate* and *digit*.

# **pedantic** *(adj.)* — **puh-DAN-tik**

### Means
Excessively concerned with minor rules, technicalities, or the parading of one's learning — correcting the comma while the building burns.

It is always an insult. The behaviour it describes is identical to *meticulous* or *scrupulous*; the difference is entirely in whether the fuss was worth it. Praise the same person and you get "rigorous"; resent them and you get **pedantic**. Built into the word is a charge of misplaced priorities — the pedant is right about the small thing and wrong about what mattered, and rather enjoys the correction.

⚠️ **pedantic ≠ pedestrian.** Both are sneers, but different ones: **pedantic** = fussing over trivial details; **pedestrian** = dull, unimaginative, ordinary. A pedantic essay nitpicks; a pedestrian essay bores.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ped-ANT-ic** — he's picking over **ants** while you're pointing at the elephant.

The hidden *ant* is the size of the detail he's chosen to die on.

### In sentences
1. His commentary on the poem was **pedantic** to the point of uselessness — six pages on the manuscript's punctuation, not a word on what the thing meant.
2. Sorry to be **pedantic**, but it's *fewer* people, not *less* people.

### In the wild
The internet's **"well, actually…"** reply — the guy who ignores your entire point to correct one detail in it. That is pedantry with a keyboard.

### Where it comes from
From Italian **pedante**, "schoolmaster" — most likely tied to Greek **paid-, paidos** = *"child."* The pedant is the schoolmaster who never left the classroom, still drilling rules at grown adults.

That root pays out widely: **pedagogy** (leading children), **pediatrician**, **encyclopedia** (a *circle of child-rearing*, i.e. general education).

🚫 **Not the *ped-* of *pedal, pedestrian, pedestal*** — that's Latin *pes, pedis*, "foot." Same-looking prefix, unrelated family.

# **placid** *(adj.)* — **PLAS-id**

### Means
Calm, still, undisturbed — a **placid** lake, a **placid** temperament, a **placid** afternoon.

The key is *absence of disturbance* rather than active peace of mind. **Serene** is elevated, almost spiritual; **tranquil** describes a setting; **placid** describes a **surface that nothing ruffles** — water, a face, a disposition. Which is why it tips so easily into faint insult: a placid person is unflappable, but also possibly unexcitable, incurious, a bit bovine. Cows are called placid. Say it of a colleague and half the room hears a compliment, the other half hears "no inner life."

⚠️ **placid ≠ pallid.** *Placid* = calm; *pallid* = **pale, drained of colour** (or, figuratively, feeble — *a pallid imitation*). One letter's difference, opposite territory.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Placid** rhymes with **flaccid** — no tension in it, and maybe no spirit either.

The rhyme carries the whole nuance: nothing is taut, nothing is rippling, which is restful on a lake and slightly damning in a person.

### In sentences
1. The river ran **placid** and glassy for a mile, giving no hint of the falls below.
2. He stayed weirdly **placid** through the whole argument, which honestly made it worse.

### In the wild
**Lake Placid**, New York — the Winter Olympics town, named for exactly the mirror-flat water the word describes. The 1999 crocodile film borrowed the name as a joke: placid lake, very unplacid contents.

### Where it comes from
Latin **placidus** = *"pleasing, gentle,"* from **placēre**, *"to please"* — the same root behind **please**, **pleasant**, **placate** (make someone pleased again), **complacent** (pleased with yourself), **implacable** (*not* able to be pleased or appeased), and **placebo** (Latin *"I shall please"* — a pill that pleases rather than cures).

# **polemical** *(adj.)* — **puh-LEM-i-kuhl**

*Also polemic (puh-LEM-ik), the noun: the attacking piece itself.*

### Means
Aggressively argumentative — written or spoken to **attack a position and demolish it**, not to weigh it.

A polemical piece is one-sided *by design*. That's the key: it isn't failed analysis, it's deliberate combat, and everyone involved knows it. Calling an essay **critical** means it examines; calling it polemical means it swings. The word is often faintly dismissive — "that's less a study than a polemic" says the author had already decided before starting — but in a debate, a philosophy seminar, or an op-ed page it can be plain description, even praise.

### Trick to lock it in
> **pole-MIC-al** — someone grabs the **mic** and goes to war with it.

A polemic is public and aimed at an opponent, never a quiet private disagreement.

### In sentences
1. His pamphlet on the reforms was frankly **polemical**, conceding nothing to the other side and pausing only to sharpen the insult.
2. Don't read the comments — that thread stopped being a discussion and went full **polemical** about an hour ago.

### In the wild
Christopher Hitchens' *God Is Not Great* is described everywhere — by admirers and critics alike — as a **polemic**: a book-length attack, not a survey.

### Where it comes from
Greek **pólemos** = *"war."* A polemic is literally warfare conducted in words, which is why the tone is always adversarial rather than exploratory.

# **precipitate** *(v. / adj. / n.)* — **pri-SIP-i-tayt**

*The verb ends **-tayt**; the adjective and noun end in a mumbled **-tuht** (pri-SIP-i-tuht).*

### Means
**Verb:** to bring something on **suddenly and ahead of its time** — *precipitated a crisis, precipitated his resignation*. In chemistry, to make a solid drop out of a solution.
**Adj.:** headlong, rash — acting before thinking. **Noun:** the solid that dropped out.

What you precipitate is nearly always bad, and usually not what you intended: the tension was already loaded, and your act merely tipped it over the edge. The word doesn't say you *created* the disaster — it says you *dropped* it early. As an adjective it's a quiet rebuke; precipitate action isn't decisive, it's undercooked.

⚠️ **precipitate ≠ precipitous.**
- **precipitate** = *hasty*, rushed (of an act or a person)
- **precipitous** = *steep*, sharply falling (of a cliff or a graph)

A **precipitous** decline in sales; a **precipitate** decision to shut the factory.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Precipitation** falls out of the sky unasked — to **precipitate** is to make something fall out early.

Same word you hear in every weather forecast: something comes down suddenly, and nobody chose the timing.

### In sentences
1. The assassination did not cause the war so much as **precipitate** it, collapsing a decade of alliances into six weeks.
2. Don't do anything **precipitate** — sleep on it, then send the email.

### In the wild
Every forecast's **"40% chance of precipitation"** is this exact word — stuff dropping out of the sky on its own schedule, not yours.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae-** (*forth*) + **caput, capitis** (*head*) = **thrown head-first off a height**. That's the whole image: the **precipice** is the cliff you go over.

The *caput* family is worth owning: **capital, captain, decapitate, per capita, chapter** (*little head*, a section heading).

# **profundity** *(n.)* — **pruh-FUN-di-tee**

### Means
**Great depth** — of thought, insight, or feeling. Also, countably, *a profundity* = a remark of that depth ("he offered a few profundities and left").

Depth, not difficulty. A dense paragraph can be merely obscure; profundity means the thought actually reaches something at the bottom. That's why the word is so often used with a raised eyebrow — *pseudo-profundity*, *mistaking obscurity for profundity*, *drunken profundities at 2am*. Straight, it is high praise and slightly grand; in the plural, it usually means someone thinks they're being deep.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pro-FOUND-ity: it has *found* the bottom. A platitude never left the surface.**

The "found" you hear in the middle is really Latin *fundus*, "bottom" — profundity is thought that got all the way down there.

### In sentences
1. Critics praised the novel's **profundity**, though a few suspected the author of dressing simple ideas in difficult sentences.
2. Three beers in, he started dispensing **profundities** about the meaning of work.

### Where it comes from
Latin **profundus** = *pro-* ("forth, toward") + **fundus** ("bottom") — literally *going toward the bottom*, first of water, then of thought.

*Fundus* is a workhorse root: **fund** (the bottom store of money), **foundation**, **fundamental**, and **founder** (a ship that goes to the bottom — not the same word as *flounder*).

# **prophetic** *(adj.)* — **pruh-FET-ik**

*The stress moves: prophet is PROF-it, but prophetic is pruh-FET-ik.*

### Means
Accurately foretelling what later happens; or having the solemn, oracular manner of a prophet.

In practice the word is a **verdict handed down afterwards**. A forecast is *prophetic* only once reality has confirmed it — you don't call a prediction prophetic while it's still pending. That's what separates it from *prescient* (clear-sighted about the future, judged on the thinking) and *ominous* (foreboding, judged on the mood): **prophetic is scored on the outcome.** It also carries a faint chill of the supernatural — the speaker knew more than they had any right to know.

⚠️ **prophecy ≠ prophesy.**
- **prophecy** *(PROF-uh-see)* = the noun, the prediction itself
- **prophesy** *(PROF-uh-sye)* = the verb, the act of predicting

He **prophesies**; what he utters is a **prophecy**.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nobody calls a prediction **prophetic** about tomorrow — only about yesterday's warning that came true.

The word is awarded in hindsight, which is why it almost always shows up with *turned out to be* or *eerily*.

### In sentences
1. His 1998 essay on the fragility of global credit markets now reads as grimly **prophetic**.
2. Remember when you said that group project would collapse? Bit **prophetic**, mate.

### In the wild
"The Simpsons predicted it" — the running internet joke about old episodes matching later news. The headlines write themselves: *eerily prophetic*. Nobody said it in 1995; everybody says it now.

### Where it comes from
Greek **prophētēs** = *"one who speaks forth"* — **pro-** (forth, before) + **phēnai** (to speak). The prophet's job was speaking *on behalf of* a god, and only secondarily speaking *ahead of* time.

That **phē-/phe-** root for speech runs everywhere: **euphemism** (speaking well of), **blasphemy** (speaking harm), **aphasia** (loss of speech).

🚫 **Not *profit*.** Identical in speech, no relation — that's Latin *proficere*, "to make progress."

# **prudent** *(adj.)* — **PROO-duhnt**

### Means
Showing good judgement about the future — acting with foresight, caution, and an eye on the consequences.

Not the same as merely cautious. A cautious person avoids risk out of nerves; a **prudent** one has weighed the odds and chosen the safer path deliberately. It's praise, but sober, adult praise — the word belongs to money, policy and risk, and it carries a faint chill of the unexciting. Call someone's decision prudent and you're saying it was sensible, not that it was brave.

⚠️ **prudent ≠ prudish.** *Prudent* = wisely cautious about consequences. *Prudish* = squeamishly proper about sex and propriety. A prudent banker; a prudish aunt.

### Trick to lock it in
> It's always a **prudent investment**, a **prudent reserve**, *prudent to wait* — never a prudent adventure.

The company prudent keeps tells you its meaning: it's the vocabulary of counting the cost before you spend.

### In sentences
1. The committee judged it **prudent** to hold reserves against a downturn no one had yet forecast.
2. Might be **prudent** to check the weather before you book that hike.

### In the wild
**Prudential**, the insurance giant — the whole business is charging you today for a risk that arrives tomorrow, which is prudence in one word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prūdēns**, a squashed form of **prōvidēns** = *"foreseeing"* (**pro-** ahead + **vidēre** to see). Prudence is literally seeing ahead.

Unsqueeze it and the family opens up: **provident** (saving for the future), **improvident** (reckless with it), **providence**, **provide**, **vision**, **evident**.

# **punctilious** *(adj.)* — **punk-TIL-ee-uhs**

### Means
Exact about the small points of correct **form** — etiquette, protocol, procedure, ceremony. A punctilious host seats the guests in the right order; a punctilious officer salutes the right way every time.

*Meticulous* is about the accuracy of the **work**; punctilious is about the propriety of the **conduct**. That's why the word lives in diplomacy, the military, and old-fashioned manners. Tone swings on context: it praises someone reliable to the last detail, but tilts toward fussy when the details being guarded are pointless ones.

**punctilious ≠ punctual.** Same Latin root, different point: **punctual** = arrives on time; **punctilious** = observes every rule of proper form. A punctilious man may still be late.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Punctual + tedious = punctilious** — on the dot about every tiny point, sometimes to the edge of tedium.

The blend holds both halves of the word: exactness about small points, plus the faint sigh it can provoke.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador was **punctilious** in matters of precedence, and would not begin the dinner until every delegation was seated in its correct rank.
2. He's **punctilious** about the group chat — full sentences, proper punctuation, and a reply to every single message.

### Where it comes from
Latin **punctum** = *"a point"* → Italian **puntiglio** = *"a fine point, a small scruple, a point of honour."* Punctilious is literally *full of fine points*.

The root pays out across the exam: **punctual** (on the dot), **punctuate** (put in the points), **puncture** (make a point-hole), **compunction** (a pricking of conscience), **pungent** (sharp enough to prick the nose).

# **recondite** *(adj.)* — **REK-uhn-dyte**

*Also heard as ri-KON-dyte; the first-syllable stress is the standard one.*

### Means
Dealing with subject matter so deep, technical or out-of-the-way that only specialists can follow it — and, of a person, steeped in exactly that kind of learning.

The obscurity is in the **difficulty**, not in any attempt to hide. *Arcane* suggests secrets guarded by initiates; *esoteric* suggests knowledge held by an in-group; **recondite** simply means you'd need years of study to reach the bottom of it. It's a formal, bookish word, and it cuts both ways: applied to a monograph it's near-admiring, applied to someone's dinner-party remarks it's a polite way of saying nobody understood a word.

### Trick to lock it in
> **re-CON-dite** — the **CON** is *hidden* right in the middle of it.

That buried CON is literally the root (*condere*, to stash away), and it's the giveaway that recondite knowledge isn't secret, just stored too deep for casual readers to reach.

### In sentences
1. His footnotes trail off into **recondite** disputes over fourteenth-century tax rolls that perhaps six people alive can adjudicate.
2. Ask him about coffee and you get twenty minutes of **recondite** stuff about grind burrs and water hardness.

### Where it comes from
Latin **recondere** = *"to put back, store away, hide"* — *re-* (back) + *condere* (to put together, stow).

The same *condere* gives you **abscond** (*abs-* away + *condere* — to stow yourself away and vanish). Recondite learning is shelved deep in the stacks; the absconder has stowed himself somewhere you can't find him.

# **scrupulous** *(adj.)* — **SKROO-pyuh-luhs**

### Means
Taking great care to do what is **right** — honest, principled, refusing the shortcut even when nobody would notice. By extension, **painstakingly exact** in carrying something out.

The moral sense is the core one. *Meticulous* and *thorough* describe a standard of workmanship; **scrupulous** describes a conscience. A meticulous accountant gets the columns to balance; a scrupulous accountant won't fudge them. When it does mean "exact" — *scrupulous attention to detail* — it still carries a whiff of duty, of care owed rather than care taken.

⚠️ **scrupulous ≠ scurrilous.**
- **scrupulous** = morally careful (high praise)
- **scurrilous** = grossly abusive, slanderous (a *scurrilous* rumour)

Near-identical shape, opposite moral verdict.

### Trick to lock it in
> You meet **un**scrupulous far more often than *scrupulous* — and never about a tidy desk.

The common negative is always an accusation of dishonesty, which fixes the positive as *moral* care rather than mere neatness.

### In sentences
1. The committee's report was **scrupulous** in acknowledging every dissenting voice, even those it went on to reject.
2. He's annoyingly **scrupulous** about splitting the bill — he'll work out who had the extra coffee.

### In the wild
Consumer-protection boilerplate runs on the negative: warnings about **unscrupulous** lenders, landlords, and brokers — the word regulators reach for when they mean "will cheat you and sleep fine."

### Where it comes from
Latin **scrūpulus** = *a small sharp pebble* — the kind in your shoe. From there it became the nagging little thing you can't ignore: a **scruple**, a prick of conscience.

So a scrupulous person is one who *feels the pebble* — who can't walk on past the small wrong. That also gives **scruple**, **unscrupulous**, **scrupulosity**.

# **tranquil** *(adj.)* — **TRANG-kwil**

### Means
Free from disturbance — calm, still, quietly peaceful.

*Calm* is often calm **after** something: the sea settles, the crowd quiets, you talk someone down. **Tranquil** implies nothing ever disturbed it in the first place — a settled, undisturbed stillness. It attaches most naturally to **places and scenes** (a tranquil lake, a tranquil village at dusk), and when applied to a person it describes an untroubled inner state rather than composure under pressure. Warm, faintly literary, always approving.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **tranquilizer** has to *make* you calm; something **tranquil** already was.

Same root, opposite route — the drug imposes stillness, while tranquil describes stillness that was never broken.

### In sentences
1. The monastery sat above the treeline, so **tranquil** that the ringing of a single bell carried for miles.
2. We found this **tranquil** little café behind the station — no music, no queue, nobody in a hurry.

### In the wild
The Moon's **Sea of Tranquility** — where Apollo 11 landed, giving the most famous radio line in history: *"Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed."* A place chosen and named for being flat, still and undisturbed.

# **vacillate** *(v.)* — **VAS-uh-layt**

*The c is soft — VAS, not VAK. Nothing to do with vaccinate.*

### Means
To swing back and forth between opinions or courses of action, never settling on one.

It describes a **pattern, not a moment**. Waver once and you're human; vacillate and you've been dithering for weeks. The word also judges: people rarely describe their own careful deliberation as vacillating. *Waver* is sympathetic, *equivocate* means you're being slippery on purpose — *vacillate* says you genuinely cannot hold a position, and the speaker's patience has run out.

⚠️ **vacillate ≠ oscillate.**
- **oscillate** = swing back and forth *regularly*, usually physical or mechanical (a pendulum, a fan, a voltage)
- **vacillate** = swing back and forth *mentally*, out of indecision

A fan oscillates. A coward vacillates.

### Trick to lock it in
> **VAS**-illate — a **vase** teetering on the shelf edge: tips one way, tips back, never commits.

The word is about the wobbling rather than the choosing, which is why it lands as an accusation of weak nerves and not of careful thought.

### In sentences
1. The board **vacillated** for three months over the merger, and by the time it committed, the offer had been withdrawn.
2. Stop **vacillating** and pick a restaurant, or I'm ordering pizza.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vacillāre** = *"to sway, to totter."* It was said of drunks and wobbly furniture long before it was said of minds — the physical unsteadiness is the original picture.

🚫 **Not from *vacuus* ("empty").** *Vacant, vacuum, vacation, evacuate* are a separate family; the shared *vac-* here is a coincidence.
