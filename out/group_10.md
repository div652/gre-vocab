# Group 10

# **acumen** *(n.)* — **AK-yuh-muhn**

*Also heard as uh-KYOO-muhn (older, more British); both are accepted.*

### Means
Keen, quick judgment in a practical arena — the ability to size up a situation and make the shrewd call.

This isn't raw brainpower or book learning. **Acumen is intelligence that pays off.** A professor can be brilliant and have no business acumen; a shop owner with no degree can have plenty. It's admiring and slightly worldly — you earn acumen through experience, not study — and it almost always comes attached to a domain: *business, political, financial, tactical, legal* acumen.

⚠️ **acumen ≠ acuity.**
- **acumen** = sharpness of **judgment** (business acumen)
- **acuity** = sharpness of **perception** (visual acuity, mental acuity)

Your eyes have acuity. Your dealmaking has acumen.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Acumen needs a job title.** Nobody "has acumen" — they have *business* acumen, *political* acumen.

The word arrives married to a field, which is exactly the nuance: it's applied shrewdness, never abstract cleverness.

### In sentences
1. Her financial **acumen** turned a failing regional bank into the most profitable arm of the group within three years.
2. He can't cook, but he's got real **acumen** for spotting which restaurants will still be open in five years.

### In the wild
Scroll any job listing on LinkedIn and you'll hit **"strong business acumen"** in the requirements — it's HR boilerplate for *knows how money actually works here*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **acumen** = literally *"a sharp point"*, from **acuere**, "to sharpen" (from **acus**, a needle). A person with acumen has a mind that comes to a point.

Same needle-sharp root runs through **acute** (a sharp angle, a sharp pain), **acuity**, and **acupuncture** — literally puncturing with needles.

# **antithesis** *(n.)* — **an-TITH-uh-sis**

*Stress lands on TITH, not on "anti" — never AN-tee-THEE-sis. Plural: antitheses, an-TITH-uh-seez.*

### Means
**1.** The direct and complete opposite of something. **2.** In rhetoric, a figure that sets two contrary ideas in balanced, parallel phrasing.

*Opposite* is a flat statement of fact; **antithesis** is a verdict. Calling someone the antithesis of professionalism doesn't just place them at the other end of a scale — it says they stand *against* the thing, that they define it by negation. The word carries debate-club formality, so it lands hardest in argument, criticism and manifesto, and it nearly always arrives in the frame "the antithesis of ___."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Anti-thesis** — not merely *unlike* your thesis, but the counter-claim planted directly opposite it.

The word wears its meaning openly: a thesis with an enemy bolted to the front, which is why it implies opposition rather than mere difference.

### In sentences
1. The committee's cautious, consensus-bound report was the **antithesis** of the bold reform its authors had promised.
2. Silent, minimalist, no logo anywhere — the place is the **antithesis** of a sports bar.

### In the wild
Armstrong's line from the Moon — *"one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind"* — is textbook **antithesis**: small against giant, man against mankind, in matched phrasing.

### Where it comes from
Greek **anti-** (*against*) + **thesis** (*a placing*, from *tithenai*, "to put"). Literally *a setting-against*.

That *tithenai* root is a GRE workhorse: **thesis** (a position put forward), **hypothesis** (placed *under* as a supposition), **synthesis** (placed *together*), **parenthesis** (placed *beside*), **epithet** (a name placed *on*).

# **ascribe** *(v.)* — **uh-SKRYBE**

### Means
To **assign** something — a cause, a quality, an authorship — **to a particular source**. Almost always in the frame *ascribe X to Y*.

It is the bookish, judgement-neutral word: you can ascribe a triumph or a disaster, a virtue or a vice, where *credit* and *blame* each pick a side. And it usually marks an **inference rather than a proven fact** — scholars ascribe a play to Marlowe because the style fits, not because they watched him write it. That hedge is why the word lives in history, criticism and law: it says *this is where I'm putting the responsibility*, not *this is settled*.

⚠️ **ascribe to ≠ subscribe to.** You **ascribe** a belief *to someone else* (you say it's theirs); you **subscribe** to a belief *yourself* (you hold it). "I don't subscribe to that theory" — not *ascribe*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A scribe** is sitting inside the word, writing your name next to the deed.

The scribe records who gets charged with it — good or bad — and he's going on the evidence, not a confession.

### In sentences
1. Nineteenth-century editors **ascribed** the anonymous elegy to Coleridge on the strength of a single borrowed image.
2. Don't **ascribe** motives to me — I said the meeting was long, not that it was pointless.

### In the wild
Sociology's **"ascribed status"** vs *achieved status*: ascribed traits are the ones stamped on you at birth — caste, race, family name — assigned rather than earned. If you've seen the pair in a textbook or a policy piece, the sense is already locked.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** ("to") + **scrībere** ("to write") — literally *to write it onto someone's account*.

*Scrībere* is a GRE workhorse: **describe** (write down), **prescribe** (write in advance — a doctor's order), **proscribe** (write someone's name on the banned list — outlaw them), **inscribe**, **subscribe**, **scripture**, **manuscript**.

# **befuddled** *(adj. (also v., past tense of befuddle))* — **bih-FUD-uhld**

### Means
Thoroughly **confused** — unable to think clearly, muddled in the head.

Where *bewildered* is wide-eyed and slightly dramatic, and *perplexed* is the polite, intellectual version, **befuddled** is soft and faintly comic. It suggests a fogged, slow-witted confusion rather than shock: the person isn't reeling, they're just not tracking. It's the word for the professor who has mislaid his glasses, the grandparent facing a self-checkout machine, the jury that the lawyer has deliberately muddled. Note also that it's usually *done to* someone — a question, a rulebook, or a drink befuddles you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Befuddled** rhymes with **muddled** — the brain is stirred, not stunned.

The rhyme keeps you on the gentle, foggy end of confusion rather than the shocked end where *bewildered* and *dumbfounded* live.

### In sentences
1. The committee's report left even its own authors **befuddled**, so densely was it hedged with qualifications.
2. I gave him the Wi-Fi password three times and he still just stood there looking **befuddled**.

### Where it comes from
From **fuddle** (1580s), *"to get drunk, to muddle with drink"* — plus the intensifier **be-** (as in *bedazzle, bewitch, besmirch*, all meaning "to thoroughly do X to someone"). So *befuddled* literally means **drunk-confused**, which is exactly the flavour it kept: hazy, slow, harmless.

That **be-** prefix is worth banking on its own — it turns a verb outward onto a victim, and it shows up all over the GRE (*beguile, belabour, bemoan, bereft*).

# **eschew** *(v.)* — **es-CHOO**

*Also heard as ess-SHOO; es-CHOO is the safer standard.*

### Means
To **deliberately avoid or abstain from** something — to keep away from it by choice.

*Avoid* is neutral: you avoid a pothole. **Eschew** carries a reason, usually a principle, a taste, or a discipline — you had access to the thing and turned it down. It's formal, slightly bookish, and almost always takes an abstract or category object: eschew violence, eschew luxury, eschew jargon, eschew meat. You can't eschew something that was never on offer.

⚠️ **eschew ≠ askew.** *Eschew* (es-CHOO) is a verb — to shun. *Askew* (uh-SKYOO) is an adjective/adverb — crooked, off-centre. A hat sits askew; a monk eschews wine.

### Trick to lock it in
> **es-CHEW** — you won't even *chew* it. The plate is pushed away on principle.

The hidden word *chew* fixes the refusal-of-something-offered sense: the food was right there and you declined it.

### In sentences
1. The order **eschews** all property, holding even a spare pair of shoes to be an indulgence.
2. He **eschews** group chats entirely — says he'd rather be unreachable than be in nine of them.

### In the wild
**"Eschew obfuscation"** — the standing joke among editors and style guides, a rule against unclear writing that breaks its own rule. If you remember the gag, you remember the word.

### Where it comes from
Old French **eschiver**, from a Germanic root meaning *"to shy away, to shun"* — the same root that gives English **shy** (and German *scheuen*). A shy creature avoids; so does someone who eschews. The related **skew / askew** branch drifted off to mean *slanting away*.

# **esoteric** *(adj.)* — **ess-uh-TERR-ik**

*Four syllables, stress on the third — not "ee-so-TERR-ik."*

### Means
Understood by, or intended for, only a **small circle of initiates** — the inner few with the special knowledge.

The key isn't difficulty, it's **access**. Quantum field theory is hard; an *esoteric* joke is one that four people in the room are equipped to get. So the word always implies an in-group and an out-group, and the speaker is usually standing outside — "esoteric debates," "esoteric jargon" carry a faint sigh of *why does this exist*. Used of mystical or occult traditions, though, it turns straight-faced and even reverent.

⚠️ **esoteric ≠ exoteric.**
- **esoteric** = for the **inner** circle
- **exoteric** = for the **outside** world, i.e. the public, popularised version

A guru's exoteric teachings go in the paperback; the esoteric ones stay with the disciples.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Esoteric — for the *e-so-few*.**

The material needn't be difficult; what makes it esoteric is that the audience is tiny and on the inside.

### In sentences
1. The society preserved a body of **esoteric** doctrine that was never committed to print and never spoken outside the order.
2. Half his references were so **esoteric** that the rest of us just nodded and hoped he'd move on.

### In the wild
Walk into any large bookshop and you'll find a shelf labelled **Esoterica** — tarot, alchemy, secret orders. Same word, used exactly as the Greeks meant it: knowledge for the initiated.

### Where it comes from
Greek **esō** = *"within."* Aristotle reportedly gave two kinds of lecture: the **esoteric** ones inside the school for enrolled students, and the **exoteric** ones outside it for the general public.

That prefix pair pays off everywhere: **eso-** (within) vs **exo-** — *exit, exodus, exoskeleton, exotic*.

# **evasive** *(adj.)* — **ih-VAY-siv**

### Means
Avoiding a direct answer or a direct hit — dodging the question, the commitment, or the pursuer.

Two lives. **Of speech:** deliberately non-committal, and almost always a soft accusation — calling someone evasive implies they have something to hide but stops short of saying they lied. It's the word journalists, lawyers and interviewers reach for because it's damning yet legally safe. **Of movement:** neutral and technical — *evasive action*, *evasive manoeuvres*, what a pilot or a driver does to avoid a collision. No guilt in that sense at all.

⚠️ **evasive ≠ elusive.**
- **evasive** = actively dodging you — *an evasive witness*
- **elusive** = hard to catch or pin down, no intent required — *an elusive scent, an elusive cure*

An evasive suspect knows you're asking. An elusive memory isn't hiding on purpose.

### Trick to lock it in
> Evasive people **evade**; elusive things **elude** — only one of them knows you're looking.

*Evasive* points at a choice being made, which is why it lands as a quiet accusation, while *elusive* is just a property of the thing itself.

### In sentences
1. Pressed three times on what he had known and when, the minister grew **evasive**, retreating into the passive voice.
2. Every time I ask what he does for work he gets weirdly **evasive** and changes the subject.

### In the wild
**"Evasive manoeuvres"** — the standing order on the bridge in *Star Trek*, and the same phrase used in real aviation and driving for a hard swerve away from a collision. That's the innocent, physical sense of the word doing its day job.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vadere** = *"to go"*, plus **ex-** = *"out"* → *evade*, "to go out of the way."

One root, a whole GRE family: **evade** (go out of), **invade** (go into), **pervade** (go through — *a smell that pervades the house*). Fix *vadere = go* and all three unlock.

# **exculpate** *(v.)* — **EKS-kul-payt**

*Also heard as eks-KUL-payt; both are accepted.*

### Means
To **clear someone of blame or guilt** — to show they weren't at fault.

This is a word of *evidence*, not of mercy. **Forgive** leaves the guilt in place and waives the punishment; **exculpate** removes the guilt itself, usually because a document, an alibi, or a witness proves the person didn't do it. It stops short of praise: to be exculpated is to be shown not-blameworthy, whereas to be **vindicated** is to be shown *right*. Register is formal and heavily legal — lawyers, inquiries, board reports. Its exact opposite, **inculpate**, is the word for evidence that points the finger back at you.

⚠️ **exculpate ≠ expurgate.** *Exculpate* frees a **person** from blame; *expurgate* strips the dirty bits out of a **text**. Same *ex-* opening, entirely different object.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ex**culpate takes the blame **ex**it; **in**culpate lets it **in**.

The prefix does all the work — the shared middle *culp* is the blame, and *ex-* / *in-* simply point it out of you or into you.

### In sentences
1. The inquiry **exculpated** the engineers entirely, laying the failure at the door of the contractor who had signed off on the welds.
2. Don't try to **exculpate** yourself — I saw you eat the last slice.

### In the wild
**"Exculpatory evidence"** is the phrase every courtroom drama and true-crime podcast runs on: in the US, prosecutors are legally obliged to hand the defence anything exculpatory — evidence that clears the accused.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** (*out of*) + **culpa** (*fault, blame*).

*Culpa* is a high-yield root: **culpable** (deserving blame), **culprit**, **inculpate**, **exculpatory**, and the confession **mea culpa** — literally *"my fault."*

# **expedite** *(v.)* — **EK-spuh-dyte**

### Means
To **make a process happen sooner** by clearing away whatever is slowing it down — paperwork, queues, approvals, red tape.

*Hasten* and *accelerate* describe raw speed; **expedite** describes **speeding up a system**. There's an implied bureaucracy, an implied backlog, and usually implied authority — someone with the power to move your file to the top of the pile. You expedite a shipment, a visa, a claim, an investigation. You don't expedite a walk, a song, or a friendship. It's a boardroom-and-back-office word, and it quietly flatters the speaker: *I know how to get things unstuck.*

⚠️ **expedite ≠ expedient.** Same Latin root, different jobs.
- **expedite** *(verb)* = **hurry it along**
- **expedient** *(adj.)* = **convenient rather than principled** — the fast option, taken because it's easy, often with a whiff of moral shortcut

*Expediting* the report is admirable. Firing the whistleblower was merely *expedient*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nobody expedites a sunset. You expedite a *request*.**

The word only attaches to things stuck in a process, which is why it always carries the smell of queues, forms and someone with the clout to skip them.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal agreed to **expedite** the hearing, citing the claimant's deteriorating health.
2. Can you call them and **expedite** it? We've been waiting three weeks for a signature.

### In the wild
Every checkout page and passport office sells you the same thing: **expedited** shipping, **expedited** processing. You pay extra not to make the truck drive faster — but to jump the queue. That fee *is* the definition.

### Where it comes from
Latin **expedīre** = *"to free the feet"* — **ex-** (out) + **pes, pedis** (foot). To unshackle someone so they can finally move.

Its exact opposite is built the same way: **impede** = *feet **in** the shackles*. Same root gives **pedestrian, pedal, pedigree, impediment,** and **expedition** (a party freed to set out).

🚫 Not the *ped-* in **pedagogy** or **pedant** — that's Greek *paid-*, "child."

# **fastidious** *(adj.)* — **fa-STID-ee-uhs**

### Means
Attentive to detail to an extreme degree — and, by extension, **hard to please**, easily put off by mess, sloppiness, or dirt.

Two shades, and the GRE uses both. Of a *worker*: exacting, nothing left unchecked. Of a *person*: fussy, faintly squeamish, offended by the imperfect.

Where **meticulous** is pure praise, *fastidious* praises and needles in the same breath — it says the care is real but has passed the point of comfort. Call an editor fastidious and you mean she catches everything; call a dinner guest fastidious and you mean he sent the plate back.

### Trick to lock it in
> fas-**TIDI**-ous — **tidy**, stressed so hard it becomes a personality.

The stressed syllable literally hides *tidi*, and that's the word: tidiness pushed past virtue into fuss.

### In sentences
1. The manuscript bears the marks of a **fastidious** editor: not a comma out of place, not a source unverified.
2. He's so **fastidious** about his kitchen that you feel bad putting a mug down.

### In the wild
Agatha Christie's **Hercule Poirot** is the type made flesh — the symmetrical breakfast eggs, the waxed moustache, the "order and method." Fastidious in both senses at once: nothing escapes him, and nothing crooked is tolerated.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fastīdium** = *"loathing, disgust"* — from *fastus* (arrogance) + *taedium* (weariness). So the original fastidious person wasn't careful; he was **disdainful**, sick of the shabbiness around him. That disgust still sits under the modern word, which is why it never reads as flat praise.

Same *taedium* gives you **tedium** and **tedious** — the bored half of the compound.

🚫 Nothing to do with **fast**. The *fasti-* is disgust, not speed.

# **feign** *(v.)* — **FAYN**

*The g is silent — one syllable, rhyming with rain.*

### Means
To **put on a false appearance** of a feeling, state, or condition — to pretend convincingly that you feel or have something you don't.

Notice what it takes as an object: you feign *surprise, ignorance, illness, indifference, sleep, enthusiasm*. It's almost always an inner state being performed outward. *Fake* is blunt and covers objects too (fake Rolex, fake passport); *feign* is bookish, and the deceit is usually social rather than criminal — the polite lie of looking interested, the schoolboy stomach ache. It implies control and composure: someone feigning is managing their face well.

**feign ≠ feint.** *Feign* is the verb for faking a feeling; a **feint** *(n.)* is a fake attack — a boxer's jab meant to draw a guard, an army's diversionary move. Same root, different jobs: you **feign** boredom, you **throw** a feint.

### Trick to lock it in
> **The classic thing to feign is a FAINT** — and the words sound nearly identical.

A staged swoon is the perfect feign: a whole bodily state performed for an audience, with nothing actually wrong.

### In sentences
1. Summoned before the committee, he **feigned** an ignorance of the accounts that no one in the room believed.
2. She **feigned** sleep until her flatmate stopped asking about the dishes.

### In the wild
Every football match where a player rolls around clutching a shin that was never touched — commentators call it exactly that: **feigning injury**.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fingere** = *"to shape, mould, invent."* The idea is sculpting something that wasn't there — which is why the same root gives you **fiction**, **figment**, **figure**, and **effigy** (a shaped likeness). Feigning is moulding an expression the way a potter moulds clay.

# **furtive** *(adj.)* — **FER-tiv**

### Means
Done quietly and quickly to avoid being noticed — sly, stealthy, hole-and-corner.

The word almost always carries guilt. *Covert* describes an operation and is neutral; *furtive* describes a **manner**, and the manner betrays the person: the darted glance, the hand sliding into a pocket, the tab closed too fast. A truly secret act is one nobody sees. A furtive act is one you *can* see — you just can't see what's behind it. That's why "furtive" in a sentence usually means the sneaking failed.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fur**tive hides a **thief** — Latin *furtum*, "theft" — and what gives him away is the glance, not the goods.

Covert means nobody notices; furtive means somebody did, which is why the word always sounds like an accusation.

### In sentences
1. Between courses he stole a **furtive** look at his watch, hoping the hostess would not see it.
2. Why are you being so **furtive** about the phone? Just show me the message.

### In the wild
Police stop-and-frisk reports are full of the phrase **"furtive movements"** — the officer's standard written justification for a search, and the exact reason civil-liberties lawyers attack it: a nervous glance isn't evidence of anything.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fur** = *thief*, **furtum** = *theft*. A furtive person moves the way a thief moves.

Same root, unexpectedly: **ferret** — Latin *furittus*, "little thief," for the animal that slinks into burrows. Which is why you **ferret out** a secret.

# **hamper** *(v. / n.)* — **HAM-per**

### Means
**Verb:** to hold back, slow down, or make difficult. **Noun:** a large lidded basket (laundry, picnic).

The verb is about **drag, not blockage**. *Thwart* defeats a plan outright; *impede* puts something in the way; **hamper** weighs you down so you can still move, just badly — clumsily, slowly, at half strength. Rain doesn't stop the rescue, it *hampers* it. Note the standard collocation: things are almost always **hampered by** something — bad weather, budget cuts, a torn hamstring, missing data. Neutral register, common in news and academic prose alike.

⚠️ **hamper ≠ tamper.** Both mean interfering with how something works, one letter apart. **Hamper** = slow it down from outside; **tamper (with)** = meddle inside it, usually to alter or sabotage. Rain hampered the investigation; someone had tampered with the evidence.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Strap a picnic hamper to your back and you're hampered — still walking, just slower.**

The basket sense gives you the verb's exact flavour: a burden that encumbers rather than a wall that stops.

### In sentences
1. Chronic underfunding **hampered** the department's research for a decade, though it never quite halted it.
2. Don't wear those boots on the hike — they'll just **hamper** you.

### In the wild
The laundry **hamper** in the corner of your room is the same word — a basket so bulky it slows you down carrying it.

# **indispensable** *(adj.)* — **in-di-SPEN-suh-buhl**

### Means
Absolutely necessary — something (or someone) you **cannot do without**.

The test is subtraction, not praise: *essential* describes what belongs to a thing's nature, *vital* is dramatic and life-or-death, but **indispensable** means take it away and the whole operation stops working. It's also an absolute — things aren't "quite" indispensable — which is why applying it to a person is the highest possible compliment, and why it's so often said sarcastically about people who think they are.

Note the spelling: **-able**, never *-ible*.

### Trick to lock it in
> Strip the **in-** and you get **dispense** — what you can hand out and be rid of.

A pharmacy *dispenses* pills across the counter and lets them go; the indispensable is the one thing that never leaves the building.

### In sentences
1. Clean water and a functioning postal service were, in the committee's view, **indispensable** to any plan for the region.
2. She's the only one who knows how the billing system actually works — she's basically **indispensable**.

### In the wild
The line attributed to Charles de Gaulle: *"The graveyards are full of indispensable men."* — the standard deflation of anyone who thinks the place can't run without them.

### Where it comes from
From Latin **dispensare**, *"to weigh out, pay out"* — built on **pendere**, *"to weigh, to hang."* A church **dispensation** was originally a weighing-up that released you from an obligation; something *dispensable* can likewise be let go.

That root pays out widely: **pending**, **pendulum**, **ponder** (weigh in the mind), **compensate**, **stipend**, **pensive**.

# **lament** *(v. / n.)* — **luh-MENT**

*The adjective flips the stress: lamentable is LAM-uhn-tuh-buhl, not luh-MENT-uh-buhl.*

### Means
**Verb:** to express grief or deep regret **out loud**. **Noun:** the expression itself — a song, poem, or outcry of mourning.

*Mourn* is the inward state; **lament** is the sound it makes. It is grief given shape and voice — which is why a lament can be composed, sung, and played at a graveside. It carries dignity, never hysteria.

The second, weaker sense is the one the GRE loves: to **lament** a decline, a lost standard, a vanished courtesy. That's regret spoken publicly with an elevated tone — a complaint that has put on a suit. Say you *lament* the state of modern journalism and you sound grave; say you *whine* about it and you sound small.

### Trick to lock it in
> **L-AMEN-T** — *amen* is hiding inside it, and grief here is spoken aloud like a prayer at a graveside.

The buried **amen** fixes the key point: a lament is uttered, performed, heard — not silently endured.

### In sentences
1. In his final essay he **lamented** the disappearance of the unhurried sentence from public life.
2. Dad still **laments** selling that car, and he's been at it for thirty years.

### In the wild
The Bible has an entire book called **Lamentations** — five poems weeping over the ruins of Jerusalem. Same root, and it is nothing but grief said out loud.

# **myopic** *(adj.)* — **mye-OP-ik**

*Also heard as mye-OH-pik; the noun is myopia, mye-OH-pee-uh.*

### Means
**Literally:** nearsighted — able to focus on what's close, blurry on anything far off.
**Figuratively (the GRE sense):** unable to see past the immediate — planning, judging, or governing with no view of long-term consequences.

It's an accusation of **narrow vision, not low intelligence**. A myopic policy is usually a clever solution to this quarter's problem that wrecks the next decade. Compare *ignorant* (doesn't know) or *foolish* (can't reason): the myopic person sees perfectly well — just not far enough. It carries a faint condescension, the tone of an analyst or editorial writer, and almost always attaches to institutions, strategies, and outlooks rather than to people's characters.

### Trick to lock it in
> **MY**-opic: all I can see is **MY** desk, **MY** quarter, **MY** problem — right now.

The word opens with *my*, which is exactly the radius of a myopic view: close, immediate, and no further.

### In sentences
1. The commission's report condemned a **myopic** energy strategy that secured cheap fuel for a decade and left the grid defenceless thereafter.
2. Cancelling training to hit the numbers this month is pretty **myopic** — who exactly is going to do the work in June?

### In the wild
It's on your glasses prescription: a minus number in the *sphere* column means **myopia**. Every optometrist uses the word literally, which is why the figurative insult lands so cleanly.

### Where it comes from
Greek **myein** (*to shut, to close*) + **ōps** (*eye*) — literally "shut-eye," from the squint nearsighted people make to sharpen a distant blur.

**ōps / opt-** is worth banking: **optic**, **synopsis** (a seeing-together), **autopsy** (seeing for oneself), **biopsy**, **Cyclops**.

# **nonchalant** *(adj.)* — **non-shuh-LAHNT**

*The ch is sh, as in French — never "non-CHAL-ant." Final t is usually pronounced in English.*

### Means
Coolly unbothered — showing no anxiety, excitement, or concern in a situation where most people would show plenty.

The word describes the **surface**, not the soul. *Indifferent* says you genuinely don't care; *nonchalant* says you don't **look** like you care — which is why it so often carries a whiff of performance. It's the shrug, the unhurried walk away from the explosion. Tone slides with context: admiring when the stakes are low (effortless cool), faintly critical when they're high (he was nonchalant about the missing money).

⚠️ **nonchalant ≠ nonplussed.** *Nonplussed* means **bewildered, at a loss** — the opposite of unbothered. The person who is nonplussed has no idea what to do; the nonchalant one just isn't showing it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nonchalant** wears its French accent like sunglasses — cool, and meant to be seen.

The word points at the visible manner rather than the inner feeling, which is why nonchalance can be faked and indifference can't.

### In sentences
1. Asked whether the verdict troubled him, the defendant gave a **nonchalant** wave, as though the whole trial had been someone else's appointment.
2. She strolled in forty minutes late, **nonchalant** as anything, holding a coffee.

### In the wild
It's become a whole personality type online — the "nonchalant" boyfriend who replies "k" to everything. That's the word doing exactly what it means: calculated unbotheredness worn as a look.

### Where it comes from
French **non** + **chaloir** = *"not to matter, not to concern,"* from Latin **calēre**, *"to be warm."* Literally: **doesn't heat up.**

That heat root is worth banking — it also gives **calorie**, **scald**, **cauldron**, **chafe**, and **chauffeur** (originally the man who *stoked* the engine).

# **partial** *(adj.)* — **PAR-shuhl**

### Means
Everyone knows sense 1: **incomplete, only a part** (*a partial refund*). The GRE wants senses 2 and 3.

**2. Biased — favouring one side.** A *partial* judge, referee, or examiner has quietly taken a side. This is the fault that **impartial** denies. It's milder and cooler than *prejudiced* or *partisan*: partiality is a soft tilt, often unconscious, often affection-driven — the teacher who marks her favourite generously. It accuses you of leaning, not of hating.

**3. *partial to* = fond of.** Only in that fixed phrase, and it's warm, mild, faintly understated: *I'm partial to a strong cup of tea.* Never "I'm partial to my wife" — it's for tastes, not loves.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Partial** hides **PART** — a partial judge *takes your part*.

To take someone's part is to side with them, which is exactly what the biased sense means — and the same lean, softened, gives you *partial to*.

### In sentences
1. The defence argued that a juror who had lunched with the prosecutor could hardly be anything but **partial**.
2. Don't ask me to pick the winner — I'm shamelessly **partial** to the underdog.

### In the wild
You meet the negative daily: news outlets and jurors swear they are **impartial**, and referees are accused of partiality every weekend. That's the word doing real work — always as the thing being denied.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pars, partis** = *"part, side."* A part of something, and a side in a quarrel, are the same word — which is why *partial* means both "incomplete" and "taking sides."

That root is everywhere: **party** (a side), **partisan** (a side-taker), **participate**, **impart**, **portion**, **apart**, **counterpart**.

# **pensive** *(adj.)* — **PEN-siv**

### Means
Deep in quiet, dreamy thought — and usually a little sad with it.

*Thoughtful* is about consideration for others; *contemplative* and *reflective* are about mental effort or depth. **Pensive** is about **mood**, and it carries a faint grey weather-front of melancholy: someone staring out of a train window, not someone solving a problem. It's also mostly an *observed* state — it describes how a person looks from the outside, which is why it attaches so easily to faces, silences and portraits.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nobody says "I feel pensive" — they say "**you look pensive**."

It's a mood read off someone's face from across the room, which is exactly why it means *quietly, sadly lost in thought* rather than *thinking hard*.

### In sentences
1. Between the two movements the conductor stood **pensive**, the baton lowered, as if reluctant to disturb what the orchestra had just done.
2. He got **pensive** after the third drink and started talking about his dad.

### In the wild
Dumbledore's **Pensieve** — the stone basin you pour memories into and brood over. Rowling built the name straight out of *pensive* (+ *sieve*), and the object does exactly what the word means.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pendere** = *"to hang, to weigh"* → **pensāre**, *"weigh carefully"* → French **penser**, *"to think."* Thinking as weighing.

That root hangs a lot of GRE words off it: **ponder** (weigh in the mind), **compensate** (counter-weigh), **suspend**, **pendant**, **pending**, **poise**.

# **portend** *(v.)* — **por-TEND**

### Means
To be a **sign or warning** that something is going to happen — to foreshadow, to be an omen of.

The subject is almost never a person: *clouds, silence, a drop in sales, a cough* portend. Things portend; people predict. And what's portended is nearly always **bad** — you can *portend* civil war, collapse, or ruin, but "portends a lovely afternoon" reads as a joke. Weightier and more solemn than *suggest* or *indicate*, which are flatly neutral; broader than *foreshadow*, which belongs to plots and novels. It's the word news anchors reach for when they want dread without saying "doom."

⚠️ **portend ≠ portentous.** *Portend* is the clean verb ("this portends trouble"). But **portentous**, its own adjective, has drifted: it usually now means **pompously self-important**, not ominous. A portentous speaker is a windbag, not a prophet.

### Trick to lock it in
> **port-END** — whatever it points to is an *ending*, not a beginning.

The hidden **end** is your reminder that the word tilts negative: things portend collapse, famine, and war far more often than they portend anything good.

### In sentences
1. The sudden withdrawal of foreign capital **portended** a crisis that the finance ministry spent a decade denying was coming.
2. That noise the engine's making? Nothing good, mate — it **portends** a very expensive Tuesday.

### In the wild
Standard market-commentary furniture: every recession cycle brings the headline *"Does the inverted yield curve portend a recession?"* — an impersonal signal, an unwelcome outcome, exactly the word's natural habitat.

### Where it comes from
Latin **portendere** = *por-* (a form of *pro-*, "forward") + **tendere**, *"to stretch"* — the omen **stretches forward** into the future.

*Tendere* is a GRE workhorse: **extend, contend, distend, tendon, tendency, intend** — and **pretend**, literally "stretch out in front" as a screen. Same stretch, different direction.

# **provincial** *(adj. / n.)* — **pruh-VIN-shuhl**

### Means
**Literally:** belonging to a province rather than the capital — *provincial government, provincial newspapers*.
**On the GRE:** narrow in outlook, unsophisticated, limited to the concerns of one small place.

The insult is aimed at the *mind*, not the postcode. A provincial person may be perfectly clever and well-off; the charge is that their frame of reference stops at the town line, so they mistake local custom for universal truth. Note who is speaking: *provincial* is a word the centre uses about the edge, and it always carries a whiff of the metropolitan sneer — which is why it can make the speaker sound as snobbish as the target sounds narrow. Compare *parochial* (same idea, colder and more institutional) and *rustic* (about manner and setting, not mental horizon).

⚠️ **provincial ≠ provisional.**
- **provincial** = *narrow-minded, from the sticks*
- **provisional** = *temporary, pending confirmation* (a provisional government, a provisional offer)

Four letters apart, nothing in common.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A provincial has seen one province — and thinks it's the world.**

The word hides its own limit: one province is the whole map to them.

### In sentences
1. Her novels were dismissed as **provincial** by critics who had never troubled to leave the capital.
2. He's not stupid, he's just **provincial** — anything past the county line may as well be the moon.

### In the wild
In France, everything outside Paris is *la province* — and when a Parisian calls something **provincial**, it is never a compliment. Same word, same sneer, in English.

### Where it comes from
Latin **provincia** = a territory conquered by Rome and run by a governor — i.e. everywhere that *wasn't* Rome. The condescension is baked in from the start: Rome was where things happened, the provinces were where you got posted.

That single root gives **province, provincial, provincialism** — all of them measuring distance from the centre.

# **rudimentary** *(adj.)* — **roo-duh-MEN-tuh-ree**

### Means
Basic and undeveloped — limited to the first rough beginnings of a thing. In biology, of an organ: only partly formed, never grown into full function (a whale's hind limbs).

Note what it does *not* say: not wrong, not broken — just barely begun. **Basic** is flat description; **rudimentary** is a measurement against what should have been there, so it usually lands as a quiet downgrade. "Rudimentary French" means you can order coffee and nothing more; "rudimentary sanitation" means a hole in the ground. Applied to a person's skill it is polite and damning at once.

### Trick to lock it in
> **RUDE** is hiding at the front — and *rude* first meant **rough, unfinished**, not impolite.

Rudimentary things are rough-hewn and stopped early, not offensive.

### In sentences
1. The excavation revealed a **rudimentary** drainage system: two stone channels and no more.
2. He's got a **rudimentary** grasp of the spreadsheet — he can open it and that's about it.

### In the wild
Standard in software release notes: "**rudimentary** support for dark mode" is the polite way of saying it technically exists and you won't enjoy it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **rudis** = *"raw, rough, unworked"* — of stone before the chisel. A **rudiment** is the first rough stage of anything.

Same root, two GRE payoffs: **rude** (originally *unpolished*, only later *ill-mannered*) and **erudite** — *e-* "out of" + *rudis*, literally **worked out of the rough**, i.e. polished by learning. Erudite is the exact opposite end of the same block of stone.

# **salutary** *(adj.)* — **SAL-yuh-teh-ree**

*Stress falls on the first syllable — not sal-YOO-tuh-ree, despite the pull of "salute."*

### Means
Producing a **good effect**, especially a corrective one; beneficial. (Older, narrower sense: health-giving.)

The thing that is salutary is usually **unwelcome at the time**. Look at what the word actually attaches to: a salutary *lesson*, a salutary *warning*, a salutary *shock*, a salutary *reminder*. It's the sharp experience that improved you — the failed exam, the near-miss, the bill that finally forced a budget. *Beneficial* is neutral about how it felt; *salutary* implies it stung and worked anyway. It's a formal, slightly stern word: the register of leader columns and school reports, not casual praise.

⚠️ **salutary ≠ salubrious.** Same root, different jobs: **salubrious** = physically healthy or wholesome *as a place or condition* (a salubrious climate, a less-than-salubrious hotel); **salutary** = beneficial *in its effect*, usually through a hard lesson.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A salutary lesson is a slap you end up saluting.**

The word hides **salut-** (as in *salute*), and that's the whole nuance: it hurt, and afterwards you acknowledge it did you good.

### In sentences
1. The collapse of the fund had at least one **salutary** consequence: no regulator could any longer pretend the risk was hypothetical.
2. Getting outplayed by a fourteen-year-old was a **salutary** experience — I've actually been practising since.

### In the wild
Every time someone raises a glass and says **"Salut!"** (or Spanish *"¡Salud!"* after a sneeze), they're using this exact root — wishing you *health*. That's the buried sense in *salutary*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **salūs, salūtis** = *"health, safety, well-being."* To **salute** someone was originally to wish health on them; a **salutation** is that wish in words; **salubrious** is the air that supplies it. *Salutary* keeps the payoff rather than the greeting: whatever leaves you better off.

# **sever** *(v.)* — **SEV-er**

*Rhymes with never — two syllables, stress up front.*

### Means
To **cut off** or **cut through** — a limb, a cable, a rope — and, figuratively, to **break a connection completely**: ties, relations, links, contact.

The force of the word is **finality**. You can weaken, strain, or loosen a relationship; when you *sever* it, there is nothing left joining the two halves. It's also the formal, almost diplomatic verb for the act — countries **sever diplomatic relations**, a firm **severs ties** with a client. In the physical sense it carries a wince: a *severed* artery, a *severed* cable, clean through.

⚠️ **sever ≠ severe.** *Sever* (SEV-er) = to cut off. *Severe* (suh-VEER) = harsh, extreme. Near-twins on the page, unrelated in root and meaning.

### Trick to lock it in
> **sever** ends in **ever** — what you sever is cut off *for ever*.

That's the whole nuance: not loosened, not strained — separated permanently.

### In sentences
1. After the memo leaked, the ministry **severed** all contact with its former ally.
2. He **severed** the cable with one snip and the whole street went dark.

### In the wild
The Apple TV+ series **Severance** — employees have their work memories surgically *severed* from their personal ones. The show is the definition acted out. (Same word behind **severance pay**: the money that goes with cutting you loose.)

### Where it comes from
Latin **separare** = *"to separate,"* worn down through Old French *sevrer*. The same root gives **separate**, **severance**, and — surprisingly — **several**, which originally meant *"separate, distinct"* before it drifted into meaning "a few."

# **slight** *(adj. / v. / n.)* — **SLYTE**

*Exactly homophonous with sleight (as in sleight of hand).*

### Means
**Adj.:** small in amount or importance — *a slight delay*, *a slight fever*; also **slender** of build.
**Verb:** to **snub** someone by treating them as unimportant. **Noun:** the snub itself.

The GRE cares about the verb. A slight is an insult delivered by *underrating*, not by attacking — you slight someone by leaving them off the list, skipping their name in the credits, greeting everyone in the room but them. Nobody raises their voice; the wound is that you weren't worth the effort. And because the offence is by definition small, the word quietly puts the injured party on trial too: *he never forgot the slight* tells you as much about him as about the man who snubbed him.

⚠️ **slight ≠ sleight.** *Sleight* means cunning dexterity and survives in exactly one phrase — **sleight of hand**. Same sound, different word: the magician has sleight, the snubbed guest nurses a slight.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Slight** hides **light** — to slight someone is to make *light* of them.

Both senses fall out of that: light in weight (a slight fever) and lightly regarded (a slight to your host).

### In sentences
1. The ambassador read the omission of his title as a deliberate **slight**, and left the reception before dinner was served.
2. Don't take it personally — she wasn't **slighting** you, she genuinely didn't see you standing there.

### In the wild
All of *Pride and Prejudice* is set in motion by a slight: Darcy declines to dance with Elizabeth at the Meryton ball, and she spends the next three hundred pages not forgiving him for it.

# **somnolent** *(adj.)* — **SOM-nuh-luhnt**

### Means
Drowsy, half-asleep — or **making** you that way. It runs both directions: a **somnolent** student and the **somnolent** lecture that produced him.

Register is literary and atmospheric rather than clinical. *Sleepy* is a plain fact; *somnolent* is a mood laid over a whole scene — a hot afternoon, a dozing village, a courtroom after lunch. It also goes figurative for things that ought to be active but aren't: a **somnolent** market, a **somnolent** regulator. Milder and more pleasant than *torpid* or *lethargic*, which carry a whiff of failure; *somnolent* just carries heavy eyelids.

### Trick to lock it in
> **SOM-nuh-luhnt** — the word yawns: one stressed syllable, then two that mumble off into sleep.

Say it aloud and the trailing "-nuh-luhnt" does the drowsiness for you, which is exactly the slow, heavy-lidded mood the word describes.

### In sentences
1. By three o'clock the courtroom had turned **somnolent**, the clerk's pen slowing to a crawl as counsel read the schedules aloud.
2. That documentary was so **somnolent** I was out cold before the opening credits finished.

### Where it comes from
Latin **somnus** = *"sleep"* — the Roman god **Somnus** himself. The root is everywhere once you see it: **insomnia** (*in-* = not, so: no sleep), **somnambulist** (sleep + *ambulare*, "to walk" — a sleepwalker), **somniferous** (sleep-bearing).

So **somnolent** is simply *"full of sleep"* — and if you know *insomnia*, you already knew the root.

# **stoic** *(adj. / n.)* — **STOH-ik**

### Means
**Adj.:** enduring pain, hardship or loss **without complaint and without visible emotion**. **Noun:** a person who does that.

The key is that the feeling is *there*. A stoic person isn't numb or indifferent — they mind very much, and simply refuse to make a scene about it. That's why the word carries quiet admiration: it implies self-command, not coldness. *Impassive* describes a blank face; *apathetic* means you don't care; **stoic** means you care and hold the line anyway.

⚠️ **stoic ≠ stolid.**
- **stoic** = bears suffering without complaint — *admiring*
- **stolid** = dull, unresponsive, showing no emotion because there's little going on — *insulting*

Same "unmoved face", opposite compliment.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Stoic** = **"stow it"** — the feelings get stowed away, not thrown away.

Stowed cargo is still on board, which is exactly the difference between a stoic and someone who simply feels nothing.

### In sentences
1. Through three years of failed treatments she remained **stoic**, answering every enquiry about her health with the same steady politeness.
2. He took the rejection email like a **stoic** — shrugged, made a coffee, and went back to work.

### In the wild
Marcus Aurelius's **Meditations** — the emperor's private notes on enduring what you can't control — is the bestseller behind the whole modern "**Stoicism**" wave on podcasts and self-help shelves. Same word, capital S.

### Where it comes from
Greek **stoa** = *"porch."* Zeno of Citium taught in Athens under the **Stoa Poikilē**, the Painted Porch — so his followers were "the porch people," **Stoics**. A philosophy of enduring hardship calmly got named after the address where it was taught.

# **supersede** *(v.)* — **soo-per-SEED**

### Means
To **take the place of** something older, weaker, or out of date — and in doing so, to **cancel its authority**. The superseded thing isn't just less popular now; it no longer counts.

Tone is procedural and bureaucratic: rules, editions, models, treaties, software versions. Compare **supplant**, which carries scheming or force — you supplant a rival, you *oust* him. Nobody is elbowed aside in a supersede; the new thing simply arrives with better standing, and the old one quietly becomes waste paper.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Super + sede = *sit above*** — the new rule takes the old one's seat.

Once something sits in that seat, the previous occupant isn't merely demoted — it's void.

### In sentences
1. The 1958 constitution **superseded** every ordinance passed under the provisional government.
2. Don't print that form — it's been **superseded**, there's a new one on the intranet.

### In the wild
Office and engineering life runs on it: the red **SUPERSEDED** stamp across an obsolete drawing, and the standard opening line of any policy memo — *"This document supersedes all previous versions."*

### Where it comes from
Latin **super** (*above*) + **sedēre** (*to sit*) — literally *to sit on top of*. That *sedēre* root is a GRE workhorse: **sedentary** (sitting), **sediment** (settling), **preside**, **reside**, **session**, **subsidy**, **assiduous** (sitting doggedly at it), **dissident** (sitting apart).

🚫 **Note the spelling: -sede, not -cede.** *Precede, concede, recede* come from *cedere*, "to go, to yield" — different root. **Supersede** is the only English word ending in **-sede**; "supercede" is simply wrong.

# **tout** *(v. / n.)* — **TOWT**

*Rhymes with *out*, not with *taught*.*

### Means
**Verb:** to promote something loudly and persistently — to talk it up, usually because you stand to gain. **Noun (chiefly British):** a street hustler who solicits business; a *ticket tout* buys up seats and resells them at a markup.

Praise is admiration offered freely; touting is salesmanship with an angle. The word quietly implicates the promoter: whoever is touting has skin in the game, and the claim is probably inflated. That's why the passive form is a journalist's favourite weapon — **"touted as the next big thing"** almost always sets up the sentence where it wasn't.

⚠️ **tout ≠ taut.** Same letters, swapped: **taut** (**TAWT**) means *pulled tight* — a taut rope, a taut thriller. A tout is a hustler; taut is tension.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tout** rhymes with **shout** — and he's shouting about it because he's selling it.

The rhyme gets you the volume, and "selling it" gets you the self-interest that separates touting from honest praise.

### In sentences
1. The ministry **touted** the reforms as the most sweeping in a generation; independent auditors found the budget unchanged.
2. He's been **touting** that juicer to everyone in the office since he got it half-price.

### In the wild
When the Oasis reunion tickets sold out in 2024, the outrage was all about **touts** — resellers who grabbed seats and flipped them for thousands. British headlines used the word straight, no explanation needed.

### Where it comes from
Old English **tōtian** = *"to peer out, stick out, crane your neck."* A tout was originally a **lookout** — the man scanning the crowd for a mark. That's still exactly where you find him: outside the gate, spotting buyers before anyone else does.

# **wane** *(v. / n.)* — **WAYN**

*Identical in sound to the name Wayne.*

### Means
**Verb:** to decrease gradually in size, strength, intensity, or influence.
**Noun:** used almost only in the phrase *on the wane* — in decline.

The decline is **slow, natural, and measured from a height**. Things that wane were once full: a moon, an empire, a reputation, someone's patience. Nothing crashes or snaps — it thins out. That built-in "after the peak" is what separates *wane* from *dwindle* (shrinking toward nothing, no peak implied) and from *plummet* (fast and alarming). *Wane* is calm about it; the speaker is observing a curve, not sounding an alarm.

⚠️ **wane ≠ wan.** *Wan* (**WON**) is an adjective meaning *pale, sickly, feeble* — a wan smile, a wan face. One letter apart, unrelated words.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nothing can wane that wasn't full first.**

The word smuggles in a former peak, which is why *waning influence* implies the person once had plenty of it.

### In sentences
1. By the third century the empire's authority over its western provinces had visibly **waned**.
2. My enthusiasm for the group project **waned** somewhere around the fourth unanswered message.

### In the wild
The moon on your weather app: **waxing** (growing toward full) and **waning** (shrinking after it). That pairing is where nearly everyone first meets the word, and it fixes the "decline from fullness" sense for free.

### Where it comes from
Old English **wanian** = *"to lessen, dwindle."* Same family as **want** — which originally meant *lack*, not *desire*, and still does in the phrase **found wanting** (= found deficient). Worth banking: on the GRE, *wanting* often means *missing something*, not *wishing*.
