# Group 38

# **check** *(v. / n.)* — **CHEK**

*The banking sense is spelled cheque in British English; same word, same sound.*

### Means
**Verb:** to stop or slow the progress of something; to hold it within limits.
**Noun:** whatever does the restraining — a curb, a brake, a counterweight.

The GRE will not test the "examine it" or "tick the box" sense you use daily. It tests this one: *she checked her anger*, *a check on executive power*, *the advance was checked at the river*.

The force of the word is **partial containment**. *Halt* stops a thing dead, *suppress* crushes it, *abolish* removes it — but a checked thing is still there, still pushing, merely held. It's the word for a counterweight rather than a kill switch, which is why power is *checked* and impulses are *kept in check* rather than destroyed.

### Trick to lock it in
> A king **in check** isn't captured — just cornered. That's the whole word.

Check restrains and confines; it never eliminates.

### In sentences
1. The treaty was designed to **check** Prussian ambition, not to dismantle it.
2. He wanted to snap back, but he **checked** himself — her father was in the room.

### In the wild
**Checks and balances** — the phrase for the whole American constitutional design. No branch is abolished; each is merely held in place by the others. That's *check* in its exact sense.

### Where it comes from
Persian **shāh** = *"king"*, borrowed through Arabic and Old French *eschec* — the warning cry when a king was under threat.

One root, a surprising family: **chess**, **checkmate** (*shāh māt*, "the king is helpless"), **exchequer** (the treasury, named for the chequered counting-cloth), **chequered**, and **cheque** (a written order that *checks* fraud by leaving a counterfoil).

# **phony** *(adj. / n.)* — **FOH-nee**

*British spelling: phoney.*

### Means
**Adjective:** not genuine — fake, counterfeit, put on.
**Noun:** the person doing the faking.

*Counterfeit* and *fraudulent* describe the object coolly; **phony** is a judgement thrown at it. It's colloquial and hostile, and it carries a sneer the neutral words don't: the speaker feels **taken in**. It also stretches past objects to people and manner — a phony accent, a phony smile, a phony friend — where it means insincere posturing rather than literal forgery. Nobody calls something phony from a neutral distance; they call it phony because they've seen through it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Phony** starts with **"fo"** — so does **fool**. You only say it once you've felt fooled.

The sound hook carries the nuance: *phony* isn't a cool description of fakery, it's the accusation you make after the fake stops working on you.

### In sentences
1. The gallery's prize Vermeer proved **phony**, a competent forgery sold twice before anyone thought to test the pigment.
2. He gave me that **phony** smile again, the one he uses on customers.

### In the wild
Holden Caulfield in *The Catcher in the Rye* uses **phony** as his whole worldview — adults, schools, movies, everyone is a phony. The word's contempt is basically the book's voice.

### Where it comes from
🚫 **Nothing to do with *phone*.** That's Greek **phōnē**, *"sound"* — *telephone, phonetics, symphony, cacophony, euphony*. Identical opening, unrelated word.

# **querulous** *(adj.)* — **KWER-uh-luhs**

*Stress falls on the first syllable — not "kwuh-ROO-luhs".*

### Means
Habitually complaining — in a whining, peevish, faintly pathetic tone.

The force of the word is in the *sound* of the complaint, not its content. An indignant complaint has moral weight; a querulous one has none — it's the sighing, put-upon grumble of someone who finds everything slightly against them. Writers reach for it most often for the old, the ill, or the exhausted, and it always tilts against the complainer: it says the grievance is small and the tone is grating.

**querulous ≠ garrulous.** *Querulous* = complaining; *garrulous* (**GA-ruh-luhs**) = endlessly chatty. A garrulous man won't stop talking; a querulous one won't stop moaning.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Quer**ulous — always up for a **quar**rel.

*Quarrel* is genuinely the same Latin root, and the sound match hands you both the meaning and its petty, bickering tone.

### In sentences
1. In her last years she grew **querulous**, convinced that the nurses were hiding her spectacles from her.
2. Three hours into the delay the whole queue turned **querulous**, muttering about refunds nobody was going to get.

### Where it comes from
Latin **querī** = *"to complain."* The same root gives you **quarrel** (via *querela*, a grievance).

🚫 **Not *query*.** That's Latin *quaerere*, "to ask, to seek" — *question, inquiry, quest, acquire*. Looks like a sibling, isn't one.
