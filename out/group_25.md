# **adept** *(adj. / n.)* — **uh-DEPT**

*As a noun ("an adept"), the stress often shifts forward: AD-ept.*

### Means
**Adj.:** highly skilled, expert — usually *adept at* or *adept in* something. **Noun:** a person who has mastered a difficult art.

The skill is **earned, not innate**. *Apt* and *gifted* point to raw talent; *adept* points to practice that has hardened into fluency, and it carries a whiff of the specialist — someone who has been *initiated* into the craft. It's warmer and more admiring than *proficient* (which merely clears the bar) and less lofty than *masterful*; *adroit* is its close cousin but leans physical or social — quick hands, quick footwork — where *adept* is about command of a skill.

⚠️ **adept ≠ adapt.** *Adept* (uh-DEPT) = **skilled**. *Adapt* (uh-DAPT) = **adjust to new conditions**. One letter, one vowel sound, two unrelated words — and *adopt* is lurking a third of the way behind.

### Trick to lock it in
> **You're born *apt*; you become *adept*.**

The extra syllable is the extra work — *adept* is the skill you attained, not the talent you started with.

### In sentences
1. A decade in the archives had made her unusually **adept** at reading water-damaged handwriting.
2. He's weirdly **adept** at parallel parking — three seconds, one go, every time.

### In the wild
Every block of placeholder text you've ever seen — *\"Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur **adipiscing** elit\"* — carries the same Latin verb, *adipisci*, \"to attain.\"

### Where it comes from
Latin **adeptus**, "having attained" (from *ad-* "to" + *apisci* "reach, obtain"). It reached English through **alchemy**: an *adept* was one who had *attained* the great secret of transmuting metal into gold.

That's why the word still implies attainment rather than gift — and why the noun sense sounds faintly like a member of a guild.

# **adverse** *(adj.)* — **AD-vers**

*Stressed on the first syllable, like adversary — not "uh-VERS", which is averse.*

### Means
Acting **against** your interests; unfavourable, harmful, hostile in effect.

It isn't a synonym for *bad*. *Adverse* is bad **in opposition to something** — a headwind, a ruling, a drug, a market all pushing against a specific interest. And it describes **conditions, forces and outcomes, not feelings**: weather, effects, publicity, a court decision. The register is formal and institutional; you meet it in medical inserts, legal judgments and risk reports far more than in speech.

⚠️ **adverse ≠ averse.**
- **adverse** = *unfavourable*, describing a thing — **adverse** weather, **adverse** effects
- **averse** = *unwilling*, describing a person — she is **averse** to flying

Test: if you can put "to" after it and a person in front, you want **averse**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Adverse** never tells you how anyone *feels* — only what's being done *to* them: adverse weather, adverse effects, adverse ruling.

Every natural collocation of *adverse* is an external force, which is exactly why a person can never be "adverse to" anything.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal reached an **adverse** finding against the bank, and the share price fell before close.
2. We'd have finished the hike, but the weather turned **adverse** and we bailed at the ridge.

### In the wild
The leaflet inside every box of medicine lists **adverse effects** (or *adverse reactions*) — the drug turning against the patient it's meant to help.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*toward*) + **vertere** (*to turn*) = "turned against you."

*Vertere* is a workhorse root: **adversary** (one turned against you), **adversity**, **avert** (turn away), **divert**, **convert**, **revert**, **versatile**, and **anniversary** (the year turning round again).

# **appropriate** *(v. / adj.)* — **uh-PROH-pree-AYT (v.) / uh-PROH-pree-uht (adj.)**

*Same spelling, two words: the verb finishes hard on -AYT, the adjective swallows it into -uht.*

### Means
**Adjective:** fitting, suitable for the occasion — the everyday sense.
**Verb (the one the GRE tests):** to **take something for your own use**, typically without the owner's permission; or, in official language, to **set aside money** for a designated purpose.

The verb sits on a moral sliding scale. When a legislature *appropriates* $4bn for flood defence, it is bloodless accounting. When a novelist *appropriates* a colleague's plot, or a settler *appropriates* land, the word carries quiet theft — cooler and more respectable than *steal*, which is exactly what makes it damning. It implies the taker had the power to simply help themselves and did.

⚠️ **appropriate ≠ expropriate.**
- **appropriate** = *I* take it for myself
- **expropriate** = an authority takes it *away from* an owner (compulsory purchase, nationalisation)

The *ex-* is the giveaway: it takes something **out of** someone else's hands.

### Trick to lock it in
> The adjective mumbles its ending; the verb **ATE** it — and what it ate was *proper* to somebody else.

Hearing the hard **-AYT** tells you it's the verb, and the "ate" tells you something has been swallowed up that wasn't yours.

### In sentences
1. The committee **appropriated** funds for the restoration, then quietly **appropriated** the credit for it as well.
2. He **appropriated** my charger, my desk and my lunch, all before ten in the morning.

### In the wild
**Cultural appropriation** — the phrase that made this verb a headline word. It works precisely because *appropriate* means taking what was *proper* to another group and wearing it as your own.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **proprius** (*one's own*) — literally *to make one's own*.

That root is a workhorse: **proper**, **property**, **proprietor**, **propriety**, **proprietary**, **expropriate**. Note the pun buried in the pair: what is *proper* (fitting) and what is *property* (owned) come from the same word — which is why *appropriate* means both "suitable" and "taken".

# **archetype** *(n. (adj. archetypal))* — **AR-kuh-type**

*The "ch" is a hard k, never "arch-" as in archway. Adjective: ar-kuh-TYPE-uhl.*

### Means
The **original model or pattern** from which all later versions are copied — and, by extension, the **perfect, defining example** of a type ("she's the archetype of the self-made founder").

It carries weight and depth. An archetype isn't just an early version; it's the deep template a whole category keeps re-enacting, which is why Jung used it for the recurring figures in myth and dream — the Hero, the Mother, the Trickster. Calling something an archetype dignifies it: you're saying it reached the bottom of the pattern, not that it's generic.

⚠️ **archetype ≠ stereotype.** An **archetype** is the deep original pattern others grow from — respectful. A **stereotype** is a flattened cliché imposed from outside — dismissive. The Wise Old Mentor is an archetype; "all mentors are bearded" is a stereotype.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside is **arch**- as in **arch**-enemy and **arch**bishop: the *chief* one, the one above all the others.

An archetype is the chief type — the boss version every lesser copy answers to.

### In sentences
1. Campbell argued that the wandering exile who returns transformed is an **archetype** common to Greek epic, Norse saga, and Hollywood alike.
2. That diner with the cracked vinyl booths and bottomless coffee is the **archetypal** American greasy spoon.

### In the wild
Luke Skywalker is the textbook case: George Lucas built *Star Wars* on Joseph Campbell's hero **archetype** — orphan, call to adventure, mentor, return — which is why the plot feels familiar the first time you see it.

### Where it comes from
Greek **arkhē** = *"beginning, first, chief"* + **typos** = *"stamp, mark, model."* Literally the **first stamp** — the die that every later impression is struck from.

Both halves pay rent across the GRE:
- **arkhē** → *archaic, archaeology, monarch, anarchy, architect, archangel*
- **typos** → *type, typical, prototype, stereotype, typography*

# **articulate** *(adj. / v.)* — **ar-TIK-yuh-luht**

*The verb is ar-TIK-yuh-LAYT — same spelling, different ending: the adjective mumbles the last syllable, the verb sounds it out.*

### Means
**Adj.:** fluent and clear in speech — able to say exactly what you mean.
**Verb:** to put something into clear words, especially something that was previously vague; also, literally, **to join by a hinge**.

The idea underneath is *jointedness*, not eloquence. To **articulate** a fear is to break a shapeless dread into named parts; an **articulate** speaker isn't just smooth, they deliver thought in distinct, well-hinged pieces you can follow. That's why bones and lorries articulate too — they're jointed so they can bend.

One register warning: as praise for a person, it can land as a backhanded compliment when the speaker is implying they didn't expect fluency.

### Trick to lock it in
> An **articulated** lorry is one long thing split at a **joint** — an articulate speaker splits one long thought the same way.

Both senses are the same act: putting joints into something so it can move and be followed.

### In sentences
1. The witness proved unexpectedly **articulate**, laying out a decade of grievance in three clean paragraphs.
2. I couldn't **articulate** why the flat felt wrong — it just did.

### In the wild
The **articulated** lorry (and the bendy bus) — the bit in the middle that hinges is the articulation. Same word, visible joint.

### Where it comes from
Latin **articulus** = *"little joint"* (from **artus**, joint). Hence **article** — originally a distinct joint or clause of a text, which is why treaties have articles. Greek cousin **arthron** gives you **arthritis**, the disease of the joints.

# **auspicious** *(adj.)* — **aw-SPISH-uhs**

### Means
Showing **signs that something will go well** — favourable, promising, of good omen.

The word describes the *omen*, not the result. A day, a start, a debut, a set of conditions can be auspicious; the success itself cannot. That's what separates it from **fortunate** or **successful**, which report what already happened. It also sits high in register — faintly ceremonial, the language of weddings, launches, and inaugurations — and it works hardest in the negative: *"not an auspicious beginning"* is a dry, polite way of saying it went badly from the first minute.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Auspicious** always attaches to a *start*: an auspicious **debut**, **day**, **omen**, **occasion** — never an auspicious ending.

If you can't put the word in front of something that hasn't happened yet, it isn't the right word.

### In sentences
1. The treaty was signed on an **auspicious** morning of clear skies and full attendance — which fooled everyone about the decade to come.
2. Losing your laptop on day one isn't the most **auspicious** start to a new job.

### In the wild
Indian weddings run on this word: families consult the calendar for an **auspicious** date and *muhurat* before anything is booked — the whole idea of a moment being *pre-approved by the signs* is the definition, live.

### Where it comes from
Latin **auspex** = *"bird-watcher"* — from **avis** (bird) + **specere** (to look). Roman officials read the flight of birds before battles and elections; good birds, good omen.

Both halves pay off elsewhere: *avis* → **aviary, aviation**; *specere* → **inspect, spectacle, conspicuous, perspective**.

Same root gives **auspices** — *"under the auspices of the UN"* = under its watchful sponsorship.

# **bereft** *(adj.)* — **buh-REFT**

### Means
**Deprived of something** — stripped of a quality, comfort, or possession you once had (*bereft of hope, bereft of charm*). Used alone, it means **left desolate**, hollowed out by loss.

The word carries a history that *lacking* does not: to be bereft, you must have **had** the thing and had it **taken**. A room can lack windows; only a person bereft of friends has lost them. The register is elevated and literary — in speech it lands as either genuine grief or dry hyperbole (*"the menu was bereft of anything edible"*).

**bereft vs. bereaved.** **Bereaved** is narrow — you have lost a *person*, to death. **Bereft** is wide — you can be bereft of money, ideas, or dignity, as well as of a person.

### Trick to lock it in
> **bereft = "be left"** — left behind, without the thing you used to have.

The sound carries the whole nuance: not merely missing something, but *left* holding nothing after it was taken away.

### In sentences
1. Stripped of her titles and her income, she returned to the village **bereft** of everything but her name.
2. He came out of that meeting completely **bereft** — three years of work, binned in ten minutes.

### In the wild
Monty Python's Dead Parrot sketch: *"'E's bereft of life, 'e rests in peace!"* — one of the shopkeeper's euphemisms for **dead**, which is exactly the kind of loss the word implies.

### Where it comes from
From **bereave** = *be-* (thoroughly) + **reave**, an old verb meaning *to rob, to plunder* (a Viking **reaver** was a raider). **Bereft** is simply its old past participle — literally **"robbed of."**

That root hands you the nuance for free: nothing is bereft that wasn't first taken.

# **captious** *(adj.)* — **KAP-shuhs**

### Means
Given to finding fault over **trivialities** — raising petty objections, seizing on small errors, quibbling for the sake of it.

A *critical* reader wants the work to be better. A **captious** one wants you to be wrong. The word passes judgement on the objector, not the object: it says the complaints are small, the motive is point-scoring, and the pleasure is in the catching. It also describes a **disposition**, not a single remark — you can be captious all afternoon. Formal, faintly withering; it's the word for the committee member who spends forty minutes on a footnote.

**captious ≠ capricious.** Both open with *cap-*, both are exam regulars. **Captious** = nitpicking, out to catch you. **Capricious** = whimsical, changing on a impulse. A captious boss objects to everything; a capricious boss objects to a different thing each day.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Capt**ious — he's out to **capt**ure you in a slip.

Not a review, a gotcha: the captious critic hunts the trivial error so he can hold it up.

### In sentences
1. The referee's report was **captious** throughout, disputing the spelling of place names while ignoring the argument entirely.
2. Don't be so **captious** — I know I said "less" instead of "fewer," and no, it doesn't change the point.

### Where it comes from
Latin **captiōsus** = *"designed to entrap"* — a **captious question** was originally a trick one, a snare dressed as an enquiry. From **capere**, *"to take, to seize."*

That root is a workhorse: **capture, captive, caption, captivate, susceptible, recuperate**. Keep the seizing sense and the word stays honest — the captious man is grabbing at your mistakes.

# **conclusive** *(adj.)* — **kuhn-KLOO-siv**

### Means
Settling a question **beyond any further argument** — proof or evidence so decisive that the matter is closed.

It's an on/off word, not a dial. *Compelling*, *persuasive*, *strong* all describe evidence that moves you while leaving room to doubt; **conclusive** leaves no room at all. That's why it lives mostly in the negative — *no conclusive evidence*, *the tests were inconclusive* — and why claiming something is conclusive is a big claim. Courtroom, lab report, and inquiry register.

⚠️ **conclusive ≠ concluding.**
- **concluding** = *last in sequence* — the concluding chapter, the concluding remarks
- **conclusive** = *settles it* — conclusive proof

A concluding argument can be entirely unconvincing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Con-CLUS-ive** — the *clus* is *close*: it **closes the case**.

Anything less than conclusive leaves the case still open.

### In sentences
1. The committee found the accounts irregular, but nothing **conclusive** enough to justify a referral to the prosecutor.
2. Two grainy photos aren't **conclusive** — get me something that actually proves he was there.

### In the wild
The stock phrase every news bulletin and inquiry report reaches for: *"there is no **conclusive** evidence that..."* — the polite way of saying we suspect it but can't prove it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **claudere** = *"to shut, to close"*, via *concludere* ("to shut up, close off").

One root, a whole GRE family: **conclude**, **preclude** (shut out in advance), **exclude**, **seclude**, **recluse** (one shut away), **closure**. Wherever you see *-clude / -clus-*, think *shut*.

# **conspire** *(v.)* — **kuhn-SPYRE**

### Means
To **plot secretly with others** toward an end that is usually illegal, treacherous, or harmful.

The word is built for **two or more**. You can *plot* alone and you can *scheme* alone, but conspiring takes accomplices — which is why the law treats conspiracy as its own crime, separate from the act itself. It also implies **concealment**: colleagues who openly agree to undercut a rival are colluding; the same people meeting in a car park at midnight are conspiring.

There's a second, softer use where the subject isn't people at all: *events conspired to ruin the trip*. Here it's half-joking fatalism — the world behaving as if it had organised itself against you.

### Trick to lock it in
> There is no such thing as a solo conspiracy — **con-** means you need someone to whisper with.

The *con-* ("together") is the whole nuance: secrecy plus a partner, not one person's private scheming.

### In sentences
1. Three junior officers were accused of having **conspired** to depose the general while he was abroad.
2. Roadworks, a dead phone and a train strike all **conspired** to make me forty minutes late.

### In the wild
Paulo Coelho's most-quoted line from *The Alchemist* — "when you want something, all the universe **conspires** in helping you to achieve it" — is the rare benign use, and it's plastered over half the internet's motivational posters.

### Where it comes from
Latin **con-** (*together*) + **spīrāre** (*to breathe*) — literally *to breathe together*, close enough to share air while whispering.

That root pays off across the exam: **inspire** (breathe in), **expire** (breathe out — then, stop breathing), **aspire** (breathe toward), **perspire**, **respiration**, and **spirit** itself.

# **delineate** *(v.)* — **dih-LIN-ee-ayt**

### Means
To **draw the outline of** something, or — far more often on the GRE — to **describe it precisely, edge to edge**, so its limits are unmistakable.

The word is about **boundaries, not detail**. You *describe* a landscape; you *delineate* a border. To delineate two theories is to show exactly where one stops and the other begins; to delineate a role is to say what falls inside it and what does not. It's a cool, technical, faintly bureaucratic verb — the language of contracts, treaties, and organisational charts, never of gossip. Its natural companion adverb is *clearly*, and the reason it collocates so hard is that the whole point of delineating is removing ambiguity.

### Trick to lock it in
> de-**LINE**-ate — you draw a **line** around it.

The word wears its meaning in the middle: not "tell me about it," but "put a line where it ends."

### In sentences
1. The treaty **delineated** the frontier river by river, leaving no stretch of the border open to interpretation.
2. Before we start, can someone **delineate** who's actually responsible for what? I've had three people tell me it's not their job.

### In the wild
Read almost any job description or team charter and you'll hit "**clearly delineated** roles and responsibilities" — corporate boilerplate for *we have drawn lines so you can't blame each other*.

# **disentangle** *(v.)* — **dis-en-TANG-guhl**

### Means
To free something from knots, snarls, or twisted-together parts — literally (cables, hair, fishing line) or figuratively (a person from a bad marriage, the facts from the rumours).

The word carries **patience**. *Extricate* is a rescue — you get out of the trap, fast, and how you did it doesn't matter. *Disentangle* is the slow, careful undoing of something that got knotted over time, strand by strand, with nothing torn. It also implies the two things were genuinely intertwined, not just stuck together: you disentangle threads, loyalties, finances, arguments — things that grew into each other.

### Trick to lock it in
> Alexander **cut** the Gordian knot. He did not **disentangle** it.

The word rules out the shortcut — disentangling is the unglamorous work of undoing every loop by hand.

### In sentences
1. The biographer's task is to **disentangle** the man from the myth that his admirers spent forty years spinning around him.
2. It took her two lawyers and a year to **disentangle** her finances from his.

### In the wild
Standard newsroom vocabulary for divorce, corporate demergers, and Brexit — "**disentangling** the UK from four decades of EU law" was the stock phrase, and it was chosen precisely because it meant slow and clause-by-clause, not one clean cut.

# **exhort** *(v.)* — **ig-ZORT**

*The h is silent — ig-ZORT, never eks-HORT. Noun: exhortation, eg-zor-TAY-shun.*

### Means
To **urge someone earnestly** to do something — by appeal, encouragement, and moral heat rather than by command.

The word carries fervour and zero authority. A general **orders**; a coach, a preacher, a campaigner **exhorts** — all passion, no power to compel. That's why exhortation so often shades into futility: newspapers *exhort* the government to act, and the government carries on regardless. It's urging that can only hope to be obeyed.

⚠️ **exhort ≠ extort.**
- **exhort** = *urge* you to do it (words only)
- **extort** = *force* it out of you by threat

The preacher exhorts the congregation; the gangster extorts the shopkeeper.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ex-HORT = ex-HOARSE** — the half-time speech that leaves the coach shouting himself raw.

All that hoarse effort and still nobody has to listen — which is exactly the flavour of exhorting.

### In sentences
1. The bishop's letter **exhorted** the faithful to charity in a season of plenty, though he could compel nothing.
2. Mum spent the whole drive **exhorting** us to be polite to Gran, and we ignored every word of it.

### In the wild
Henry V's **"Once more unto the breach, dear friends"** is the textbook exhortation — a leader with a broken army and nothing but words, roaring men back at a wall.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** (thoroughly) + **hortārī** (*to urge, encourage*) — literally *to urge hard*. Same root gives **hortatory** and **hortative**, both meaning "urging" and both fair GRE game.

🚫 **Nothing to do with horticulture.** That's *hortus*, "garden."

# **frailty** *(n.)* — **FRAYL-tee**

### Means
**1.** Physical weakness — the delicacy of the very old, the ill, the underfed.
**2.** A moral weak spot — a small, habitual failing in an otherwise decent person.

The word asks for sympathy rather than blame. *Weakness* can be a verdict on you; **frailty** treats the flaw as built in, part of the material you're made of, not a choice you made. Hence "human frailty" — the plural **frailties** almost always means the little vices we forgive: vanity, gossip, a weakness for gambling. Calling someone frail is tender; calling them weak is a judgement.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Frail** is **fail** with an *r* — frailty is the weakness that makes you *fail*, not the wickedness that makes you *guilty*.

The word points at breakability, so the tone is pity, not accusation.

### In sentences
1. Her mind stayed razor-sharp long after the **frailty** of her body confined her to the chair.
2. Look, he's a good boss — buying a boat he can't afford is just one of his **frailties**.

### In the wild
Hamlet's bitter line about his mother's hasty remarriage: **"Frailty, thy name is woman!"** — one of the most quoted half-lines in Shakespeare, and it's the *moral* sense, not the physical one.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fragilis** = *"breakable,"* from **frangere** = *"to break."* The same Latin word entered English twice: **fragile** straight from the Latin, and **frail** worn down through Old French *frele*.

That root breaks open a whole shelf: **fragment**, **fraction**, **refract** (light broken at an angle), **infringe** (break in on a right), **frangible**.

# **grievance** *(n.)* — **GREE-vuhns**

### Means
A **real or imagined wrong that gives you grounds to complain** — and, by extension, the formal complaint itself.

Not the same as an annoyance or a gripe. A grievance carries a claim of **injustice**: someone did something to me, and I am owed redress. That's why it lives in institutional language — *grievance procedure*, *file a grievance*, *union grievance* — where a complaint gets a case number.

It also has a slow-burning political sense: grievances are **nursed, harboured, aired**. A gripe evaporates; a grievance is kept.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A grievance is grief with paperwork.**

The word literally hides *grieve* — but the *-ance* turns private hurt into a filed, formal claim against somebody.

### In sentences
1. The petition set out the colonists' **grievances** against the Crown, item by numbered item.
2. He's still got a **grievance** about being passed over in 2019 — brings it up every single time.

### In the wild
*Seinfeld*'s Festivus has a fixed order of ceremony: the Feats of Strength, and before them **the Airing of Grievances** — where Frank Costanza tells everyone at the table exactly how they've disappointed him that year.

### Where it comes from
Latin **gravis** = *"heavy."* A grievance is a wrong that **weighs on you**.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **grave** (serious), **grievous** (seriously bad), **grief**, **aggravate** (literally *make heavier*), **gravity**, **gravitas**.

# **harangue** *(n. / v.)* — **huh-RANG**

*The -gue is silent: two syllables, not "huh-RANG-yoo."*

### Means
**Noun:** a long, loud, hectoring speech — scolding or ranting dressed up as address.
**Verb:** to subject someone to one.

Three things have to be true before it's a harangue: it goes on **too long**, it comes **at** people rather than to them, and it's **moralising or angry**. A *lecture* can be boring but well-meant; a *tirade* is a hot burst of rage; a harangue is the sustained version — the speaker has the floor and the audience can't escape. It's always the listener's word, never the speaker's: no one has ever described their own remarks as a harangue.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ha-RANG** — it still **rang** in your ears an hour after he stopped.

The buried *rang* catches the two things that define the word: loud, and far longer than anyone wanted.

### In sentences
1. The general's farewell address turned into a forty-minute **harangue** on the moral decay of the younger officers.
2. Ask him one question about crypto and he'll **harangue** you until your food goes cold.

### Where it comes from
Through French from Old Italian **aringa**, *"a public speech"* — from **aringo**, the assembly ground, and behind that a Germanic word for **ring**: the ring of people standing round the speaker. Baked into the word is the crowd that has to stand there and take it.

# **ploy** *(n.)* — **PLOY**

### Means
A **cunning little manoeuvre** designed to get an advantage — a tactic with a hidden motive behind it.

Scale is what marks it out. A **plot** is grand and sinister, a **scheme** is elaborate, a **stratagem** is military; a *ploy* is small, tactical and slightly cheap — one move, not a campaign. It's also the word people reach for once they've *seen through* the thing: "that's just a ploy to get you to stay on the call." Naming something a ploy is halfway to dismissing it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ploy** rhymes with **toy** — a toy-sized trick, and it *toys* with you.

Both halves of the rhyme carry the nuance: a ploy is small-time rather than grand, and its purpose is to play someone.

### In sentences
1. The delegation's sudden concern for procedure was a transparent **ploy** to stall the vote until morning.
2. He forgot his wallet again — I'm starting to think it's a **ploy**.

### In the wild
Its natural habitat is the phrase **"just a marketing ploy"** — the limited-time-only countdown, the "only 2 left in stock!" banner. You meet the word every time you don't fall for one.

### Where it comes from
**Ploy is *employ* with the front bitten off** — an 18th-century Scots clipping meaning "an employment, a bit of business." A ploy is simply what you *employ* to get your way.

Underneath sits Latin **implicāre**, "to fold in, entangle" — which also gives you **imply, implicate, complicate, pliable**.

# **poise** *(n. / v.)* — **POYZ**

### Means
**Noun:** composed, dignified self-assurance — especially under pressure. Also **physical balance**: a body held steady, nothing wobbling.
**Verb:** to hold balanced or suspended — *she poised the pen above the form*; *the cat was poised to spring*.

Both senses are one idea: **weight held in perfect balance.** *Calm* describes an inner state nobody can see; **poise is composure that shows** — steady voice, unhurried hands, no fidgeting. It also carries a whiff of grace and breeding, so it's what you praise in a diplomat, a dancer, or a fifteen-year-old handling a hostile question well. And *poised to* adds a second flavour entirely: not relaxed at all, but coiled and ready, weight already shifted toward the leap.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Poise** rhymes with **noise** — and poise is what makes none.

The rattled person clatters, stammers, and fusses; the poised one produces silence and stillness under exactly the same pressure.

### In sentences
1. Cross-examined for six hours, the witness answered every question with the same unhurried **poise**.
2. I'd have burst into tears — she just said "thanks for the feedback" and sat back down. Total **poise**.

### In the wild
Beauty pageants literally **score** it — Miss Universe judges contestants on *poise* alongside the interview, which tells you the word means bearing you can watch, not a feeling you have.

### Where it comes from
Old French **pois** = *"weight,"* from Latin **pendere**, *"to weigh, to hang."* Poise is weight hanging in balance — which is why a cat can be *poised* to pounce and a person *poised* under fire: both are holding a load without tipping.

That root is everywhere on the GRE: **pendulum**, **suspend**, **pending**, **pensive** (weighing thoughts), **ponder**, **compensate** (weigh out payment), and **equipoise** (equal weight — perfect balance).

# **pomposity** *(n.)* — **pom-POS-uh-tee**

### Means
Inflated self-importance — especially the **grand, ceremonious manner** of someone who treats their own opinions like state announcements.

Arrogance is about *claiming superiority*; pomposity is about the **style**: the orotund voice, the long Latinate words, the pause before the obvious. It attaches to the delivery more than the belief. And it's a comic charge, not a fearsome one — the pompous man is ridiculous, someone to be punctured at a dinner party, not feared. Nobody has ever described their own manner as pomposity.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pomp**osity = **pomp** with no occasion — a one-man parade.

Ceremony is fine at a coronation; pomposity is bringing that same fanfare to a status meeting.

### In sentences
1. The committee's report collapsed under its own **pomposity**, taking four hundred words to recommend a slightly larger bin.
2. He can't just say "I disagree" — there's always this **pomposity** where he clears his throat and says "if I may offer a perspective."

### In the wild
Elgar's **"Pomp and Circumstance"** — the march every graduation processes to. Same *pomp*: grand ceremonial display. Pomposity is that music playing in someone's head while they answer an email.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pompa** = *"solemn procession,"* from Greek **pompē** (*a sending, a parade*). The word started as pure spectacle — the parade itself — and only later curdled into the sneer at people who march through ordinary life as if a crowd were watching.

Family: **pomp**, **pompous**, **pageant**.

# **proxy** *(n. (also adj.))* — **PROK-see**

### Means
**1.** A person **authorised to act for another** — to vote, sign, or attend in their place; also the authority itself (*"she holds my proxy"*).
**2.** A **stand-in measure**: something used to represent a thing you can't observe directly. *"Tree rings are a proxy for past rainfall."*

Nothing shady about it — a proxy is an *appointed* substitute, openly delegated, whereas an **impostor** or **front** substitutes without permission. What it does carry is a hint of **second-hand-ness**: a proxy is not the real thing, only the thing standing where the real thing should be. Hence **proxy war** (the superpowers fight through other people's armies) and **by proxy** (it happened, but you weren't there for it).

### Trick to lock it in
> **A proxy acts *pro* you — with your say-so. An impostor acts *as* you — without it.**

The *pro-* ("on behalf of") is the whole word: authorised substitution, not impersonation.

### In sentences
1. Unable to attend the shareholders' meeting, the trustee voted by **proxy**.
2. Test scores are a lousy **proxy** for how much a kid actually understands.

### In the wild
A **proxy server** does exactly what the word says: it fetches the page *on your behalf*, so the site sees the middleman's address instead of yours.

### Where it comes from
A squashed-down **procuracy** — from Latin **procurare** = *"to manage on behalf of"* (**pro-** "for" + **cura** "care"). Same *cura* runs through **procure, curator, curate, cure, secure, accurate**, and **sinecure** (a job *without* care — i.e. no actual work).

🚫 Nothing to do with **proximity**. That's *proximus*, "nearest" — the resemblance is pure accident.

# **relent** *(v.)* — **ri-LENT**

### Means
To **soften a hard position and finally give way** — to stop refusing, stop punishing, stop pressing. Also used of bad things easing off: rain, heat, pain, pressure **relent**.

You cannot relent unless you were first immovable. The word carries its own backstory: a refusal held long enough that giving in becomes news. *Yield* can happen instantly and under any pressure; *relent* implies you were the harsh one, and the softening is a small mercy. That's why the negative — **relentless** — is the commoner word: it names the person who never has that moment.

**relent ≠ repent.** One letter apart, opposite machinery: **relent** = drop your *opposition* (you stop refusing someone else); **repent** = regret your *own* sin. A tyrant relents; a sinner repents.

### Trick to lock it in
> **re-LENT** — you finally *lend* what you'd spent a month refusing to give.

The hidden *lent* is the handover, and the *re-* is the long refusal that came before it — no prior hardness, no relenting.

### In sentences
1. After three days of siege the governor **relented**, and the gates were opened at dawn.
2. Mum said no to the dog for two years, then **relented** the week I moved out.

### Where it comes from
Latin **lentus** = *"pliant, slow, sticky"* — the thing that bends rather than snaps. To relent is to bend at last.

Same root gives the musical marking **lento** (*slow*), and the word you'll meet far more often, **relentless** — literally *unbending*.

🚫 Not from **lenient** (Latin *lēnis*, "soft, mild"). Similar sound, similar mood, unrelated root.

# **rhetoric** *(n.)* — **RET-uh-rik**

*Silent h, and the stress moves in the adjective: rhetorical is ruh-TOR-i-kuhl.*

### Means
**1.** The art of persuasive speaking and writing — its techniques, structures, and figures. **2.** Language crafted to persuade or impress but thin on substance or sincerity.

The word runs hot or cold depending on who's holding it. In a classroom it's a neutral discipline: Aristotle wrote a treatise on it, and *rhetorical devices* are just tools. In politics and journalism it's nearly always a slur — "**empty rhetoric**", "inflammatory rhetoric" — talk offered *instead of* action or truth. What stays constant across both senses: rhetoric is about **how** something is said, never whether it's true. That's why the GRE likes it — passages use the neutral sense while everyday instinct expects the insult.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Empty** rhetoric. **Heated** rhetoric. **Campaign** rhetoric. — judge it by the company it keeps.

Alone the word names a neutral craft, but the adjectives that habitually attach to it all mean "all talk, no substance," which is exactly the split you need to hold.

### In sentences
1. Cicero's mastery of **rhetoric** could turn a losing case into an acquittal before the jury had stopped listening.
2. Cut the **rhetoric** and just tell me what the rent's going to be next year.

### In the wild
You already use the adjective without thinking: a **rhetorical question** is asked purely for effect, with no answer wanted — proof that the root is about *effect on the listener*, not information.

### Where it comes from
Greek **rhētōr** = *"public speaker, one who addresses the assembly."* The word is built on the speaker, not the subject — which is exactly why it always describes delivery rather than truth.

# **rigor** *(n.)* — **RIG-er**

*British spelling: rigour. Hard g, as in "big" — never "RIJ-er."*

### Means
**1.** Strict, unbending exactness — especially of method, proof, or reasoning. **2.** Severity; harsh enforcement. **3.** (usually plural, *rigors*) punishing conditions: *the rigors of the Arctic winter*.

One word, two opposite emotional charges, decided entirely by what is being rigorous. Applied to a **method**, it's the highest compliment in academic English — *methodological rigor* means nothing was hand-waved. Applied to a **person or a regime**, it's cold and unforgiving. Applied to **circumstances**, it's plain suffering. Note the register: this is a word for journals, courts, and training manuals, not for casual complaint.

**rigor ≠ vigor.** One letter apart, opposite feel: **rigor** is stiffness and strictness, **vigor** is energy and vitality. A rigorous argument is airtight; a vigorous argument is energetic — and possibly sloppy.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Rigor** is **rigid** — rigid *method* earns praise, rigid *conditions* inflict pain.

Same stiffness either way, and which sense you're in depends only on whether the stiffness is in an argument or in the weather.

### In sentences
1. The referees rejected the paper not for its conclusions but for its lack of statistical **rigor**.
2. Two weeks into boot camp the **rigor** of it had him asleep by nine every night.

### In the wild
**Rigor mortis** — the stiffening of a body after death, the phrase every crime drama uses to time a murder. Same word, literal sense: *stiffness*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **rigor** = *"stiffness, numbness,"* from **rigēre**, *"to be stiff."* Everything follows from stiffness: **rigid** (won't bend), **rigid**ity, **rigorous** (won't bend the standards), **rigor mortis** (won't bend at all).

# **sparse** *(adj.)* — **SPAHRS**

### Means
Thinly scattered over an area; few and far between — **sparse hair, a sparse crowd, sparse vegetation, sparse notes**.

The key idea is **distribution, not absence**. Sparse things *are* there; they're just spread thin, with gaps between them. That's why it pairs so naturally with space and surfaces (*sparsely populated*, *sparse coverage*) and why it's the neutral, almost measuring word — a scientist calls data sparse without complaining about it, whereas *meagre* or *paltry* would be a judgement.

⚠️ **sparse ≠ scarce.**
- **sparse** = thinly *spread out* (a sparse audience — plenty of empty seats between people)
- **scarce** = in short *supply* (tickets were scarce — hard to get any at all)

Water can be scarce in a region; the villages there are sparse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **SPARSE = SPARE + S** — what's there is spare, spaced out, with room to spare between.

Hearing *spare* inside it keeps you on the right idea: not gone, just stretched thin across the gaps.

### In sentences
1. The plateau supports only **sparse** scrub, its shrubs standing metres apart on bare rock.
2. His notes for the meeting were pretty **sparse** — three bullet points and a question mark.

### In the wild
In tech it's everywhere: a **sparse matrix** is one that's mostly zeros, with the few real values scattered thinly through it — same word, same picture, just in numbers.

### Where it comes from
Latin **spargere** = *"to scatter, sprinkle."* Sparse things have been sprinkled, not poured.

Same root: **disperse** (scatter apart), **intersperse** (scatter among), and **aspersion** — to *cast aspersions* is literally to sprinkle dirt on someone's name.

# **steadfast** *(adj.)* — **STED-fast**

*The first syllable rhymes with *bed*, not *bead*.*

### Means
Firm and unwavering — in loyalty, belief, or resolve — especially when there is pressure to give way.

This is a word of praise, and it praises *constancy under strain*. **Resolute** describes a decision; **persistent** describes effort; **stubborn** describes an obstruction. **Steadfast** is about staying — the friend who doesn't drift when your reputation goes bad, the refusal to abandon a position when abandoning it would be easier. It leans slightly noble and old-fashioned, so it attaches naturally to people, allies, faith and support rather than to trivial things: a steadfast ally, not a steadfast preference for oat milk.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Stead + fast** — and *fast* here means **stuck fast**, not speedy. Think "hold fast," "stuck fast," colour**fast**.

The old sense of *fast* is *fixed in place*, so a steadfast person is fixed in their place — they don't move when moved against.

### In sentences
1. Through two decades of exile and ridicule, she remained **steadfast** in the belief that the verdict would eventually be overturned.
2. He's not clever, he's not quick, but he's **steadfast** — he'll be there at 6 a.m. every single time you ask.

### In the wild
The Psalms' refrain **"his steadfast love endures forever"** — the standard English rendering, and the reason *steadfast love* still sounds like the word's natural home: loyalty that outlasts everything.

### Where it comes from
Old English **stede** (*place*) + **fæst** (*firm, fixed*) = "fixed in place."

**stede** survives all over English once you spot it: **instead** (in the place of), **home**stead, bed**stead**, "in his **stead**", and **steady**.

# **suspect** *(adj. (also n., v.))* — **SUS-pekt**

*Adjective and noun are SUS-pekt; the verb shifts to suh-SPEKT. The GRE tests the adjective.*

### Means
**Adj.:** open to doubt — dodgy, questionable, not to be relied on. **Not** "feeling suspicion," but *attracting* it.

Direction is the whole point: the adjective is always passive. A **suspect** claim is one *we* doubt; it says nothing about what the claim thinks. Compare *suspicious*, which swings both ways — a suspicious neighbour may be watching you, or being watched.

Tone is dry understatement. Calling data "suspect" is a quiet, professional way of saying it may be garbage: cooler than *fraudulent*, more damning than *unproven*. It's the word an auditor or a referee uses when they don't yet want to say *fake*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **SUS**-pect starts with **sus** — and "sus" means exactly that: dodgy, not to be trusted.

The slang is literally clipped from this word, so the stress that lands on **SUS** is also the meaning of the adjective.

### In sentences
1. The committee dismissed the survey, noting that its sampling method was **suspect** and its margins of error implausibly narrow.
2. Three sources, all quoting each other? That timeline looks pretty **suspect** to me.

### In the wild
Sports commentary lives on this adjective — "their defence is **suspect**", "the knee is **suspect**". It's the pundit's polite way of saying *this will fall apart under pressure*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** ("under") + **specere** ("to look") — to look at someone *from underneath*, sideways, askance. That sidelong glance is still in the word.

**specere** is a GRE workhorse: **inspect**, **spectacle**, **conspicuous**, **circumspect** (looking around you = cautious), **perspicacious**, **prospect**, **retrospect**.

# **tedious** *(adj.)* — **TEE-dee-uhs**

*Also heard as TEE-juhs in fast speech.*

### Means
Tiresome because it is **too long, too slow, and too samey** — dull in a way that wears down your patience.

The complaint is about **duration, not difficulty**. *Hard* work can be gripping; *tedious* work is easy and endless — form-filling, a meeting that will not end, a speaker circling the same point. *Boring* describes a thing that fails to interest you; **tedious** describes a thing that keeps going *after* it stopped interesting you. It's a mild, civilised word — you can call a report tedious in writing without sounding rude, which is exactly why people reach for it.

**tedious vs. odious** — they rhyme perfectly and that's the trap. **Tedious** = boring you to death. **Odious** = deserving hatred. A tedious colleague wastes your afternoon; an odious one deserves your contempt.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dull** takes one syllable. **Te‑di‑ous** takes three — the word drags on longer than it needs to, exactly like the thing it describes.

The extra syllables are the meaning: tedium is what happens when something outstays its welcome.

### In sentences
1. The transcript ran to four hundred pages of **tedious** procedural wrangling before a single witness was called.
2. Sorting the receipts took all evening — mind-numbingly **tedious**, but somebody had to do it.

### In the wild
It's the standard word in every software pitch: *"automate the tedious parts of your workflow."* Nobody says *difficult* there — the promise is to delete the slow, repetitive bits, not the hard ones.

# **vitality** *(n.)* — **veye-TAL-uh-tee**

### Means
The state of being strongly, visibly alive — energy, animation, glow — and, more abstractly, **the capacity to survive and keep going**.

Two settings, one idea. Of a person, it's health you can see: colour in the face, spring in the step. Of a thing — a language, a party, a neighbourhood, a business — it's the sign that it hasn't ossified and won't die out. **Energy** is a quantity you spend today; **vitality** is the underlying life that keeps producing it. That's why you can be exhausted and still have vitality, and why an economy can be "busy" without it.

Always approving, and slightly formal — an obituary or a review says a writer "retained her vitality into her eighties"; nobody says it about a caffeinated Tuesday.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Vitamins** give you **vitality** — same first four letters, same job: keeping the life in you.

Vitamins aren't a stimulant that spikes you, they're what sustains life over time — exactly the difference between mere energy and vitality.

### In sentences
1. Despite three decades in exile, the movement retained a **vitality** that its state-funded rivals had long since lost.
2. He's seventy-four and still swims every morning — the man's got more **vitality** than my flatmate.

### In the wild
In RPGs — *Dark Souls*, *Elden Ring*, countless others — **Vitality** is the stat that governs how much health you have. Level it up, and you take more punishment before dying. That's the word, literally: the size of your reserve of life.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vita** = *"life."* That one root pays out across the exam: **vital** (life-sustaining, hence essential), **vitamin**, **revitalise**, **viable** (able to live), and **curriculum vitae** — "the course of a life."

🚫 **Not *vitiate*** (VISH-ee-ayt, "to spoil, corrupt"). That's Latin *vitium*, "fault" — same first three letters, opposite family. *Vitality* fills a thing with life; *vitiate* ruins it.

# **whimsical** *(adj.)* — **WIM-zi-kuhl**

### Means
Playfully odd and fanciful; acting on passing fancies rather than reason.

Two settings, two flavours. Applied to **art, design, or manner**, it's warm praise: quaint, imaginative, gently silly — whimsical hats, whimsical little illustrations. Applied to **decisions, rules, or power**, it turns critical: choices made on a passing impulse, with nothing solid behind them. *Capricious* is that same unpredictability with the charm stripped out and menace added; *whimsical* stays light, unless the person indulging their whims controls your fate.

### Trick to lock it in
> The word wears its own root on its sleeve: **whim**-sical — ruled by a **whim**.

A whim is small, harmless and short-lived, which is why the word stays charming — right up until the whim belongs to your boss.

### In sentences
1. The architect gave the sober granite façade one **whimsical** touch: a stone owl peering from the cornice.
2. He's a great cook but a **whimsical** one — you never know if dinner's at seven or eleven.

### In the wild
"Whimsical" is the adjective every critic reaches for with a **Wes Anderson** film — the symmetrical pastel dollhouse worlds of *The Grand Budapest Hotel*. Same for Tim Burton and Willy Wonka: charming, fanciful, running on its own private logic.

# **yield** *(v. / n.)* — **YEELD**

### Means
**Verb, sense 1:** to *produce* or give forth as a return — a crop, a profit, a result. **Sense 2:** to *give way* — to surrender ground, position, or the right of way under pressure. **Noun:** the amount produced (a harvest's yield, a bond's yield).

Yielding is softer than surrendering. *Capitulate* and *submit* imply defeat; *yield* implies **give** — the thing bends rather than breaks, and often does so gracefully, reasonably, or tactically ("I yield to my colleague"). It can even be a courtesy rather than a loss. Note the two adjectives the GRE loves: **yielding** = pliant, soft, easily pressed; **unyielding** = rigid, inflexible, refusing an inch.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside **yield** is **field**: a field gives up its crop, a driver gives up the road.

Every sense is the same act of *giving over* — producing, conceding, bending — which is exactly why **unyielding** means "won't give an inch."

### In sentences
1. The negotiators, unyielding for three days, finally **yielded** on the question of tariffs.
2. Push the door hard — the latch sticks, but it'll **yield** eventually.

### In the wild
The red-and-white triangular **YIELD** sign at a junction: not "stop," not "go" — *give way to whoever's already there.* That's the whole nuance of the word in one road sign.

### Where it comes from
Old English **gieldan** = *"to pay, repay"* (cognate with German **Geld**, "money"). Payment is the thread through all three senses: a field pays you a crop, a bond pays you a **yield**, and a driver pays over the right of way.

Same root gives you **guild** — originally a group bound by the dues its members paid in.
