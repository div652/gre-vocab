# **adulterate** *(v.)* — **uh-DUL-tuh-rayt**

### Means
To make something **impure or inferior by mixing in a foreign, cheaper substance** — watering the milk, cutting the olive oil with sunflower oil, padding the drugs with baking soda.

*Dilute* is neutral and often deliberate housekeeping; **adulterate is an accusation.** It implies secrecy, profit, and degradation — someone sold you the pure thing and handed you the cut thing. It stretches beyond food, too: a text, a doctrine, a bloodline can be adulterated. And you'll meet the negative twice as often as the verb: **unadulterated** = pure, nothing mixed in ("pure, unadulterated joy").

⚠️ **adulterate ≠ adulate.** *Adulate* (AJ-uh-layt) is to praise someone fawningly — flattery, not fraud. One debases a product, the other inflates a person.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Adultery** adds an outsider to a marriage; **adulterate** adds an outsider to the product.

Same Latin root, same idea — something pure is corrupted by letting in what doesn't belong, and both words carry the smell of betrayal.

### In sentences
1. Inspectors found the honey had been **adulterated** with corn syrup and sold at a premium.
2. Don't **adulterate** a good whisky with Coke, mate.

### In the wild
You've heard the negative form a hundred times without noticing the verb: commentators shouting about **"pure, unadulterated joy"** when a last-minute goal goes in — *unadulterated* means nothing has been mixed in to weaken it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **adulterare** = *"to corrupt,"* from **ad-** (to) + **alter** (other) — literally *to make other than it was.*

That *alter* is a workhorse: **alter, alternative, alter ego, altercation, alternate,** even **altruism** (living for the *other*).

# **advocate** *(v. / n.)* — **AD-vuh-kayt (v.) / AD-vuh-kut (n.)**

*The ending shifts with the job: you AD-vuh-KAYT for a cause, you are an AD-vuh-kut. Same split as separate, estimate, deliberate.*

### Means
**Verb:** to publicly plead for, argue in favour of, recommend a cause or course of action.
**Noun:** the person doing the pleading — a champion, a supporter who speaks up (and in Scots and much European usage, literally a courtroom lawyer).

The word is louder than *support*. You can support something silently, in a poll or in your heart; to **advocate** it you have to open your mouth and take a public side. It also carries the flavour of speaking *on behalf of someone else* — the patient, the refugee, the defendant — which is why "advocate" attaches so naturally to the powerless. Note the preposition: you advocate *for* a person, but you simply advocate *a policy* (no "for" needed in careful prose).

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside is **vocate** — *voice*. An advocate lends you theirs.

Support is silent; advocacy is audible, which is the whole difference between agreeing with a cause and advocating one.

### In sentences
1. The commission's report **advocated** a phased withdrawal of the subsidy, over the objections of three of its own members.
2. She's been a fierce **advocate** for her brother ever since the school tried to quietly drop him from the programme.

### In the wild
**Playing devil's advocate.** It's a real Vatican job — the *advocatus diaboli* was the official appointed to argue *against* a proposed saint, hunting for reasons the candidate didn't deserve canonisation. Someone paid to plead the opposing side.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **vocāre** (*to call*) — an advocate was someone **called to your side** in court.

That *vocāre* root is a GRE goldmine: **vocation** (a calling), **evoke** (call forth), **revoke** (call back), **provoke** (call forth anger), **invoke**, **convoke**, and **equivocate** (call it two ways at once).

# **aggrandize** *(v.)* — **uh-GRAN-dyze**

*Noun form shifts: aggrandizement is uh-GRAN-diz-muhnt, not -dyze-ment.*

### Means
To increase someone's power, wealth, or standing — or, more often, to make them *appear* greater than they are.

The word rarely comes out neutral. When a general aggrandizes his territory, it's expansion at someone's expense; when a colleague aggrandizes himself in a meeting, it's puffery. Compare **enhance**, which improves the thing itself: aggrandizing usually inflates the *reputation* while the substance stays put. The most common form on the GRE is **self-aggrandizing** — the man who tells the story so that he ends up the hero of it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **GRAND** is sitting right there in the middle — to a-GRAND-ize is to make someone *grand*, usually grander than the facts allow.

The hidden word gives you the meaning, and the gap between "grand" and "actually grand" gives you the sneer.

### In sentences
1. Every reform he proposed had the convenient effect of **aggrandizing** the office he happened to hold.
2. It was a team effort, but you'd never know it from his **self-aggrandizing** LinkedIn post.

### In the wild
**Self-aggrandizing** is a staple of political journalism — it's the standard polite way a columnist calls a politician's grand gesture an exercise in self-promotion.

### Where it comes from
Latin **grandis** = *"great, large,"* with *ad-* ("toward") — literally *to make greater*, via French *agrandir*.

One root, a useful cluster: **grand**, **grandeur**, **grandiose** (impressively big, or absurdly pretending to be), **grandiloquent** (speaking in swollen language).

# **alacrity** *(n.)* — **uh-LAK-ruh-tee**

### Means
**Brisk, cheerful eagerness** — moving fast *because you're glad to*.

Speed alone isn't alacrity. A prisoner marched to work moves quickly; a volunteer who jumps up before you finish asking moves with alacrity. The word carries **visible willingness** — the promptness is evidence of the enthusiasm. It's warm, faintly formal, and lives almost entirely in one phrase: *with alacrity*. Watch for dry irony too — "he accepted the free upgrade with alacrity" gently mocks how fast the hand went up.

⚠️ **alacrity ≠ celerity.** *Celerity* is bare speed (same root as *accelerate*) — a missile has celerity. *Alacrity* is speed **plus gladness**. Swap them and you've stripped out the cheerfulness the GRE is testing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **a-LACK-rity** — yet it means there's **no lack** of willingness.

The sound plants "lack" in the middle of a word that means the exact opposite: zero hesitation, zero reluctance.

### In sentences
1. The junior clerks, sensing that promotion hung on it, took up the thankless assignment with surprising **alacrity**.
2. Ask him to review your code and he'll stall for a week — mention free pizza and he's there with real **alacrity**.

### In the wild
Shakespeare's *Richard III*, the night before Bosworth: *"I have not that alacrity of spirit, / Nor cheer of mind, that I was wont to have."* Richard defines the word for you in the next line — **alacrity = cheer of mind**.

### Where it comes from
Latin **alacer / alacris** = *"brisk, lively, cheerful"* — briskness and good cheer bundled into one adjective from the start.

Same root, travelling through Italian: **allegro**, the musical direction meaning not merely *fast* but *fast and merry*. If you know what allegro sounds like, you know what alacrity feels like.

# **ambivalent** *(adj.)* — **am-BIV-uh-luhnt**

*Stress lands on BIV, not on AM — not "AM-bi-valent."*

### Means
Having **two opposing feelings about the same thing at the same time** — attraction and repulsion, wanting and dreading.

The word is routinely misused to mean *meh, don't care*. It means the opposite of that: an ambivalent person cares **twice**, in contradictory directions, and is stuck. Someone **indifferent** feels nothing about the job offer; someone **ambivalent** desperately wants it and desperately doesn't. Zero versus a tug-of-war. Register is educated and slightly clinical — it comes out of psychiatry — so it suggests a mind examining its own divided state rather than a shrug.

⚠️ **ambivalent ≠ ambiguous.**
- **ambivalent** = *the person* is torn between two feelings
- **ambiguous** = *the thing* can be read two ways

His reply was ambiguous; his feelings were ambivalent.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ambi-VALENT-ine's Day** — two hearts pulling at once, not a heart that's switched off.

The buried *valentine* keeps feeling *in* the word, blocking the standard error of using it for apathy.

### In sentences
1. The committee remained **ambivalent** about the merger, alive to its savings and equally alive to what it would cost the town.
2. I'm pretty **ambivalent** about going — I'd love to see everyone, and I'd love to be in bed by nine.

### In the wild
Facebook's old **"It's complicated"** relationship status is ambivalence in two words — not *no feelings*, but too many, pointing opposite ways.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ambi-** = *"both"* + **valēre** = *"to be strong, to be worth."* Both sides are **strong** — that's the whole word, and the reason it can't mean indifference.

**ambi-** gives you **ambidextrous** (skilled with both hands), **ambiguous** (drivable both ways), **ambit**.
**valēre** gives you **valid**, **value**, **valiant**, **prevail**, **convalesce** — all about strength or worth.

# **ameliorate** *(v.)* — **uh-MEEL-yuh-rayt**

*Also said uh-MEE-lee-uh-rayt (5 syllables). The stress never moves off MEEL — not "a-mel-ee-OR-ate."*

### Means
To make something bad **less bad** — to improve conditions that were painful, harsh, or deficient.

The word carries a precondition: nothing gets ameliorated unless it was already in a poor state. You *improve* a recipe, a serve, a profit margin; you *ameliorate* suffering, overcrowding, soil, working conditions. And it promises only partial repair — a policy that ameliorates poverty has softened it, not ended it. Register is formal and institutional: it belongs to policy papers, court judgments and NGO reports, and sounds faintly pompous in a kitchen.

### Trick to lock it in
> **a-MEAL-iorate** — a hot meal doesn't cure poverty, it just makes it less bad.

The buried "meal" gives you both halves of the word: the situation was already grim, and the fix is real but partial.

### In sentences
1. Drainage works did something to **ameliorate** the flooding, though the worst-hit streets flooded again that winter.
2. A decent night's sleep won't fix the project, but it might **ameliorate** your mood about it.

### In the wild
Standard equipment in policy and legal prose — aid reports and judgments constantly promise "measures to ameliorate the effects of" a drought, a recession, a lockdown. Spot it there and you'll notice it never says the problem was solved.

### Where it comes from
Latin **melior** = *"better"* (as in *meliorism*, the belief the world can be made better).

Worth pairing with its opposite: **pejor** = *"worse"*, which gives you **pejorative** — a word chosen to make something sound worse. Melior up, pejor down.

# **amenable** *(adj.)* — **uh-MEE-nuh-buhl**

*Also heard as uh-MEN-uh-buhl; both are accepted.*

### Means
**1.** Willing to go along with a suggestion; open to persuasion, easy to work with.
**2.** *(of a thing)* Susceptible to a given treatment or method — **amenable to** analysis, surgery, compromise.

Almost always followed by **to**. Sense 1 describes low resistance, not enthusiasm: someone amenable doesn't push back, which is a step below *eager* and a step above *grudging*. It says nothing about whether they're warm — a cold, formal colleague can be perfectly amenable. Sense 2 is the workhorse of academic and medical prose, where it's coldly neutral: a problem amenable to modelling is simply one the method can get a grip on.

⚠️ **amenable ≠ amiable.** *Amiable* = friendly, likeable by nature (a disposition). *Amenable* = willing to comply, or open to a method (a response). A charming host is amiable; a colleague who accepts your timeline without a fight is amenable — and one can easily be either without being the other.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Amen**-able — they say *amen* to your plan.

*Amen* means "so be it" — quiet assent rather than applause, which is exactly the temperature of the word.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal found the minister **amenable** to reason once the political cost of refusal was made plain.
2. Ask him — he's usually **amenable** to swapping shifts if you give him a day's notice.

### In the wild
Standard in clinical language: a tumour is described as **amenable to resection**, meaning surgery can actually get at it. Same word you'd use for a cooperative patient — one sense about people, one about problems.

### Where it comes from
Old French **amener** = *"to lead to"* (*à* + *mener*, to lead). Amenable is literally **able to be led** — which covers both senses: a person you can lead to your view, a problem you can lead to a solution. Same *mener* gives **demeanour** (how you conduct — lead — yourself) and **promenade** (a leading-forward, a stroll).

🚫 **No connection to *amare*, "to love."** That root gives *amiable, amicable, amorous* — the sound-alikes that cost marks.

# **anachronistic** *(adj.)* — **uh-nak-ruh-NIS-tik**

*The noun shifts its stress: anachronism = uh-NAK-ruh-niz-um.*

### Means
Belonging to a different time period than the one it appears in — chronologically misplaced.

It cuts two ways. Neutrally, it's a **factual error**: a wristwatch on a gladiator, a character in 1850 mentioning a telephone. Judgementally, it's a **verdict on something that has outlived its era** — a rule, an institution, a job title that should have died decades ago. That second use is where the GRE lives, and it carries a dry contempt: calling a law anachronistic says it isn't just old, it's *absurd* that it survived. Note the direction — anachronistic things are almost always relics dragged forward, not things arriving too early.

⚠️ **anachronistic ≠ anarchistic.** *Anachronistic* = out of **time** (*chronos*). *Anarchistic* = against **rule** (*archos*). One letter's difference on the page, two unrelated roots.

### Trick to lock it in
> **CHRON** sits right in the middle — a clock stuck in the wrong century.

Spot *chron* (as in *chronological*) inside the word and you've got the whole meaning: the timeline is off.

### In sentences
1. The committee dismissed the dress code as an **anachronistic** relic of a workplace no one under fifty remembered.
2. Fax machines in a 2026 office? Completely **anachronistic**.

### In the wild
The **Starbucks cup left on the table in *Game of Thrones*** — a paper coffee cup in medieval Westeros, the most-screenshotted anachronism in television history.

### Where it comes from
Greek **ana-** (*back*) + **chronos** (*time*) — literally "back-timed."

*Chronos* is a workhorse root: **chronic** (lasting over time), **chronology**, **chronicle**, **synchronise** (same-time), **chronometer**.

# **audacious** *(adj.)* — **aw-DAY-shuhs**

### Means
Daring far beyond what caution allows — either **boldly original** (an audacious plan, an audacious heist) or **shamelessly cheeky** (an audacious lie, an audacious request).

Which one you get depends entirely on whether the speaker admires the risk. *Brave* praises; *impudent* condemns; **audacious** sits on the fence and takes its charge from the sentence around it. Either way the person has ignored a limit everyone else respected — the only question is whether the room applauds or gasps. Note the strength: audacious is never mild, and never accidental.

### Trick to lock it in
> **au-DAY-shus** — the noun is *audacity*, and you've heard it snarled: **"the audacity!"**

That snarl is the cheeky half of the word, so remember the adjective as the same nerve viewed from a friendlier angle.

### In sentences
1. The firm made an **audacious** bid for a rival three times its size, and the market did not stop talking about it for a week.
2. He had the **audacious** cheek to ask for a raise seven days after writing off the company van.

### In the wild
Obama's bestseller **_The Audacity of Hope_** — the title works precisely because it's a boast and a confession at once: hoping that hard is nerve.

### Where it comes from
Latin **audēre** = *"to dare"* → *audāx, audācis* (bold) → **audacity**.

🚫 **Not *audīre*, "to hear."** That's the other family entirely: *audio, audience, audible, audition, auditor*. Same first three letters, no relation.

# **avaricious** *(adj.)* — **av-uh-RISH-uhs**

### Means
Greedy for **money and possessions** — grasping, hoarding, never satisfied.

Not general greed: this word is specifically about **wealth**. A glutton isn't avaricious; a landlord raising rent on a dying tenant is. It's also a heavy, moralising word — *greedy* is what you call a child reaching for a third biscuit, **avaricious** is what a judge or a novelist calls a man whose whole character has been eaten by money. Using it is passing sentence.

⚠️ **avaricious ≠ voracious.**
- **avaricious** = hungry for **money**
- **voracious** = hungry, full stop — huge appetite for food, or for books

A voracious reader is a compliment. An avaricious one would be stealing the library.

### Trick to lock it in
> **av-a-RICH-us** — the stressed syllable is literally **RICH**.

Say it aloud and the word tells you what it wants: riches, not food, not power.

### In sentences
1. The estate passed to an **avaricious** cousin who sold the orchards within the month.
2. He's not ambitious, he's **avaricious** — he'd bill his own mother for the taxi.

### In the wild
**Avarice** is the formal name of one of the **seven deadly sins** — the one everyone else just calls Greed.

### Where it comes from
Latin **avēre** = *"to crave"* → **avarus**, *"greedy."*

Same craving root gives you **avid** — an *avid* golfer craves the game, an *avaricious* one craves the prize money.

# **banal** *(adj.)* — **buh-NAL**

*Rhymes with canal. BAY-nuhl and buh-NAHL are also accepted, but buh-NAL is safest.*

### Means
So ordinary, so predictable, so often repeated that it has gone flat — a remark, plot, compliment or design with no originality left in it.

**Banal** is a verdict, not a description. *Common* and *ordinary* just report frequency; **banal** adds contempt and boredom — the thing is stale *and* the speaker is tired of it. It attaches most naturally to things that were *supposed* to be interesting: art, conversation, writing, ideas. A bus timetable can't be banal; a poem, a wedding speech, or a corporate slogan very much can.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ban**al ideas should be **banned** — you've heard them a thousand times.

The hidden word **ban** is also the actual root, and the exasperation in "ban it already" is exactly the contempt the word carries.

### In sentences
1. The gallery's much-hyped centrepiece proved **banal** — a slogan in neon, a gesture exhausted decades ago.
2. His best man's speech was so **banal** I could have written it myself: she's the lucky one, he's punching above his weight, to the happy couple.

### In the wild
Hannah Arendt's phrase **"the banality of evil"** — her report on the Eichmann trial, arguing the worst crimes were carried out by a dull functionary doing paperwork, not a monster.

### Where it comes from
From Old French **banal**, "shared by all the lord's tenants," from **ban** — a feudal lord's proclamation summoning everyone. The village mill and oven were *banal*: everybody had to use them. Compulsory for all → used by all → utterly common → dreary.

Same **ban** root: **banish**, **banns** (of marriage, proclaimed publicly), **abandon**, **contraband**, **bandit** (a man proclaimed an outlaw).

# **benign** *(adj.)* — **bih-NINE**

*The g is silent — never "ben-ig-n". It comes back in benignant and malignant.*

### Means
Gentle and kindly in manner; harmless in effect; **medically, not cancerous**; of weather or conditions, mild and favourable.

Most of its force is **negative** — it says what *isn't* there. A benign tumour isn't a healthy one, just one that won't kill you; a benign smile is pleasant but tells you nothing. Compare **benevolent**, which is active goodwill (someone *doing* good), and **benign**, which is mostly the absence of threat. That's why GRE passages use it for things that look dangerous and turn out not to be: a benign policy, a benign amendment, benign neglect.

⚠️ **benign ≠ benighted.** *Benighted* (bih-NYE-tid) means **ignorant, unenlightened** — from *night*, morally in the dark. Nothing to do with kindness: a benighted era is a backward one.

### Trick to lock it in
> **benign rhymes with *fine* — and "it's fine" is exactly how much reassurance it gives.**

Fine isn't good, it's just not bad — which is the whole register of the word.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal dismissed the clause as **benign**, a formality with no power to compel anyone.
2. The dog looks terrifying but he's completely **benign** — he'll just drool on you.

### In the wild
The word you wait for after a biopsy. **"The results came back benign"** is the good-news line in every hospital scene — and note what it actually promises: not cured, just not cancer.

### Where it comes from
Latin **bene** = *"well"* (+ *gignere*, "to be born" — literally *well-born*, hence well-natured).

**bene** is one of the best roots on the GRE: **benefit, benefactor, benevolent, benediction, beneficiary**. Its twin is **male-** = *badly*: **malign, malevolent, malediction, malefactor**. Learn the pair and you get twenty words — *benign* and *malign* are exact opposites, built the same way.

# **brazen** *(adj. / v.)* — **BRAY-zuhn**

### Means
**Adj.:** boldly and openly wrong — doing the improper thing in full view, with no shame and no attempt to hide it.
**Verb (usually *brazen it out*):** to face down an accusation by refusing to look embarrassed.

Bold is admiring, audacious can be admiring, **brazen almost never is**. The scandal isn't the act, it's the *openness* — a brazen lie is one told to your face by someone who knows you know. It carries a hint of contempt for the audience: the person isn't sneaking, because sneaking would concede that the rule mattered.

⚠️ **brazen ≠ blazon.**
- **brazen** = shameless (from *brass*)
- **blazon** = to display or proclaim publicly, as on a coat of arms — hence **emblazoned** across a banner.

Both are about being seen; only *brazen* is about not caring.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Brazen has *brass* in it — and brass doesn't blush.**

The metal face is the whole point: the person can't redden, so the boldness reads as shamelessness rather than courage.

### In sentences
1. The minister offered a **brazen** denial, contradicting a recording that had been broadcast an hour earlier.
2. He just walked out with the office monitor under his arm — you have to admire the **brazen** cheek of it.

### In the wild
Newsrooms have a fixed collocation for it: a **brazen daylight robbery**. The word is doing real work there — not that the thieves were skilled, but that they didn't bother with the dark.

### Where it comes from
Old English **bræsen** = *"made of brass."* The metaphor jumped from the metal to the face: hard, unblushing, unmoveable. That's why you can **brazen it out** — put on a brass front and refuse to concede that you've been caught.

# **calumny** *(n.)* — **KAL-uhm-nee**

*Adjective form: calumnious (kuh-LUM-nee-uhs) — stress moves.*

### Means
A **false statement made deliberately to destroy someone's reputation** — or the practice of making them.

*Slander* is the courtroom word; *calumny* is the moral one. It carries the speaker's disgust with it: you don't call something calumny unless you think the victim is innocent and the accuser is vile. And unlike *rumour* or *gossip*, which can drift into being by accident, calumny is **aimed** — someone chose the lie and chose the target. Formal, faintly biblical, and best deployed when you want the accusation itself to sound shameful.

**calumny ≠ calamity.** *Calumny* is a slander; a *calamity* is a disaster. Similar rhythm, unrelated words — a calumny can *cause* a calamity, which is exactly why the exam likes the pair.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Calumny** = a poisonous **column** — a lie set in print to ruin a name.

The sound hook holds the nuance: a column is written, published and deliberate, never an accidental slip of gossip.

### In sentences
1. The biography rescues her from two centuries of **calumny**, showing that the letters used to condemn her were forged.
2. He didn't just disagree with me — he went around the office spreading outright **calumny** about my expenses.

### In the wild
Hamlet to Ophelia: *"Be thou as chaste as ice, as pure as snow, thou shalt not escape **calumny**."* — i.e. innocence is no defence against a determined liar.

### Where it comes from
Latin **calumnia** = *"a false accusation, a trick,"* from **calvī**, *"to deceive."*

The surprise: **challenge** is the same word. *Calumnia* → Old French *chalenge*, which originally meant an accusation or false claim, before softening into "a dare." So a challenge and a calumny are etymological twins — one kept the fight, the other kept the lie.

# **candid** *(adj.)* — **KAN-did**

### Means
Honest and open, especially about something awkward, unflattering, or usually kept quiet. Second sense: **unposed** — a candid photo is one taken before anyone arranged their face.

Where *blunt* and *brutal* imply the speaker enjoyed it, and *frank* is merely direct, **candid** carries a whiff of *disclosure* — the speaker had a reason to hide this and chose not to. That's why it's almost always approving: you're candid **about** your own failures, doubts, or finances. A CEO who is candid about a bad quarter earns credit; one who is blunt about it just upset the room.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Candid** is a **candle** — it lights up the part of the room you'd rather leave dim.

Same root, and it captures the nuance exactly: candour isn't just saying things, it's illuminating what was conveniently in shadow.

### In sentences
1. In a **candid** memoir, the former minister admits he knew the figures were wrong and signed off anyway.
2. Can I be **candid**? That shirt is not doing what you think it's doing.

### In the wild
**Candid Camera**, the hidden-camera show that ran for decades — the whole premise is catching people unposed, before they've had time to arrange themselves.

### Where it comes from
Latin **candidus** = *"shining white"*, from **candēre**, *"to glow"* — the same root as **candle** and **incandescent**.

The bonus: a Roman seeking office wore a bleached white toga to advertise his purity — hence **candidate**. So *candid, candle, incandescent, candidate* all come from one glow.

# **castigate** *(v.)* — **KAS-tuh-gayt**

### Means
To **rebuke or criticise severely** — a punishing telling-off, usually delivered publicly and from a position of moral authority.

Where *criticise* is neutral and *scold* is domestic, **castigate** is the register of editorials, inquiry reports and judgements: the speaker is not just disagreeing, they are pronouncing you at fault. It implies the target *deserved* it, and it implies an audience — you castigate someone **for** a specific failing, on the record. Almost never used of a quiet word in private.

⚠️ **castigate ≠ instigate.**
- **castigate** = *lash* someone for what they did
- **instigate** = *start* the thing off

The rioters were **instigated** by one man; the judge later **castigated** them all.

### Trick to lock it in
> **You castigate in print; you yell in person.**

If there's no audience and no moral high ground, it isn't castigation — it's just shouting.

### In sentences
1. The commission's final report **castigated** the regulator for a decade of wilful inattention.
2. My editor **castigated** me for missing the deadline — three paragraphs of email, all of it deserved.

### In the wild
It's a newsroom workhorse: whenever a headline says an inquiry, a court or an opposition leader **"castigated"** someone, that's the word doing its one job — official blame, said out loud.

### Where it comes from
Latin **castigāre** = *castus* ("pure, chaste") + *agere* ("to drive") — literally **to drive someone back into purity**. Punishment as moral correction, not revenge.

That root pays out widely: **chaste**, **chasten**, **chastise** (the same word worn down through French), **caste** (a "pure" bloodline), and **incest** — *in-* + *castus*, "not chaste."

# **caustic** *(adj.)* — **KAW-stik**

### Means
**Literally:** able to burn or eat away flesh and material by chemical action — lye, acid, drain cleaner.
**Figuratively:** bitingly sarcastic; scathing.

A caustic remark is corrosive, not merely clever. Sarcasm can be affectionate and sarcasm can be dumb; **caustic** is neither — it's sharp, controlled, and aimed to strip something off the person it hits. There's usually bitterness behind it, which is why *caustic* describes critics, exes and burnt-out colleagues far more often than comedians.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Caustic soda strips paint. Caustic wit strips skin.**

The chemical meaning is the literal one, so the figurative sense keeps the damage — a caustic line is meant to leave a mark, not get a laugh.

### In sentences
1. His **caustic** review of the production was quoted for years, chiefly by people who had not seen it.
2. Don't ask her for feedback before coffee — she goes full **caustic**.

### In the wild
The **caustic soda** on the drain-cleaner bottle under your sink — the skull-and-crossbones warning is the whole meaning of the word in one label.

### Where it comes from
Greek **kaiein** = *"to burn"* → **kaustikos**, "burning."

Same root gives you **cauterize** (seal a wound by burning it) and **holocaust** (*holos* "whole" + *kaustos* "burnt" — burnt whole). Once you hear the burn in it, the sarcastic sense stops being a separate definition to memorise.

# **construe** *(v.)* — **kuhn-STROO**

### Means
To **assign a particular meaning** to words or actions — to take them *as* meaning something.

Where *interpret* is broad and neutral (you interpret a poem, a dream, a dataset), **construe** shows up precisely where a reading is **contested or could go the other way**. It carries a whiff of the courtroom: someone is deciding which of two possible meanings a sentence or a gesture will officially bear. That's why it so often appears defensively — *"don't construe my silence as consent"*, *"this shall not be construed as an admission"*. If nothing is ambiguous, nobody needs to construe it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **con-S-TRUE** — out of an ambiguous phrase, you pick which reading is **true**.

The word literally hides *true* in it, and construing is the act of settling which meaning counts as the true one.

### In sentences
1. The court **construed** the clause narrowly, holding that it covered damage to the building but not to its contents.
2. He asked if I was busy this weekend, and I stupidly **construed** that as an invitation.

### In the wild
Buried in almost every contract and terms-of-service you've ever scrolled past: *"This Agreement shall be **construed** in accordance with the laws of..."* — lawyers use the word constantly, because their whole job is fixing which reading wins.

### Where it comes from
Latin **construere** = *con-* ("together") + **struere** ("to build") — literally *to build up*. You build a meaning out of the parts of a sentence.

Same *struere* gives you **construct**, **structure**, **instruct** (build knowledge into someone), **obstruct**, **destroy**. Add *mis-* and you get **misconstrue**: you built the meaning wrong.

# **contrite** *(adj.)* — **kuhn-TRYTE**

*Rhymes with *right*. Also heard with front stress: KON-tryte.*

### Means
Feeling and showing **deep, humbled regret** for something you did wrong.

*Sorry* is the minimum; *apologetic* is the performance; **contrite** is the state of being genuinely crushed by your own guilt — head down, defences gone, no excuses offered. It presumes real fault, not a mishap, and it carries a moral, almost confessional weight. Note how often it attaches to *appearance*: someone **looks** contrite, **sounds** contrite — which is exactly why "suitably contrite" is so often said with a raised eyebrow.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Contrite** has **trite** in it — and *trite* means *worn out*. A contrite person has been worn down by guilt.

Both words come from the same idea of rubbing something away, so the contrite one isn't just apologising — they've been ground flat.

### In sentences
1. The minister issued a **contrite** statement, accepting full responsibility and declining to name anyone else.
2. He turned up the next morning looking thoroughly **contrite**, holding two coffees and not saying much.

### In the wild
The **Act of Contrition** — the prayer said in Catholic confession, and the one every dying gangster gasps out in a mob film. Same word, same idea: you're not just apologising, you're broken up about it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **conterere** = *"to grind down, wear away"* (*com-* intensive + *terere*, "to rub"). To be contrite is to be **ground to pieces** by remorse.

That root pays out across the GRE: **trite** (a phrase rubbed smooth by overuse), **attrition** (wearing an enemy or a workforce down), **detritus** (what's rubbed off — debris), **detriment** (damage by wearing away).

# **convoluted** *(adj.)* — **KON-vuh-loo-tid**

### Means
Twisted, coiled, folded in on itself — and so, of an argument, plot, sentence, or process: **so intricate it can no longer be followed**.

*Complex* can be a compliment and *complicated* is often just an honest fact, but **convoluted** blames the maker: the tangle didn't have to be there. It carries a faint whiff of suspicion too — convoluted excuses, convoluted accounting, convoluted procedures all hint that the twisting is doing work someone wants hidden. The literal sense (folded, coiled) survives mainly in anatomy: the *convoluted* tubules of the kidney.

### Trick to lock it in
> **No one has ever been praised for a convoluted explanation** — the word is a verdict, not a description.

Reach for it only when you mean the complexity was avoidable and someone chose it.

### In sentences
1. The statute's **convoluted** drafting had spawned three decades of contradictory rulings.
2. Her explanation for the missing money got more **convoluted** every time she told it.

### In the wild
It's the standard word in film reviews for a Christopher Nolan plot — *Tenet* and *Inception* get called **convoluted** almost as a reflex, meaning: too many folds, and not all of them earned.

### Where it comes from
Latin **volvere** = *"to roll"*, with **con-** = *"together"* — rolled up together, coiled.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **revolve** (roll back), **evolve** (roll out), **involve** (roll in), **devolve** (roll down), **voluble** (words that roll out easily), and **volume** (a rolled scroll).

# **covet** *(v.)* — **KUV-it**

*First syllable rhymes with love, not cove.*

### Means
To want something badly — specifically something that **belongs to someone else**.

Plain *wanting* is morally blank; coveting is loaded. The word carries a whiff of sin and envy: you are eyeing your neighbour's car, your colleague's job, your friend's spouse. It also implies the desire is **sustained and inward** — a slow ache you nurse, not a quick impulse.

One twist worth knowing: the participle **coveted** flips positive. "The coveted Booker Prize" just means everybody wants it, and the prestige rubs off on the thing, not the shame onto the wanter.

⚠️ **covet ≠ covert.** One transposed letter, opposite worlds: **covet** = *desire what isn't yours*; **covert** (KOH-vert / KUV-ert) = *hidden, secret* — a covert operation. A spy runs covert missions; a thief covets the jewels.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Covet** hides **Cupid** — same root, and Cupid's arrow always points at somebody *else's* beloved.

The Cupid link gives you both halves of the word: burning desire, aimed where it has no business being.

### In sentences
1. For thirty years he had **coveted** the corner office, and on the day he got it he found the view unremarkable.
2. Don't pretend — you've **coveted** that jacket since the moment she walked in wearing it.

### In the wild
The Tenth Commandment: *"Thou shalt not **covet** thy neighbour's house... nor his wife."* Note that the sin isn't wanting a house — it's wanting **his**.

### Where it comes from
Latin **cupere** = *"to desire"*, via **cupiditas** and Old French *coveitier*.

Same root gives you **Cupid** (the god of desire), **cupidity** (greed for money — a favourite GRE word), and **concupiscence** (lust).

# **craven** *(adj. (also n.))* — **KRAY-vuhn**

### Means
Cowardly — but **contemptibly, shamefully** so.

*Afraid* is a feeling anyone can have; *timid* is a temperament; *craven* is a **verdict**. It says the person had a duty to stand firm and folded anyway, usually to save their own skin. Nobody calls themselves craven — it's an accusation, and a withering, slightly old-fashioned one, at home in editorials and courtrooms rather than conversation. Its natural companions are *capitulation, surrender, silence, appeasement*: the cowardice of someone who **gave in** when it counted.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Craven = caves in.**

Say it fast and "craven" collapses into "cave-in" — which is exactly what the craven person does the moment pressure is applied.

### In sentences
1. The committee's **craven** silence on the matter told the public everything it needed to know.
2. He talked a big game all week, then made some **craven** excuse and didn't show up.

### In the wild
It's the standard insult of political journalism — *craven capitulation*, *craven refusal to condemn*. If you've read a newspaper opinion page, you've met this word doing exactly that job.

### Where it comes from
Probably from Old French **cravanter**, *"to crush, overwhelm"* — the craven is the man already broken. In medieval trial by combat, a fighter who cried **"craven!"** formally surrendered: he kept his life and lost his honour. That's still the flavour — defeat you chose.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *crave*.** Different word entirely; a craven person wants less, not more.

# **decorum** *(n.)* — **dih-KOR-uhm**

### Means
Behaviour that fits the occasion — dignified, restrained, correct by the unwritten rules of the setting. The adjective is **decorous**.

Note what it is *not* about: goodness. Decorum governs **form**, not morality — you can be a scoundrel and still observe perfect decorum at the funeral. It's also entirely situational: what counts as decorum in a courtroom differs from a wedding, and breaching it offends the *occasion* rather than any principle. The word itself is formal and faintly institutional — judges demand "order and decorum," Parliament invokes it, your friends never do.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Decorum** is **décor** for behaviour.

Décor is what makes a room look right for its purpose; decorum is what makes your conduct look right for the room — both are about surface fittingness, not inner worth.

### In sentences
1. The chamber maintained a chilly **decorum** even as the two ministers traded accusations through gritted teeth.
2. Try to show some **decorum** at dinner — it's her parents, not the lads.

### In the wild
Wilfred Owen's *Dulce et Decorum Est* — Horace's line that it is "sweet and **fitting** to die for one's country," which Owen calls "the old Lie." The poem's whole force comes from that word meaning *seemly, what the occasion demands*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **decēre** = *"to be fitting, to be seemly."* The neuter of *decōrus* ("becoming") gave the noun.

The root pays out widely: **decent** (fitting, hence respectable), **decorous**, **decorate**, **décor** — and, negated, **indecent**. All circle the same idea of *what suits*.

# **deft** *(adj.)* — **DEFT**

### Means
Skilful and **quick** — handling something with neat, economical, unfussy competence.

*Skilful* is a general compliment; **deft** is about the *lightness of touch*. A deft move looks effortless and takes no more effort than it needs — a surgeon's stitch, a diplomat sidestepping a hostile question, a chef boning a fish in four seconds. It praises finesse rather than power or brilliance, and it's often used of small physical movements or delicate social manoeuvres. Nothing in it is negative: deft is admiring, mildly formal, and a favourite of critics ("a deft touch").

### Trick to lock it in
> **Deft** is **daft** with one letter changed — and one is all quick skill, the other all foolishness.

They really are relatives (both from Old English *gedæfte*, "gentle, meek"), but the meanings split hard, so let the near-spelling remind you deft = clever hands, daft = no sense at all.

### In sentences
1. The negotiator's **deft** handling of the walkout kept both delegations at the table.
2. She **deftly** flipped the pancake without even looking up from her phone.

### In the wild
The stock film-review phrase **"a deft touch"** — critics reach for it whenever a director handles tricky material lightly instead of hammering it.

### Where it comes from
🚫 **Not related to *deaf***, despite the spelling. But it *is* the same word as **daft**: Old English *gedæfte* meant "mild, gentle," which drifted one way into "meek → silly" (*daft*) and the other into "fitting, skilful" (*deft*).

# **demur** *(v. (also n.))* — **dih-MER**

### Means
To **raise an objection or hesitate** before agreeing — to balk, politely.

The force is deliberately low. A person who *refuses* has said no; a person who **demurs** has only signalled reluctance — a raised eyebrow, a "well, I'm not sure that's quite right," a delay dressed as courtesy. It's the vocabulary of boardrooms, courts and diplomacy, where open refusal is rude but silence is consent. Often it's a way of not answering at all: *asked to comment, he demurred.*

⚠️ **demur ≠ demure.**
- **demur** *(dih-MER, verb)* = object mildly, hesitate
- **demure** *(dih-MYOOR, adj.)* = modest, shy, reserved in manner

One letter, different word class, different meaning. A demure guest might still demur.

### Trick to lock it in
> **demur** ends in **-mur**, as in **murmur** — an objection *murmured*, never shouted.

That murmur captures the strength of it: audible disagreement, well short of a no.

### In sentences
1. Counsel **demurred** at the judge's characterisation of the evidence, though he declined to press the point.
2. I asked if he'd cover my shift and he **demurred** — didn't say no, just went very quiet about it.

### In the wild
Standard press-conference shorthand: when a reporter asks a politician whether she'll run and the write-up says *"she demurred,"* it means she dodged without ever refusing.

### Where it comes from
Latin **morari** = *"to delay"* (from *mora*, a delay), with **de-**. To demur was originally to **hold things up** — which is exactly why it still means hesitating rather than refusing.

Same root: **moratorium** (an official delay), and the legal **demurrer** (a plea that stalls a case).

- **Intensity scale** · [Mild objection to revolt](groups.md#intensity__mild-objection-to-revolt) — with *dissent, flout, mutiny*

# **derivative** *(adj. / n.)* — **dih-RIV-uh-tiv**

### Means
**Adj.:** taken from an existing source rather than invented — and therefore **unoriginal**.
**Noun:** a thing that comes from something else (in finance, a contract whose value is drawn entirely from an underlying asset).

As criticism it is cold rather than angry. Calling a novel *derivative* is not calling it stolen or fake — the writer did the work, honestly. The charge is that everything worth noticing in it came from somewhere else, so there is nothing of the maker in it. Reviewers, examiners and critics reach for it precisely because it sounds measured: *plagiarised* is a legal accusation, *hackneyed* is a sneer, **derivative** is a diagnosis.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Derivative = *derive* + im*itative*** — it flows from a source, so it has no source of its own.

The insult is not theft but dependence: pull out what it borrowed and nothing is left standing.

### In sentences
1. The jury praised her technique but found the collection **derivative**, a careful echo of designers who had shown a decade earlier.
2. It's not a bad album, it's just **derivative** — you can name the band behind every track.

### In the wild
A financial **derivative** — an option, a future — has no value of its own; every penny it is worth is drawn from the asset underneath it. That is the adjective in one object.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dē-** (*away*) + **rīvus** (*stream*) → **dērīvāre**, *to draw water off from a channel*. A derivative work is a ditch led off someone else's river.

Same root: **river**, **derive**, and — pleasingly — **rival**, from *rivales*, two people forced to share one stream.

### Also sits in
- **Meaning cluster** · [Not Original](groups.md#meaning__not-original) — with *pastiche, hackneyed, conventional*
- **Connotation** · [Stale from repetition](groups.md#connotation__stale-from-repetition) — with *hackneyed, banal, bromide, platitude*
- **Opposites** · [Fresh-minted vs. secondhand](groups.md#antonym__fresh-minted-vs-secondhand) — with *ingenious, unprecedented, coin, iconoclastic, hackneyed, banal, pastiche, bromide, platitude, mimic*

# **desiccate** *(v.)* — **DES-i-kayt**

### Means
To dry out **completely** — to remove every trace of moisture, preserving the thing as a husk.

*Dry* is a state; **desiccate** is a process taken to its limit. You don't desiccate a shirt on a radiator — you desiccate fruit, soil, a corpse. The result is shrivelled and rigid, but it *keeps*.

The figurative use is where the GRE lives: a **desiccated** prose style, a **desiccated** old don, a **desiccated** marriage — technically intact, emotionally drained of all juice. It's a sneer about lifelessness, not about aridity.

⚠️ **desiccate ≠ desecrate** (DES-uh-krayt). *Desiccate* = dry to a husk; *desecrate* = violate something sacred. A drought desiccates a field; a vandal desecrates a grave.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Desiccate** doesn't dry something **off** — it dries something **out**.

Towels dry you off; a desiccant packet dries the whole box out until nothing living is left in it.

### In sentences
1. Centuries in the sealed tomb had **desiccated** the body until the skin lay on the bone like paper.
2. Forty years of committee work had **desiccated** him — all procedure, no pulse.

### In the wild
Every shoebox, vitamin bottle and beef-jerky bag contains a little sachet marked **DESICCANT — DO NOT EAT**: silica gel, there to desiccate the air inside.

### Where it comes from
Latin **siccus** = *"dry"*, with **de-** as an intensifier: *dry thoroughly*. That intensifying **de-** is why the word means total drying rather than mere dampness removed.

### Also sits in
- **Opposites** · [Fertile vs. barren](groups.md#antonym__fertile-vs-barren) — with *fecund, fruitful, burgeon, replenish, barren, fallow, futile*

# **diatribe** *(n.)* — **DEYE-uh-treyeb**

*Three syllables, never "dye-uh-TRIB."*

### Means
A **long, bitter, abusive attack in words** — spoken or written — against a person, group, or idea.

Two things define it: **length** and **venom**. A sharp insult isn't a diatribe; a diatribe goes on, and on, past the point where anyone is still persuaded. The word is almost always used *against* the speaker — calling something a diatribe concedes that it was forceful but denies that it was reasoned. A critic delivers a *critique*; a critic who has lost the room delivers a *diatribe*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Die-a-tribe** — it drags on so long the whole tribe would rather die than hear the end.

The sound hook stores the two things that matter: it's an attack, and it's punishingly long.

### In sentences
1. His resignation letter began as a courtesy and ended as a six-page **diatribe** against the board.
2. Ask him about parking and you'll get a twenty-minute **diatribe** about the council.

### In the wild
Standard press shorthand: a politician "launched into a **diatribe** against the media." The verb *launch into* is the collocation to memorise — it's how the word almost always arrives.

### Where it comes from
Greek **dia-** (*through*) + **tribein** (*to rub, wear away*) — a discourse that **rubbed away the hours**. In Greek it meant a learned lecture; English kept the length and dropped the learning.

Same *tribein* gives **tribology**, the science of friction and wear.

# **incredulous** *(adj.)* — **in-KRED-yuu-luhs**

*Often said in-KREJ-uh-luhs in fast speech; both pass.*

### Means
Unwilling or unable to believe something — and **showing it**.

This word lives on a face, not on a fact. An *incredible* story provokes an **incredulous** listener: raised eyebrows, flat "you're joking," the pause before the laugh. It's stronger than *skeptical* (which is a considered stance, often habitual) and lighter than *dismissive* — the incredulous person hasn't decided you're lying, they just can't get the claim to go down. Note the tell: things are almost never incredulous; **people, looks, tones and stares are**.

⚠️ **incredulous ≠ credulous.**
- **credulous** = believes *too easily* — gullible, a soft touch
- **incredulous** = won't believe *at all* — the raised eyebrow

The conman needs a **credulous** mark; he fears an **incredulous** one.

### Trick to lock it in
> The story is incred**ible**; the face is incred**ulous**.

*-ible* endings describe the thing being judged, while *-ulous* joins *jealous, zealous, querulous* as a word for how a **person** is reacting.

### In sentences
1. The witness's account was met with **incredulous** silence from the bench.
2. He gave me this **incredulous** look, like — you drove four hours for a sandwich?

### In the wild
Caravaggio's famous painting **"The Incredulity of Saint Thomas"** — Doubting Thomas, refusing to believe until he can put his finger in the wound. That's the word in one image: not deceit, just a man who cannot accept what he's being told.

### Where it comes from
Latin **crēdere** = *"to believe"* + *in-* (not). One of the highest-yield roots on the exam: **credible** (believable), **credence** (belief granted), **credentials** (things that make you believable), **creed** and **credo** (what one believes), **credit** (belief in your ability to pay), **credulous** (believing too readily).

# **ingenuous** *(adj.)* — **in-JEN-yoo-uhs**

*Four syllables, stress on JEN — not "in-JEE-nee-uhs," which is *ingenious*.*

### Means
Innocently frank; open and trusting because you have never learned to be otherwise.

An ingenuous person says the true thing without calculating what it costs them. *Candid* is a choice; *ingenuous* is a condition — it comes from inexperience, not courage. That's why the word runs warm but slightly condescending: the speaker is charmed and also faintly aware that this person will be eaten alive. Push it further and you're at *naive*; pull it back toward polish and you lose it entirely.

**ingenuous ≠ ingenious.** One letter, opposite compliments: **ingenuous** = *guilelessly open*; **ingenious** = *cleverly inventive*. Calling a con artist's scheme "ingenuous" says he's a sweet innocent, which is precisely what he isn't.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside in-**genu**-ous is **genuine** — no filter, no angle.

And the sneaky cousin **dis**ingenuous is someone *faking* that openness, which fixes both words at once.

### In sentences
1. Her ingenuous account of the meeting, offered without a trace of self-protection, damaged her far more than a lie would have.
2. He asked what her salary was — not rude, just ingenuous enough to think it was a normal question.

### In the wild
The **ingénue** — the stock innocent-young-woman role in theatre and film (Audrey Hepburn built a career on it). Same root, same quality: wide-eyed and unguarded.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ingenuus** = *"native-born, freeborn"* — from *in-* (in) + *gignere* (to beget). A freeborn Roman had no need to flatter or dissemble, so "freeborn" slid into "frank, unguarded."

The *gen-* root is everywhere: **genuine, genus, generate, congenital, indigenous** — and yes, **ingenious** too (Latin *ingenium*, inborn talent). Same family, diverged completely: one inherits honesty, the other inherits brains.
