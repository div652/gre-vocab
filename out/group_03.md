# **abate** *(v.)* — **uh-BAYT**

### Means
To **decrease in force or intensity** — and, transitively, to reduce something to a lower level (*abate a nuisance*, *abate the rent*).

What abates is a **pressure**, not a quantity: storms, pain, fever, noise, panic, criticism, enthusiasm. You wouldn't say a crowd abated, but the crowd's fury would. It also carries a sense of *relief* — the thing was pressing on you and has eased off. Register is a notch above plain speech: a newsreader says the floodwaters abated; your friend says the rain let up. Watch for **unabated**, which is the form you'll meet most often on the test — "the shelling continued unabated" = no let-up at all.

⚠️ **abate ≠ abet.**
- **abate** = die down, lessen
- **abet** = *help someone do wrong* (as in **aid and abet**)

One letter, opposite worlds: a storm abates; an accomplice abets.

### Trick to lock it in
> Wait with **bated** breath — *bated* is just **abated** with its head cut off: breath beaten down to nothing.

Bated breath isn't *less* breath, it's breath held under pressure — exactly the kind of force-easing that abate describes.

### In sentences
1. Only when the monsoon **abated** did the villagers venture back to inspect what the water had taken.
2. Give the ibuprofen twenty minutes — the throbbing should **abate** enough for you to sleep.

### In the wild
**Tax abatement** is everyday property-market vocabulary: a city knocks down your property tax for a few years to lure you into building there. Same word, same idea — the burden beaten down.

### Where it comes from
Old French **abattre** = *"to beat down"* (Latin **battuere**, *to beat*). The storm is literally beaten flat.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **battle**, **batter**, **combat**, **debate** (beat an argument about), **rebate** (a beating-back of the price), and **abattoir** — the slaughterhouse, where things are beaten down.

# **abjure** *(v.)* — **ab-JOOR**

### Means
To **renounce, repudiate, or give up** something formally and solemnly — a belief, a claim, a loyalty, a habit — usually **on the record and under oath**.

You can *quit* a habit privately. To **abjure** it is to disown it out loud, in a way that binds you: a heretic abjures his heresy, a new citizen abjures foreign allegiance, a reformed man abjures drink. The word carries the weight of ceremony, and often a whiff of pressure — someone made you say it. Compare **renounce** (neutral, can be quiet) and **recant** (specifically taking back something you *said*); abjure covers what you *believed in* or *were bound to*.

⚠️ **abjure ≠ adjure.**
- **abjure** = swear *away* — you reject it
- **adjure** = swear *at* someone — you earnestly urge or command them

*She abjured her old party; then she adjured her friends to leave it too.*

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ab-** (away, as in *absent*, *abduct*) + **-jure** (swear, as in *perjure*) — you don't just drop it, you **swear it away**, out loud.

The *-jure* is what makes it public and binding: abjuring is a ceremony, not a quiet change of heart.

### In sentences
1. The treaty obliged every signatory to **abjure** the use of force in settling territorial claims.
2. He's **abjured** caffeine about four times now, and the announcements are getting less convincing.

### In the wild
Galileo, hauled before the Inquisition in 1633, was forced to **abjure** his belief that the Earth moves around the Sun — kneeling, reading the renunciation aloud, in the classic textbook scene.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ab-** (*away*) + **jūrāre** (*to swear*), from **jūs, jūris** = *law, oath*.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **perjure** (swear falsely), **jury** (sworn panel), **jurisdiction** (where your law speaks), **jurisprudence**, **conjure** (originally: swear together), and **abjure**'s twin **adjure**.

# **anomalous** *(adj.)* — **uh-NOM-uh-luhs**

*Stress shifts from the noun: **an**omaly is AN-uh-muh-lee, but **anom**alous is uh-NOM-uh-luhs.*

### Means
Departing from what is standard, expected, or predicted by the pattern — irregular, off-model.

This is the lab-coat word for *odd*. **Weird** is a reaction, **aberrant** hints that something has gone morally or biologically wrong, but **anomalous** passes no judgement at all: it simply says *this case does not fit the model*. It also quietly assumes the model is sound and the case is the outlier — which is why a scientist calls a reading anomalous before deciding whether it's an error, a fluke, or a discovery.

⚠️ **anomalous ≠ analogous.**
- **anomalous** = doesn't fit the pattern
- **analogous** = parallel to something else, comparable

An anomalous result breaks the trend; an analogous case matches a different one.

### Trick to lock it in
> **a-NOM-alous** — hear the stressed **NOM** as *norm* with the r rubbed out: the thing that won't sit on the norm.

It's a pure sound hook rather than the true root, but it locks in the key idea — the word labels something as off-pattern without calling it wrong.

### In sentences
1. The committee flagged an **anomalous** spike in third-quarter revenue: no launch, no campaign, just a number that refused to fit the curve.
2. Look, one good week is **anomalous** — don't rebuild the whole strategy around it.

### In the wild
In *The Matrix Reloaded*, the Architect calls Neo **\"the anomaly\"** — the one variable the system's model can't account for. That's the word exactly: not evil, just unpredicted.

### Where it comes from
Greek **an-** (*not*) + **homalos** (*even, level*), from **homos** = *"same."* Literally *not level with the rest*.

That root gives you **homogeneous**, **homonym**, **homologous** — all "same-ness" words.

🚫 **Not from *nomos* (law)**, despite the look of *autonomous* and *economy*. Anomalous breaks a *pattern*, not a rule.

# **antipathy** *(n.)* — **an-TIP-uh-thee**

*Stress lands on TIP, not the anti- — it is not "AN-tee-path-ee".*

### Means
A deep, settled **dislike** or aversion — for a person, a group, an idea, a food.

Antipathy is **cold, not hot**. Hatred burns and wants something to happen; antipathy just wants distance. It's also often **unaccountable** — you feel it before you can justify it, which is why the word suits instinctive recoil ("a lifelong antipathy to crowds") as much as considered opposition. And it's **standing, not momentary**: a flash of irritation is never antipathy. Formal register — journalists write of *public antipathy toward* a policy where speech would just say *people hate it*.

⚠️ **antipathy ≠ apathy.**
- **apathy** = *no* feeling — you don't care
- **antipathy** = feeling *against* — you care, and you're against it

The apathetic voter stays home shrugging; the antipathetic one stays home in disgust.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Anti-path** — you'd take a different path to avoid them.

That captures the coldness: antipathy doesn't attack, it steers clear.

### In sentences
1. Her antipathy toward the new director was never spoken aloud, but it shaped every appointment she made.
2. I've got a serious antipathy to group chats — nothing personal, I just can't.

### Where it comes from
Greek **anti-** (*against*) + **pathos** (*feeling, suffering*) — literally *feeling-against*.

That second root is a GRE workhorse: **sympathy** (feeling *with*), **empathy** (feeling *into*), **apathy** (*without* feeling), **pathos**, **pathetic**, **pathology** (the study of what a body suffers).

# **arcane** *(adj.)* — **ar-KAYN**

### Means
Known or understood by **very few** — obscure, mysterious, the property of specialists or initiates.

The word carries a whiff of *restricted access*: arcane material isn't merely hard, it's **walled off**, whether by secrecy (rites, spells) or by sheer impenetrable specialism (the arcane provisions of the tax code). Two flavours, then — solemn and magical, or dry and faintly exasperated. When a journalist calls a rule arcane, they mean *needlessly* obscure, and someone should have simplified it years ago.

⚠️ **arcane ≠ archaic.**
- **arcane** = *hidden* — few people understand it
- **archaic** = *old* — nobody uses it any more

A medieval spelling is archaic. A working rule nobody can decipher is arcane.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Arcane** keeps its secrets in an **ark** — sealed, and only the initiated hold the key.

The point isn't difficulty but *access*: arcane knowledge is shut away, and you're on the outside of the box.

### In sentences
1. Behind the tapestry lay a chamber of **arcane** symbols, meaningless to anyone outside the order.
2. Getting reimbursed involves some **arcane** three-form process only Priya in accounts actually understands.

### In the wild
Netflix's **Arcane** (the *League of Legends* series) — the title is doing real work: the plot turns on hextech, a hidden magic only a handful of people can wield or comprehend.

### Where it comes from
Latin **arca** = *"chest, strongbox"* → **arcānus** = *"shut in a box, secret."* Same **arca** gives English **ark** — Noah's, and the Ark of the Covenant, which only priests could approach.

Behind it sits **arcēre**, *"to shut in, keep away"* — which also yields **coerce** (*co-* + *arcēre*, shut someone in) and **exercise** (*ex-* + *arcēre*, drive out).

# **arduous** *(adj.)* — **AR-joo-uhs**

*British speakers often say AR-dyoo-uhs.*

### Means
Demanding severe, **sustained** effort; laborious and wearying.

*Difficult* is about complexity — a hard puzzle is difficult in an instant. *Arduous* is about **exertion stretched over time**: the climb, the training, the three-year lawsuit. It describes tasks, journeys and processes, never people, and it carries a faint note of respect — an arduous thing is worth finishing, which is why it pairs so naturally with *journey*, *task*, *negotiations*, *ascent*.

**arduous ≠ ardent.** *Arduous* = exhausting to do (Latin *arduus*, steep). *Ardent* = burning with passion (Latin *ardēre*, to burn). An **ardent** fan endures an **arduous** queue.

### Trick to lock it in
> Put an H on the front: **(h)arduous** — *hard* + drags on *continuous*ly.

The hidden *hard* gives you the difficulty and the *-uous* tail gives you the part that matters — it goes on and on.

### In sentences
1. The restoration of the fresco proved an **arduous** undertaking, occupying two conservators for the better part of a decade.
2. Four buses and a ferry to get there — honestly, the trip was more **arduous** than the wedding.

### In the wild
News writing has practically welded the phrase together: refugees, expedition teams and hostages all make **"an arduous journey."** If you've read a headline, you've met the word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **arduus** = *"steep, high."* The original arduous thing was a hillside — hard because it's uphill, and uphill all the way. That steepness is still doing the work in the modern sense.

# **artless** *(adj.)* — **AHRT-luhs**

### Means
**Without guile** — natural, unaffected, saying exactly what you mean because it never occurred to you to do otherwise. A secondary sense: **unskilled, crude** (a genuinely artless drawing).

The GRE almost always wants the first sense, and the trick is that the *art* being denied is **artifice** — cunning, calculation, technique — not painting. So an artless remark isn't clumsy, it's uncalculated. Tone is usually warm but faintly condescending: you'd call a child's honesty artless, and a diplomat's too, but only to say she'd never survive the job. Compare *naive*, which says the person is wrong about the world; *artless* only says they aren't working an angle.

⚠️ **artless ≠ the opposite of artistic. artful ≠ artistic either.**
- **artless** = *guileless*, no cunning
- **artful** = *crafty*, full of cunning

Neither word is about art galleries.

### Trick to lock it in
> Artless: **less art, more heart.**

The *art* being subtracted is artifice — scheming, angling, technique — so what's left is unguarded sincerity.

### In sentences
1. Her testimony was so **artless** that the jury believed her before the lawyers had finished objecting.
2. He asked, with **artless** curiosity, how much everyone at the table earned.

### In the wild
The **Artful Dodger** in *Oliver Twist* is a pickpocket, not a painter — proof that this *art* means cunning. Artless is exactly what the Dodger is not.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ars, artis** = *"skill, craft"* — which slid easily into *"trickery."* That gives **artifice**, **artificial**, **artisan**, and — via *in- + ars* — **inert**, literally *"without skill,"* now "doing nothing at all."

# **ascetic** *(adj. / n.)* — **uh-SET-ik**

*Not *acetic* (uh-SEE-tik), which is vinegar.*

### Means
**Adj.:** severely self-denying — no luxury, no comfort, no indulgence. **Noun:** a person who lives that way, usually for religious or moral discipline.

The defining thing is that it's **chosen**. A poor man is deprived; an ascetic has *refused*. That's why the word carries either quiet admiration (monk, saint, serious artist) or a faint charge of extremism, depending on who's using it — and why "he lives an ascetic life" says something about his will, not his bank balance.

It stretches to style, too: an **ascetic** room, an ascetic prose style — stripped bare on purpose.

⚠️ **ascetic ≠ aesthetic.** *Ascetic* (uh-SET-ik) = stripped of pleasure. *Aesthetic* (es-THET-ik) = concerned with beauty. One letter, opposite instincts — and the GRE knows it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Asc-etic** rhymes with **athl-etic** — both are in training; the ascetic just trains at *going without*.

It locks in the key nuance: this is disciplined self-denial by choice, not poverty by accident.

### In sentences
1. The philosopher retreated to an **ascetic** cell furnished with a cot, a lamp, and nothing else.
2. Since January he's been on this **ascetic** kick — no sugar, no drinking, up at five.

### In the wild
Before he became the Buddha, Siddhartha spent six years as an **ascetic** — starving himself down to the ribs — then rejected it for the Middle Way. The whole story turns on the word.

### Where it comes from
Greek **askēsis** = *"training, exercise"* — the word for an athlete's regimen. Early Christian monks borrowed it for spiritual training, and the gym-work sense hardened into fasting and self-denial. So an ascetic is literally someone **in training**.

# **assuage** *(v.)* — **uh-SWAYJ**

*Two syllables, not three — the -uage is "swayj", never "swahzh" or "soo-ahj".*

### Means
To **make a painful feeling less intense** — grief, guilt, fear, anger, hunger, thirst.

The object is almost always an *emotion*, not a person: you assuage someone's **fears**, but you mollify the angry **man**. And it's partial relief, not a cure — assuaged guilt is quieter guilt, still there. The register is formal and slightly consoling, the language of eulogies, diplomacy and financial press.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Assuage** near-rhymes with **a stage** — grief drops down a stage, it doesn't leave the stage.

The word promises reduction, never removal.

### In sentences
1. Nothing the priest said could **assuage** her grief; it only made the silence afterwards easier to bear.
2. He bought her flowers to **assuage** his guilt about forgetting the anniversary.

### In the wild
Business-page boilerplate: *"The central bank moved to **assuage** investor fears."* Whenever an institution is doing damage control, this is the verb the headline reaches for — always with *fears*, *concerns*, or *doubts*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **suavis** (*sweet*) — literally **to sweeten** something bitter. That root gives you **suave** (smooth, sweet-mannered) and, via the cousin verb *suadēre* "to urge sweetly," **persuade**, **dissuade** and **suasion**. English **sweet** is the same ancient root.

# **betray** *(v.)* — **bih-TRAY**

### Means
**1.** To hand over or be disloyal to someone who trusted you — a friend, a country, a cause, a confidence.
**2.** To **reveal unintentionally** something you were trying to keep hidden.

Sense 2 is the one the GRE cares about, and it turns on *involuntariness*. You don't betray a feeling on purpose — the subject is usually a small traitorous detail (a tremor, a flush, a pause) that leaks the truth its owner was working to conceal. "His smile **showed** his nerves" is odd; "his smile **betrayed** his nerves" is exact, because it implies the nerves were meant to stay private. Even in this quiet sense the word keeps a whiff of treachery: something has gone over to the other side.

⚠️ **betray ≠ belie.** In the same sentence frame they point opposite ways:
- Her calm face **betrayed** her panic → the panic *got out*.
- Her calm face **belied** her panic → the panic stayed *hidden*; the face lied about it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Be-TRAY** — you serve your friend up to the enemy on a **tray**, and if your hands shake, the tray hands over your secret too.

Both senses are one act — something gets *handed over* against someone's interest, whether it's a person or a piece of the truth.

### In sentences
1. A faint tremor in his voice betrayed the confidence his words were claiming.
2. I told her I didn't mind, but my face betrayed me instantly.

### In the wild
Poker's whole vocabulary runs on this sense: a **tell** is any twitch or hesitation that *betrays* your hand — you never mean to show it, which is exactly the point.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tradere** = *trans* ("across") + *dare* ("give") — **to hand over**.

The same root splits into opposite moralities: **tradition** (knowledge handed *down* to you) and **treason / traitor / betray** (a person handed *over* to the enemy). Also **extradite** — handed across a border.

# **bucolic** *(adj.)* — **byoo-KOL-ik**

*Not BUCK-uh-lik — the first syllable is byoo, as in beautiful.*

### Means
Of the countryside, and **charmingly** so: green fields, grazing animals, unhurried quiet.

The word never describes farm work as work. *Agrarian* is economic, *rustic* is rough and a bit unpolished, *rural* is just a census category — **bucolic** is the countryside seen from outside and admired. It carries a faint whiff of the picturesque: someone calling a village bucolic is enjoying the view, not mucking out the barn. Occasionally used drily, exactly because that gap between the pretty picture and the actual mud is easy to poke at.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Bu**colic, **bo**vine — same cow. Bucolic is cow-country as *scenery*.

The *bou-* is Greek for ox, so the word literally means cowherd-ish — and cows in a field are the postcard, not the labour.

### In sentences
1. The novel opens on a **bucolic** valley of hedgerows and slow rivers, all of which the railway will have swallowed by chapter nine.
2. Two days of **bucolic** bliss and then the Wi-Fi died and we drove straight back to the city.

### In the wild
Estate agents and travel brochures live on this word — "bucolic charm", "bucolic setting" is standard copy for any cottage with a field near it. If you've read a countryside listing, you've read *bucolic*.

### Where it comes from
Greek **boûs** = *ox, cow* → **boukolos** = *cowherd*. The same **bou-** pays off elsewhere: **bovine** (cow-like), **bulimia** (*bous* + *limos*, "ox-hunger"), and **butter** (*bou-tyron*, cow-cheese).

# **burgeon** *(v.)* — **BER-juhn**

*Soft g, exactly like surgeon — never a hard "BUR-gun".*

### Means
To sprout, bud, and then **grow or expand rapidly and healthily**.

It carries two things ordinary *grow* doesn't: an **organic, living** flavour, and a sense of **promising beginnings** — something small getting visibly bigger, fast. A tumour doesn't burgeon; a career, a city, a music scene does. That's why the word is almost always positive, and why you meet it mostly as the adjective **burgeoning** in front of a noun: *a burgeoning demand, a burgeoning talent, a burgeoning middle class*. Note also that it needs no object — things burgeon on their own; you don't burgeon them.

### Trick to lock it in
> **b-URGE-ON** — growth being *urged on*.

The hidden word gives you the nuance exactly: not steady growth, but growth with momentum behind it.

### In sentences
1. By the 1890s the port had **burgeoned** into the busiest on the coast, its warehouses spilling out past the old walls.
2. Her side hustle **burgeoned** into an actual business before she'd even quit her day job.

### In the wild
Business and tech journalism runs on it — "a **burgeoning** startup scene", "**burgeoning** demand for chips". If you've read a market report, you've read this word.

### Where it comes from
Old French **burjon** = *"a bud, a shoot."* The literal sense is a plant putting out buds — which is why the word still smells of springtime and early promise rather than mere increase.

🚫 **Not related to *bourgeois*.** That's from Germanic *burg*, "town" — *borough, burgher, Edinburgh*. Similar look, unrelated root.

# **cacophonous** *(adj.)* — **kuh-KOF-uh-nuhs**

*Noun form: cacophony, kuh-KOF-uh-nee. Stress never falls on the first syllable.*

### Means
Harsh, jarring, discordant — a mixture of sounds that clash instead of blending.

Loudness alone isn't enough. A jet engine is deafening but not cacophonous; it's one sound. Cacophony needs **many sounds fighting each other** — horns, shouting, drills, a dozen conversations. And it's a verdict, not a measurement: the speaker is telling you the noise is *ugly*. It transfers easily to non-sound clashes too — a cacophonous colour scheme, a cacophonous chorus of opinions on a policy — always with that sense of too many things at once, none of them agreeing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Caca**-phonous — it *sounds* like **caca**.

The nursery word for filth is hiding in plain sight at the front, and it's no accident — that *caco-* really does mean "bad", so cacophonous is literally bad-sounding, not merely loud.

### In sentences
1. The square at dusk was **cacophonous** — vendors bellowing, mopeds, and the call to prayer all colliding over one another.
2. Six people on the call unmuted at once and it turned into a **cacophonous** mess.

### In the wild
The orchestra tuning up before the conductor walks on — thirty instruments sawing away in different keys. That two-minute racket is the textbook cacophony, and the silence that follows it is the point.

### Where it comes from
Greek **kakos** = *"bad"* + **phōnē** = *"sound, voice."* Bad-sound.

Both halves pay off elsewhere. *Phōnē* gives you **symphony** (sounds *together*), **euphony** (*good* sound — the exact antonym), **phonetics**, **telephone**. And *kakos* gives you **kakistocracy**, government by the *worst* people — a word worth knowing on its own.

# **canonize** *(v.)* — **KAN-uh-nyze**

*British spelling: canonise.*

### Means
**1.** For the Church to formally declare a dead person a **saint**. **2.** More broadly, to **enshrine** something as officially great, authoritative, beyond questioning — admit a book, film or figure into "the canon."

This is always an act of *institutional power*, not personal enthusiasm. You can adore a novel; only the syllabus, the academy, the Church can canonize it. And the effect is permanence — canonization freezes a reputation, puts it past debate. Because of that finality, the word turns dryly ironic the moment the thing being enshrined doesn't deserve it: *a mediocre album canonized by critics who were there at the time.*

⚠️ **canon ≠ cannon.** A **canon** is a rule or an official list (one *n*); a **cannon** fires cannonballs (two *n*s). Nothing is ever "cannonized."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Canonize = put on the CANON — the official list you're no longer allowed to argue with.**

The word is hiding in plain sight: canonizing isn't praising something, it's *filing* it somewhere permanent by authority.

### In sentences
1. Pope Francis **canonized** her in 2016, nineteen years after her death and two verified miracles later.
2. Give it ten years and they'll have **canonized** that show — you won't be allowed to say it was boring.

### In the wild
Every fandom argument about what "counts" — is the *Star Wars* Expanded Universe **canon**? — is a fight over what's been canonized. Same word, same idea: an authority decides what's officially real and what gets thrown out.

### Where it comes from
Greek **kanōn** = a *straight measuring rod* — hence a rule, a standard. What measures up gets on the list.

That rod runs through **canon** (the approved body of scripture or works), **canonical** (authoritative, standard), **canon law**, and **canonize** itself.

# **censure** *(v. / n.)* — **SEN-sher**

*Second syllable is "sher," not "sure" — it does not rhyme with "ensure."*

### Means
**Verb:** to condemn someone **formally and publicly** for misconduct. **Noun:** that condemnation itself.

Censure is criticism with **institutional standing behind it** — a vote, a ruling, a signed statement from a body entitled to pass judgement. Your friend can criticise you; only a board, a court, a legislature or a professional association can censure you. And it stops at words: a censured official keeps the job and the salary but wears the mark permanently. That's the scale — heavier than *rebuke*, more public than *reprimand*, one step short of *expel*.

⚠️ **censure ≠ censor.**
- **censure** = *condemn the person*
- **censor** = *delete the message*

A senator can be censured. A senator's speech can be censored.

### Trick to lock it in
> **You get censured *by* a body, never by a person — it takes a vote.**

If nobody had to raise a hand for it, it's just criticism.

### In sentences
1. The committee voted to **censure** the judge for accepting undisclosed gifts, though it stopped short of demanding his resignation.
2. He got off with a **censure** — a strongly worded letter in his file and absolutely nothing else.

### In the wild
The word surfaces in the news almost only in one setting: the US Congress voting to **censure** one of its own — a formal slap on the record that carries no fine, no removal, just the permanent black mark.

### Where it comes from
Latin **censēre** = *"to assess, to judge."* One root, four GRE words: **census** (counting), **censor** (suppressing), **censure** (condemning), **censorious** (habitually fault-finding).

# **chicanery** *(n.)* — **shi-KAY-nuh-ree**

*The ch is a sh, as in French — never "CHICK-uh-nery."*

### Means
**Trickery** — specifically the clever, fiddly, technically-legal kind: deception through loopholes, quibbles, procedural sleight-of-hand and fine print.

Scale matters. A mugging isn't chicanery; a contract clause buried on page 40 is. It implies **cunning rather than force**, and usually a professional setting where the rules themselves are the weapon — lawyers, politicians, accountants, election officials. The word carries a dry, faintly amused contempt: you say it about someone who *won*, but disgracefully.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Chicanery** starts like **chic** — it's trickery in a good suit.

Nothing crude or violent about it: the chicaner wins by being slick, technical and hard to pin down.

### In sentences
1. The judge dismissed the filing as procedural **chicanery** designed to run out the clock rather than to argue the case.
2. Three hidden fees and an auto-renewal? That's not a deal, that's **chicanery**.

### In the wild
In Formula 1, a **chicane** is that tight artificial S-bend bolted onto a straight to stop cars going flat out — same word, and it's literally a swerve to get around something. Chicanery is a chicane through the rules.

### Where it comes from
French **chicaner** = *"to quibble, to wrangle over legal niceties."* The legal flavour never washed out — that's why chicanery still smells of courtrooms and small print rather than street crime.

# **coalesce** *(v.)* — **koh-uh-LES**

*Three syllables — not "COAL-ess". The "coal" is co- + al-.*

### Means
To come together and **merge into one whole** — droplets, factions, ideas, a plan.

The key is that the parts stop being separate. A crowd *gathers* and stays a crowd of individuals; things that **coalesce** fuse, and you can no longer pick out what went in. It's also gradual and usually unforced — nobody coalesces things, they coalesce. That's why it fits mercury beads, rival factions, and half-formed thoughts equally well, and why "the committee coalesced the reports" sounds wrong: use *merged* or *combined* when there's an agent doing the joining.

⚠️ **coalesce ≠ convalesce.**
- **coalesce** = separate things *grow together*
- **convalesce** = a sick person *gets better* (recuperate)

Factions coalesce; patients convalesce.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-esce = gradually becoming**: adol*esce*nt, conval*esce*, coal*esce*. Never a snap — always a process.

That Latin *-esc-* ending marks a slow becoming, which is exactly the nuance: coalescing is drift and fusion over time, not an instant merger.

### In sentences
1. Over three decades the scattered guilds **coalesced** into a single national union.
2. Give me a day — the idea's still bits and pieces, it hasn't **coalesced** yet.

### In the wild
Election-night newsreading: when a primary field thins, commentators say the party is **coalescing around** a frontrunner — the rivals' support pooling into one bloc.

### Where it comes from
Latin **com-** ("together") + **alescere** ("to grow up"), from **alere**, *"to nourish."*

Same root: **coalition** (groups grown together), **adolescent** (still growing up), **alimony** (money to nourish), **aliment**. Coalition is the giveaway — a coalition is literally what coalescing produces.

# **cogent** *(adj.)* — **KOH-juhnt**

*Soft *g*, as in *gem* — not "COG-ent".*

### Means
Of an argument, case, or explanation: **clear, logical, and forceful enough to compel agreement.**

The praise lands on the *reasoning*, not the speaker. **Eloquent** is about style, **persuasive** is about effect — a con man can be both. **Cogent** says the logic itself holds: tight, well-evidenced, hard to answer. It's cool, formal praise, the kind a judge, examiner, or peer reviewer gives — and its negative is quietly brutal, because "no cogent explanation was offered" means the reasoning was not merely unconvincing but structurally empty.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A cogent argument has *cogs* — it's a machine that drives you to the conclusion.**

The gears mesh: each step turns the next, so you're moved along by the mechanism rather than talked into it.

### In sentences
1. The dissent was the more **cogent** opinion: fifteen pages, no rhetoric, every claim carrying its own evidence.
2. He talked for ten minutes and never made one **cogent** point.

### In the wild
Standard courtroom and peer-review currency — judgments regularly say a party \"advanced no **cogent** reason,\" which is a polite way of saying the argument had nothing under it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **cogere** = *"to drive together, to compel"* (**co-** *together* + **agere** *to drive*). A cogent argument literally **forces** you along — the same *agere* behind **agent, agenda, agile, act, exact, agitate**.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *cognition*.** That's *cognoscere*, "to know" — *recognise, cognizant, incognito*. Cogent isn't about being knowledgeable; it's about being irresistible.

# **compelling** *(adj.)* — **kuhm-PEL-ing**

### Means
**Adj.:** so convincing, or so gripping, that you can't resist it — either an argument you can't refute or a story you can't put down.

Two lanes, same force. **Compelling evidence** leaves no room to disagree; **a compelling film** leaves no room to look away. Either way the word implies *pressure*: a merely *persuasive* argument invites you to agree, a **compelling** one gives you no honest alternative. Strong praise, and slightly more objective-sounding than *fascinating* — it says the power is in the thing, not in your taste.

There is also a plainer legal/formal use — **a compelling reason** = one that genuinely *forces* the decision (as in "compelling state interest").

⚠️ **compelling ≠ compulsive.**
- **compelling** = irresistible to *others* (a compelling case)
- **compulsive** = driven by an urge *within* (a compulsive liar)

A compulsive liar can't stop; a compelling liar can't be doubted.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Compelling** has **compel** sitting inside it — it doesn't ask you, it drives you.

If you can shrug it off, it wasn't compelling.

### In sentences
1. The prosecution assembled a **compelling** case: three witnesses, a dated receipt, and CCTV footage timed to the minute.
2. I meant to watch one episode, but it was so **compelling** I lost the whole night.

### In the wild
It's the default word on every book jacket and streaming blurb — *"a compelling read," "compelling television."* Critics reach for it precisely because it praises the grip, not just the quality.

### Where it comes from
Latin **com-** (intensive) + **pellere** = *"to drive, to push."* Literally *to drive someone into it.*

That root drives a whole GRE family: **propel** (drive forward), **expel** (drive out), **repel** (drive back), **dispel** (drive apart — *dispel a rumour*), **impel** (drive on), **compulsion**, **pulse**.

# **contend** *(v.)* — **kuhn-TEND**

### Means
Two senses, both about **strain against opposition**:

**1. To assert in the face of disagreement.** Not merely to *say* — to maintain a position that someone else is pushing back on. This is why academic and journalistic prose reaches for it: "critics **contend** that the study was underpowered" flags the claim as *contested* and quietly distances the writer from it. Swap in "argue" and you lose the hint of a live dispute; swap in "claim" and you add a sneer that *contend* doesn't have.

**2. To struggle or compete.** *Contend with* a problem = grapple with it, difficulty implied but not defeat. *Contend for* a prize = be a serious rival for it, not a hopeful bystander.

### Trick to lock it in
> **contend *that*** = argue · **contend *with*** = struggle · **contend *for*** = compete.

The preposition, not the verb, tells you which of the three senses is running.

### In sentences
1. Several economists **contend** that the shortage was engineered, not accidental.
2. Between the strike and the rain, we had enough to **contend with** before the van broke down.

### In the wild
Marlon Brando in *On the Waterfront*: **"I coulda been a contender."** A contender is one who *contends for* the title — a real threat, not a filler on the card.

### Where it comes from
Latin **contendere** = *con-* ("together") + **tendere** ("to stretch") — to strain against. That's the through-line: an argument, a rival, and a hardship are all things you pull taut against.

*tendere* is a GRE workhorse: **tension, tendon, extend, intend, pretend, portend, distend** — and **contentious**, which is just *contend* turned into a personality.

# **copious** *(adj.)* — **KOH-pee-uhs**

### Means
Present in **large quantity** — plentiful, abundant, more than enough.

It leans toward things that are **produced, poured out, or consumed**: copious notes, copious tears, copious rainfall, copious amounts of coffee. You'd rarely call a mountain range copious, because copious describes *output and supply*, not sheer size. Tone is mildly formal but common in speech, and the flavour is generous rather than excessive — say **profuse** for gushing, **prodigal** for wasteful. Copious just means the well kept giving.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Copious** notes fill **copies** and **copies** of pages — same word underneath.

*Copy* and *copious* are literally the same Latin root, so copious means abundance that keeps being turned out, not a single big pile.

### In sentences
1. The witness gave **copious** testimony, filling three days of transcript with detail no one had asked for.
2. He took **copious** notes in the meeting and still had no idea what was decided.

### In the wild
The **Cornucopia** in *The Hunger Games* — the giant golden horn spilling out weapons and supplies at the start of the Games. Same root, and it's an image of overflow.

### Where it comes from
Latin **copia** = *"abundance, plenty"* — itself *co-* + *ops* ("wealth, resources," the root behind **opulent**).

The surprise: **copy** comes from here too. A medieval *copia* was an abundance of a text — make more of it, and you've made a copy. **Cornucopia** = *cornu* (horn) + *copia* = the horn of plenty.

# **cosmopolitan** *(adj. / n.)* — **koz-muh-POL-ih-tuhn**

### Means
**Adj.:** at home in many countries and cultures; free of local or national narrowness. Of a *place*, it means the mix — people, food, languages from everywhere. Of a *person*, it means the temperament: unfazed by difference.

**Noun:** someone who is that.

The praise is not for the passport stamps but for the absence of provincial reflex — a cosmopolitan doesn't find foreignness startling. **Worldly** hints at experience of vice; **urbane** is about polish and manners; **cosmopolitan** is about *range*. Its true opposites are **parochial** and **provincial**, and GRE passages love that axis.

⚠️ **cosmopolitan ≠ metropolitan.**
- **metropolitan** = of a big city — a matter of **size** (metropolitan area, metropolitan police)
- **cosmopolitan** = of many cultures — a matter of **mix**

A town can be metropolitan and still deeply parochial.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cosmopolitan** sounds like *cosmo-politician* — someone who can work any room, in any country.

The word rewards social ease among strangers, not miles travelled.

### In sentences
1. Trieste under the Habsburgs was a genuinely **cosmopolitan** port, where Italian, Slovene and German were all spoken on the same street.
2. She's the most **cosmopolitan** person I know — three languages, five cities, and she still can't work the London Tube.

### In the wild
**Cosmopolitan** the magazine, and the **Cosmo** cocktail *Sex and the City* made famous — both sell exactly one fantasy: the worldly, city-slick woman who belongs anywhere.

### Where it comes from
Greek **kosmos** (*world, order*) + **politēs** (*citizen*) — literally **"citizen of the universe."** Diogenes the Cynic, asked where he was from, is said to have replied *kosmopolitēs*.

Both halves pay off elsewhere: *kosmos* gives **cosmos, cosmic, microcosm** — and **cosmetic**, because *kosmos* also meant *ordered arrangement*, i.e. adornment. *Polis* gives **politics, police, metropolis, acropolis**.

# **deference** *(n.)* — **DEF-er-uhns**

### Means
Respectful yielding — letting someone else's judgment, wishes, or authority prevail over your own.

The yielding is **voluntary and measured**. Obedience is compelled; **servility** and **obsequiousness** are grovelling; deference is the polite half-step back you take for seniority, rank, or expertise, and it usually reflects well on you. Note the common frame **"in deference to"** — *in deference to her grief, we cancelled the party* — where it means "out of respect for," not "in submission to." Push it too far and it curdles: *deferential* can be a quiet insult, describing someone with no opinions of his own.

⚠️ **deferential ≠ differential.** *Deferential* = respectfully yielding. *Differential* = to do with a difference (a differential rate, a car's differential). One bows; the other subtracts.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Deference** = **defer** + **-ence**: you *defer to* them — hand over the call, with a slight bow rather than a white flag.

The bow marks the respect and the "slight" marks the limit — it's courtesy, not surrender.

### In sentences
1. The junior barrister spoke last, in **deference** to counsel who had argued the case for thirty years.
2. I don't actually like sushi — I picked the place out of **deference** to the birthday girl.

### In the wild
In law, **"Chevron deference"** was the rule that courts should defer to a government agency's reading of a statute — the judges standing back and letting the experts' interpretation stand. The Supreme Court scrapped it in 2024, and every headline was about courts *no longer deferring*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dēferre** = *"to carry down, bring low, submit"* — **de-** (down) + **ferre** (to carry). You carry your own view downward so someone else's can sit on top.

**ferre** is a GRE workhorse: **transfer** (carry across), **refer** (carry back), **confer** (carry together), **infer** (carry in), **offer**, **suffer**, **fertile**.

# **desultory** *(adj.)* — **DESS-uhl-tor-ee**

*Stress the FIRST syllable — not "de-SULT-ory", which is the usual slip.*

### Means
Moving from one thing to another without plan, method, or enthusiasm — **aimless and half-hearted at the same time**.

Two ingredients have to be present. Random alone isn't enough: *desultory* carries a distinct droop of energy, the sense that nobody much cared whether the thing got finished. A *desultory* conversation drifts and dies; *desultory* revision is twenty minutes here, a different chapter there, nothing retained. It's a quietly damning word — the critic's way of saying an effort was made, technically.

**desultory ≠ dilatory.**
- **desultory** = doing it *aimlessly*, hopping about
- **dilatory** = doing it *late*, dragging your feet

A dilatory student hasn't started. A desultory one has started four times.

### Trick to lock it in
> **De-SULT-ory work gets no re-SULT — you leap off before anything lands.**

Both words hide Latin *salire*, "to leap," and the hidden **result** is exactly what desultory effort never produces.

### In sentences
1. The committee's **desultory** inquiry sprawled over three years and concluded nothing.
2. We had a **desultory** chat about the weather and then both pretended our phones had buzzed.

### In the wild
Cricket and football writing leans on it constantly — "a **desultory** afternoon session," "**desultory** passing in midfield" — meaning the players were technically out there and not really playing.

### Where it comes from
Latin **desultor** — the circus trick-rider who **leapt from one galloping horse to another** (*de-* "off" + *salire* "to leap"). Desultory is the mind doing that: never on one horse long enough to ride it anywhere.

*Salire* is a high-yield GRE root: **salient** (leaping out at you), **resilient** (leaping back), **exult**, **insult**, **assail**, **somersault**.

# **diffident** *(adj.)* — **DIF-i-duhnt**

*Stressed on the first syllable, exactly like *confident* — never "dif-FID-ent."*

### Means
Lacking confidence in yourself; hanging back, hesitant to speak up or push your own claim.

The word describes **self-doubt made visible as reserve**. *Shy* is a temperament around people; *modest* is a virtue you can choose; **diffident** is the suspicion that you aren't quite good enough, showing up as a half-raised hand. It's also cooler than *insecure* — no neediness, no drama, just someone quietly underrating themselves. The tone is formal and faintly sympathetic: the speaker usually thinks the diffidence is unwarranted.

⚠️ **diffident ≠ indifferent.**
- **diffident** = doubts *himself*
- **indifferent** = doesn't *care*

A diffident student cares enormously and says nothing; an indifferent one doesn't care and says nothing. Same silence, opposite cause.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Con**fident, **dif**fident — same word, prefix swapped: *with* trust vs. trust taken **away**.

Both are built on Latin *fides* (trust), so diffident is confident's exact negative — and the trust being withheld is trust in yourself.

### In sentences
1. Brilliant on paper but **diffident** in person, she let three louder colleagues present her findings before she said a word.
2. He gave this **diffident** little shrug when I asked if he'd won — turns out he'd come first.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dis-** (*apart, away*) + **fīdere** (*to trust*) — trust withdrawn.

One root, a whole GRE shelf: **confident** (with trust), **fidelity** (faithfulness), **fiduciary** (trusted with money), **infidel** (without the faith), **perfidy** (treachery), **bona fide** (in good faith) — and the classic dog's name **Fido**, "I trust."

# **dilatory** *(adj.)* — **DIL-uh-tor-ee**

*Not "dye-LAY-tuh-ree" — the stress is on the first syllable, and it has nothing to do with *dilate*.*

### Means
Slow to act; inclined to delay — and, said of an action, **done in order to cause delay**.

Calling someone **slow** describes a pace. Calling them **dilatory** files a complaint. The word carries an edge of blame: the delay was avoidable, and quite possibly the point. That's why it lives in formal registers — memos, court filings, school reports, letters from a solicitor. A friend who takes three weeks to reply is *terrible at texting*; a contractor who takes three weeks to reply is **dilatory**, and you're building a case.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dilly-dally** → **dilatory**: the same dragging of feet, in a suit and tie.

Both start *dil-* and both mean stalling, but *dilly-dally* is a parent nagging a child while *dilatory* is what goes in the formal complaint.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal found the company's disclosure **dilatory**, noting that documents requested in March had arrived, incomplete, in November.
2. Stop being so **dilatory** about the visa — the flights are in six weeks.

### In the wild
A live term of art in law and parliament: a judge or a Speaker can rule a motion **dilatory** — filed for no purpose but to burn the clock. It's the official word for stalling, which is exactly the flavour it keeps in ordinary prose.

### Where it comes from
Latin **differre** = *"to put off"* (dis- "apart" + ferre "to carry"), whose participle stem is **dilat-**. Delay is literally *carrying the thing away from now*. The same *-lat-* stem runs through **relate, translate, collate, elate, legislate** — all things carried.

🚫 **Not *dilate*.** That's *dilatare*, from *latus* meaning "wide" — pupils dilate, cervixes dilate. Identical-looking stem, unrelated root: one is *carried*, the other is *broad*.

# **equivocate** *(v.)* — **ih-KWIV-uh-kayt**

### Means
To speak **deliberately ambiguously** in order to avoid committing yourself, conceal the truth, or leave yourself an escape hatch.

The key is that it isn't a lie. A liar says something false; an equivocator says something *technically defensible* that he knows you will read the wrong way. That's why the charge stings — it accuses you of both cowardice and calculation at once. The word is almost always hostile: nobody describes their own answer as equivocating.

⚠️ **equivocate ≠ vacillate.**
- **equivocate** = waver in your **words** — say something slippery on purpose
- **vacillate** = waver in your **mind** — genuinely can't decide

A vacillator is indecisive. An equivocator has decided — to say nothing pinned down.

### Trick to lock it in
> You already know **unequivocal** — "no room for doubt." Equivocate is what's left when you take the *un-* off: the room for doubt, left in on purpose.

Everyone offers *unequivocal* support; the moment the support gets hedged, qualified and re-worded, you're watching someone equivocate.

### In sentences
1. Pressed on whether he had authorised the payment, the minister **equivocated** for twenty minutes and conceded nothing.
2. Don't **equivocate** — are you coming to the wedding or not?

### In the wild
*Macbeth* runs on this word — the drunk Porter jokes about "an equivocator, that could swear in both the scales against either scale," and Macbeth dies realising the witches' promises were exactly that: true in a sense he never meant.

### Where it comes from
Latin **aequus** (*equal*) + **vox, vocis** (*voice*) — a statement with **two equal voices**, pointing both ways at once.

That *voc-* root is a GRE workhorse: **vocal, vociferous, vocation** (a *calling*), **evoke, revoke, provoke, unequivocal**.

# **polarize** *(v.)* — **POH-luh-ryze**

*British spelling: polarise.*

### Means
To split a group into **two sharply opposed camps**, with nothing left in between. (Technical sense: to give something poles — as with light, magnets, or charge.)

The key isn't that people disagree; it's that the **middle ground vanishes**. A polarizing figure or issue drags everyone toward one extreme or the other — you can't stay lukewarm about it. That's why *polarizing* is the standard word for a celebrity or policy people either adore or loathe, never merely quite like.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Poles** are as far apart as two points can get — and nobody lives at the equator anymore.

To polarize is to push every last person to the North or South pole of an argument, emptying out the temperate middle.

### In sentences
1. The referendum **polarized** a country that had, until then, treated the question as a matter of mild indifference.
2. That show **polarizes** everyone I know — half my friends call it a masterpiece, the other half won't finish episode one.

### In the wild
**Polarized sunglasses** — the lens filters light down to a single plane and kills the glare. Same idea, physical version: everything gets forced into one orientation.

# **prodigal** *(adj. / n.)* — **PROD-i-guhl**

### Means
**Adj.:** spending money or resources with reckless, wasteful abandon. **Noun:** the person who does it.

This is a moral verdict, not a neutral description. *Extravagant* can be admiring; **prodigal** says the money was *squandered* — poured out until there was none left, usually by someone who should have known better. It carries the smell of an inheritance blown, a fortune wasted.

One twist the GRE loves: **prodigal has nothing to do with coming back.** Because of the parable, people use it to mean "the returning one." The prodigal son was prodigal *before* he came home — that's why he had nothing left.

A second, milder sense survives with *of*: **prodigal of praise**, *prodigal of detail* — lavishly generous, no insult intended.

⚠️ **prodigal ≠ prodigy.**
- **prodigal** = wasteful spender
- **prodigy** = astonishingly gifted young person

Mozart was a prodigy. His patron's heir was probably prodigal.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Prodigal** = *"prod-it-all"* — he prods it all out the door until the vault is empty.

The word is about the money going *out*, not the man coming *back*.

### In sentences
1. Two generations of **prodigal** heirs reduced the estate to a mortgaged shell.
2. He got the bonus on Friday and by Sunday it was gone — absolutely **prodigal** with it.

### In the wild
The **Parable of the Prodigal Son** — the boy who demands his inheritance early, blows it all abroad, and crawls home broke. The story is so famous that the word got dragged toward "returning"; remember that the title describes how he *spent*, not how he *came back*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prodigere** = *"to drive forth, squander"* — **pro-** ("forth") + **agere** ("to drive"). You're driving your money out the door.

**agere** is a workhorse root: **agent, agenda, agile, act, exact, navigate**.

🚫 **Not the source of *prodigy*.** That's Latin *prodigium*, "an omen, a portent" — a marvel, not a waster.

# **verbose** *(adj.)* — **ver-BOHS**

### Means
Using far more words than the job needs; wordy, padded, bloated.

The complaint is about the **prose, not the person**. Call someone *garrulous* or *loquacious* and you're describing a chatterbox; call their report *verbose* and you're describing a style problem — the ideas may be fine, but they arrive buried under three clauses of throat-clearing. It's the standard verdict on legal contracts, academic papers and consultants' slide decks, and it's cool and clinical rather than heated: an editor's word, not an insult shouted across a room.

One exception to the sting: in software, **verbose mode** is a neutral setting — you *want* the extra output.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Verb + -ose** — and *-ose* means "full of": grandi**ose** (full of grandeur), belli**cose** (full of war), verb**ose** (full of words).

Lock the suffix once and you get a free read on a whole family of GRE adjectives that all mean "overloaded with the thing named."

### In sentences
1. The judgment ran to four hundred **verbose** pages, of which perhaps twenty carried the reasoning.
2. Just tell me what broke — your emails are so **verbose** I never get to the point.

### In the wild
Any command line: `curl -v`, `npm install --verbose`. The `-v` flag literally means "print every last detail" — the one place being verbose is a feature.

### Where it comes from
Latin **verbum** = *"word."* The family is huge and all of it is about words: **verb**, **verbal**, **verbatim** (word for word), **proverb**, **adverb**.
