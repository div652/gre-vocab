# **abject** *(adj.)* — **AB-jekt**

### Means
**1.** Sunk to the lowest possible state — utterly wretched, hopeless, degraded: *abject poverty, abject misery, abject failure.*
**2.** Of a person's behaviour: **grovelling**, self-abasing, without a shred of dignity: *an abject apology, abject surrender.*

*Abject* isn't just a strong "very bad." It marks a **loss of dignity** — the thing or person has been brought so low that there's nothing left to salvage. *Extreme poverty* is a measurement; **abject** poverty is degrading. And when it describes behaviour it turns faintly contemptuous: an abject apology is one that crawls, and the speaker rather despises the crawling.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Abject** only ever marries misery — abject poverty, abject terror, abject failure, abject apology. There is no abject joy.

It's not a general intensifier you can bolt onto anything, but a word that says *this has hit the bottom and lost its dignity there*.

### In sentences
1. The report described families living in **abject** squalor, without water, power, or any prospect of relief.
2. He sent an **abject** three-page apology, which somehow made everyone think less of him.

### In the wild
**"Abject poverty"** is near-fixed in news and charity-appeal language — the UN, Oxfam, and every foreign-desk report reach for it. If you've heard the word at all, that's the phrase it came in.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ab-** (*away, down*) + **jacere** (*to throw*) → *abjectus*, "thrown down." The abject person has been cast to the floor.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **eject** (throw out), **reject** (throw back), **project** (throw forward), **inject**, **dejected** (thrown down in spirit), **conjecture** (throw ideas together), **trajectory** (the path of a throw).

# **amicable** *(adj.)* — **AM-i-kuh-buhl**

*Stress the first syllable — not "uh-MIK-uh-buhl."*

### Means
Characterised by goodwill; conducted **without hostility**.

The word almost always shows up where a fight was expected and didn't happen. An **amicable** divorce, an **amicable** settlement, an **amicable** parting of ways — it describes the *tone of a dealing between parties*, not affection. Two people can end things amicably and never speak again. It's the absence of rancour, not the presence of warmth, and it carries a slightly formal, almost legal air.

**amicable ≠ amiable.**
- **amicable** describes a *relationship or dealing* — an amicable settlement
- **amiable** describes a *person's disposition* — an amiable colleague

Two amiable people can still have a bitter split; two cold people can split amicably.

### Trick to lock it in
> You never hear "an amicable friendship" — only an amicable **divorce**, **split**, or **settlement**.

The word attaches itself to situations that could have turned ugly, which is exactly why it means *peaceable* rather than *affectionate*.

### In sentences
1. The border dispute was resolved **amicably**, both delegations signing before the deadline.
2. They split up last year but it was pretty **amicable** — he still crashes on her couch when he's in town.

### In the wild
Every celebrity breakup statement runs the same line: *"The couple have decided to separate amicably and ask for privacy at this time."* If a PR team wrote it, the word is in there.

### Where it comes from
Latin **amicus** = *"friend"* (from *amare*, to love). That root is worth banking: **amity** (friendly relations), **amiable**, **amigo**, **enamoured**.

Best of all, negate it: *in- + amicus* → **inimical** (hostile, harmful) and plain old **enemy** — literally *not-a-friend*.

# **animosity** *(n.)* — **an-uh-MOSS-uh-tee**

### Means
Strong, active hostility toward someone — ill will with heat behind it.

Dislike is a preference; **animosity** is a charge. It's usually *personal* and usually *has a history* — a feud, a snub, a rivalry that never healed — and it leaks out in tone, in refusals to cooperate, in the way two people avoid each other's name. It stops short of *hatred* (which is total) and it isn't the cool, refined distaste of *antipathy*; animosity is the bad blood you can feel in the room.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Anim**osity is **anim**ated dislike — hostility with a pulse.

The same *anim-* that makes cartoons move makes this dislike *live* — it acts, it simmers, it isn't the passive kind.

### In sentences
1. Decades after the partition, the **animosity** between the two families had hardened into a silence neither side would break.
2. There's still real **animosity** there — he hasn't spoken to his old boss in six years.

### In the wild
Standard sports-commentary vocabulary: derby matches and title fights are always sold on "the **animosity** between these two" — the grudge, not the skill, is the story.

### Where it comes from
Latin **animus** = *spirit, mind, passion*. Animosity first meant *spiritedness, boldness* — high spirits that soured into hostile ones.

The root is a GRE workhorse: **unanimous** (*one* spirit), **magnanimous** (*great* spirit — generous), **pusillanimous** (*tiny* spirit — cowardly), **equanimity** (*even* spirit — calm), plus **animate** and **animal** (things with breath in them).

# **aver** *(v.)* — **uh-VER**

*Rhymes with *prefer* — not AY-ver.*

### Means
To **state firmly as a fact** — to assert something confidently, often in the face of doubt or contradiction. In law, to allege as a fact in a pleading.

*Claim* leaves room for scepticism, *allege* actively flags the thing as unproven, but **aver** puts the speaker's own credibility behind the statement: they are vouching, flatly, without hedging. It is formal and slightly stiff — you meet it in courtrooms, reportage and 19th-century novels, almost always followed by *that*. Nobody avers a guess.

⚠️ **aver ≠ avert.** One letter apart, opposite worlds: **aver** = *declare it true*; **avert** = *turn away / prevent* (avert your eyes, avert disaster).

### Trick to lock it in
> **a-VER** = *assert* + *verify*: you don't just say it, you vouch for it.

The **ver** at its heart is the *true* of *verdict* and *verify*, which is why an averment is offered as fact rather than opinion.

### In sentences
1. The witness **averred**, without qualification, that she had seen the defendant leave the building shortly after midnight.
2. He still **avers** he never touched the last slice, crumbs on his shirt notwithstanding.

### In the wild
Open almost any American legal complaint and every numbered paragraph begins the same way: *"Plaintiff avers that…"* — the word is a lawyer's default verb for putting a fact on the record.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vērus** = *"true"*, via *ad-* + *vērāre* → Old French *averer*, "to prove true."

That root is a GRE goldmine: **verify, veracity, veritable, verdict** (*vere dictum*, "truly said"), **verisimilitude**, and plain old **very** (originally "truly").

# **barrage** *(n. / v.)* — **buh-RAHZH**

*Brits stress the first syllable: BA-rahzh. The ending is *zh* as in vision — it rhymes with mirage, not with porridge.*

### Means
**Noun:** a heavy, continuous outpouring aimed at a target — originally artillery fire, now equally questions, criticism, texts, complaints.
**Verb:** to hit someone with exactly that.

Volume alone isn't a barrage; the word carries *direction and sustained pressure*. A **torrent** is shapeless, a **volley** is one burst — a barrage is aimed, relentless, and leaves the receiver pinned down with no chance to answer. That's why it pairs so naturally with *criticism*, *questions*, *abuse*: things fired at a person who can't fire back.

### Trick to lock it in
> **BAR**rage — a **bar** of fire laid across your path: you can't get through, and you can't get a word in.

The hidden *bar* is the literal root, and it captures the nuance — a barrage doesn't just arrive, it *blocks* you.

### In sentences
1. Behind a creeping **barrage** of shellfire, the infantry moved up to the ridge.
2. He'd been home ten seconds before his mum **barraged** him with questions about the car.

### In the wild
Newsroom boilerplate: a minister always *"faced a barrage of criticism"* — never a trickle, never a helping. If you can hear that headline phrase, you've got the word.

### Where it comes from
French **barre** = *"bar"* → **barrer**, *"to block off."* A *barrage* in French is a **dam** — a barrier across a river (Britain still uses it that way: the Cardiff Bay Barrage). In WWI, *tir de barrage* meant **"barrier fire"**: a curtain of shells dropped to seal off a stretch of ground. The military sense walked into English from there, then spread to words.

Same **barr-** family: **bar, barrier, barricade, debar, embargo**.

# **cathartic** *(adj. / n.)* — **kuh-THAR-tik**

### Means
**Adj.:** providing release from strong or bottled-up emotion, so that you feel purged and lightened afterwards. **Noun (medical, older):** a purgative — a drug that empties the bowels.

The relief is on the far side of something intense: you sob, you scream into a pillow, you finally say the thing at the funeral. *Soothing* calms you down, *therapeutic* helps you heal over time; **cathartic** discharges a build-up in one go, and the experience itself is often unpleasant. That's why grief, rage, and horror films can all be cathartic, and a warm bath usually isn't.

### Trick to lock it in
> Before it cleaned out feelings, a **cathartic** cleaned out bowels.

The medical sense is the literal one — a purge — so remember cathartic as *emptying out*, not *cheering up*.

### In sentences
1. The confession was **cathartic** for her, though it cost her every friendship she had left.
2. Honestly, deleting that group chat was weirdly **cathartic**.

### In the wild
Aristotle's *Poetics* is where the idea lives: tragedy exists to give the audience **catharsis** — a purging of pity and fear. Every \"I watched a sad film and cried and felt better\" is that theory in action.

### Where it comes from
Greek **katharos** = *"pure, clean"* → **katharsis**, a cleansing. The medieval **Cathars** took the same root as their name: "the pure ones."

# **decipher** *(v.)* — **di-SEYE-fer**

### Means
To work out the meaning of something that resists being read — a coded message, a dead script, a doctor's handwriting, someone's motives.

The word always implies **obstruction plus effort**: there is a meaning in there, and something is in the way. *Decode* and *decrypt* are technical and systematic (you have a key, a method, a machine); *decipher* can be pure squinting persistence, and works just as well on a scrawled note as on Linear B. *Interpret* assumes the text is legible and the difficulty is in the meaning; *decipher* assumes the difficulty is in getting at the message at all. The negative, **indecipherable**, is the form you'll meet most often on the exam.

### Trick to lock it in
> **De-cipher** = take the **cipher** off it — strip the disguise, and the meaning was there all along.

The *de-* is subtraction, so you're not creating meaning, you're removing whatever was hiding it.

### In sentences
1. Scholars spent two decades attempting to **decipher** the tablets, whose script had no surviving bilingual key.
2. I still can't **decipher** what my dad wrote on this shopping list — is that "eggs" or "legs"?

### In the wild
**The Imitation Game** — Turing and the Bletchley Park team **deciphering** Enigma is the most famous act of deciphering in modern history, and the film is essentially two hours of the word in action.

### Where it comes from
**Cipher** comes from Arabic **ṣifr** = *"zero, empty"* — the same word that gave English **zero**. When Arabic numerals reached medieval Europe, the strange new symbols looked like secret writing, so *cipher* drifted from "zero" to "code."

Both senses survive: you **decipher** a coded message, and a person of no consequence is dismissed as **"a mere cipher"** — a zero, a placeholder. That second sense is the one the GRE likes.

# **delusion** *(n.)* — **dih-LOO-zhun**

### Means
A **false belief held with full conviction and immune to evidence** — and, by extension, the state of holding one.

A mistake gets corrected. An illusion dissolves the moment someone shows you the wires. A **delusion survives its own refutation** — that's the defining feature, and why the word indicts the *believer*, not the belief. There's a clinical shadow on it too (psychiatry uses *delusion* as a technical term for a fixed false belief), so calling someone's plan a delusion isn't saying it's wrong; it's saying they can't be reached. In everyday use — *delusions of grandeur*, *she's delusional* — the tone drops from diagnostic to mocking.

⚠️ **delusion ≠ illusion.**
- **illusion** = out in the world — a trick of light or stagecraft that fools *everyone* looking
- **delusion** = inside the head — it fools only its owner

You share an illusion with the whole audience. You hold a delusion alone.

### Trick to lock it in
> A mistake dies when you show it the evidence; a **delusion** grips harder.

What makes a belief a delusion isn't that it's false — it's that contradicting it only tightens the hold.

### In sentences
1. The regime's last months were governed by the **delusion** that the army was still loyal and the borders still held.
2. If you reckon you'll pass that exam without opening the book once, that's not confidence, mate, that's a **delusion**.

### In the wild
**\"Delusions of grandeur\"** is the phrase you've heard a hundred times — and it tells you something: delusions tend to arrive *flattering*, sized to fit the person holding them.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dēlūdere** = *"to play false, to mock"* — from **lūdere**, *"to play"* (*ludus* = a game). A delusion is reality playing you for a fool.

That root pays out across the whole exam: **ludicrous** (fit to be laughed at), **prelude** / **interlude** (played before / between), **allude** (play toward — hint at), **elude** (play away from — escape), **collude** (play together — secretly), **illusion**, **elusive**.

# **dispense** *(v.)* — **dis-PENS**

### Means
**1. To hand out in measured portions** — medicine, cash, advice, justice.
**2. dispense with** — to do without something, to waive it as unnecessary.

Sense 1 is cool and official: pharmacists, courts, and machines *dispense*; friends *share* and hosts *offer*. The word implies an allotted amount, a rule behind it, and no warmth in the giving — which is why "dispensing wisdom" is faintly sarcastic, as if the advice came out of a slot.

Sense 2 is the one the GRE actually tests, and it's brisk to the point of dismissal: *let's dispense with the formalities* means sweep them aside, we're above that.

⚠️ **dispense ≠ disperse.**
- **dispense** = give out in portions (a pharmacy dispenses pills)
- **disperse** = scatter, break up (police disperse a crowd)

One distributes, one dissolves.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **dispenser** never gives you the whole bottle — one measured squirt, by the rules.

That metered, impersonal quality is the whole flavour of the word — and *dispense **with*** is the opposite move: no portion at all, the thing is waived entirely.

### In sentences
1. The colonial magistrate was empowered to **dispense** justice without appeal, a licence that corrupted him within a year.
2. Let's **dispense with** the introductions — we've all read each other's emails.

### In the wild
Every ATM screen that says **"Cash dispensed"** — a machine handing you an exact allotted amount, never a penny more.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dispendere** = *"to weigh out"* — *dis-* (out) + **pendere** (to weigh, to hang, to pay). Weighing out is literally what a dispenser does.

*pendere* is a high-yield GRE root: **pension** (money weighed out), **compensate** (weigh against), **ponder** (weigh in the mind), **pendant** (hanging), **expend**, **indispensable** (can't be waived).

# **eloquent** *(adj.)* — **EL-uh-kwuhnt**

*Three syllables, stress up front — not "el-OH-kwent".*

### Means
Speaking (or writing) **fluently, gracefully, and persuasively** — with language that moves people.

*Articulate* only means you make yourself clear; **eloquent** means you make people *feel* it. It is praise, and fairly high praise: it implies command of rhythm and phrasing, not just vocabulary. Note the second sense the GRE likes — a thing can be eloquent without words at all: an **eloquent silence**, an eloquent glance, statistics that are eloquent about a country's decline. There, it means *vividly expressive*.

**eloquent ≠ loquacious.** Same root, opposite compliment: **eloquent** = speaks *well*; **loquacious** = speaks *a lot* (and usually too much). The bore at the party is loquacious; the person who quiets the room is eloquent.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Eloquent** is **elegant** with a **Q** — elegance, but in speech.

The near-rhyme carries the nuance exactly: eloquence is not loud or lengthy but *graceful*, which is why a silence can be eloquent too.

### In sentences
1. His eulogy was brief and **eloquent**; not a word of it was wasted, and half the church was in tears by the end.
2. She didn't say anything — but the look she gave him was pretty **eloquent**.

### In the wild
It's the standard word reached for about King's *I Have a Dream* — routinely called the most eloquent speech in American history, and a good ear-test for what eloquence actually sounds like.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** (*out*) + **loquī** (*to speak*) — "speaking out."

That root is a GRE goldmine: **loquacious** (talkative), **colloquial** (speaking together — everyday talk), **soliloquy** (speaking alone), **circumlocution** (talking *around* the point), **grandiloquent** (speaking grandly, i.e. pompous), **elocution**, and **ventriloquist** — literally a *belly-speaker*.

# **enthrall** *(v.)* — **en-THRAWL**

*British spelling: enthral, one L.*

### Means
To hold someone's attention so completely that they lose all awareness of anything else — to captivate, spellbind, charm into helplessness.

*Interest* and *fascinate* leave you in charge of yourself; **enthral** takes that away. The enthralled person isn't choosing to keep watching — they *can't stop*. It's surrender, and it's almost always pleasurable surrender, which is what separates it from *transfix* (shock-frozen) or *obsess* (uncomfortable, self-generated). Usually passive: **you are enthralled *by* something**, and the something has the power.

### Trick to lock it in
> **En-THRALL** = *put into thrall* — and a **thrall** is a **slave**.

The enthralled audience isn't merely watching the performer — it belongs to them.

### In sentences
1. The soprano's final aria **enthralled** a hall of two thousand into total silence.
2. I meant to watch one episode and it **enthralled** me straight through till 3 a.m.

### In the wild
**"Enthralling"** is the standard blurb word on book jackets and Netflix descriptions — *an enthralling thriller*. Critics reach for it precisely when they mean *you will not be able to put this down*.

### Where it comes from
**en-** (*put into*) + **thrall**, from Old Norse **þræll** = *"slave."* A Viking thrall was literally chattel — the bottom of Norse society.

The old sense survives intact in **"in thrall to"**: *in thrall to her addiction* means enslaved by it, not delighted by it. Same root, no metaphor softening.

# **eradicate** *(v.)* — **ih-RAD-ih-kayt**

### Means
To destroy something **completely and permanently** — to pull it out root and all so it cannot come back.

The word carries totality. *Reduce* and *curb* admit a remainder; **eradicate** claims there is none left to regrow. It also comes loaded with a target: you eradicate diseases, poverty, corruption, pests — almost never something good. And it has a faintly institutional ring, the language of campaigns and five-year plans rather than of one person tidying up.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A radish is a root — er*adic*ate is yanking the root out, so nothing grows back.**

Cutting the weed off at ground level is *removal*; taking the root is eradication, which is why the word implies permanence and not just absence.

### In sentences
1. The commission was charged with **eradicating** the corruption that had grown into every level of the ministry.
2. You can't just spray the kitchen — you've got to **eradicate** the nest or they're back next week.

### In the wild
Smallpox is the standard example: the WHO declared it **eradicated** in 1980 — not merely controlled, but gone from the planet. Polio campaigns still use the same word, deliberately.

### Where it comes from
Latin **rādīx, rādīcis** = *"root."* *E-* (out) + *rādīc-* = "root it out."

Same root gives **radical** (going to the root of things), **radish** (the root you eat), and **radix** in maths.

# **fledgling** *(n. / adj.)* — **FLEJ-ling**

*Two syllables, not three — the -dg- is a plain j.*

### Means
**Noun:** a young bird that has just grown its flight feathers and left the nest; by extension, a beginner.
**Adj.:** new, inexperienced, only just up and running — *a fledgling democracy, a fledgling airline*.

Where *novice* is flatly factual and *amateur* edges toward incompetent, **fledgling** is sympathetic: it says *untested and fragile, but airborne*. The word grants potential. Calling a company fledgling means it might make it; calling it amateurish means it won't. Note that it's applied to **ventures and institutions** far more often than to people.

### Trick to lock it in
> **fledge** (grow flight feathers) + **-ling** (the little-one suffix: duck*ling*, sap*ling*, year*ling*).

A little thing that has *just* got its wings — which is why the word sounds hopeful rather than insulting.

### In sentences
1. The treaty was signed by a **fledgling** republic that had held exactly one election.
2. He's a **fledgling** cook — everything's edible, nothing's on time.

### In the wild
Business and foreign-desk journalism runs on it: **fledgling startup**, **fledgling democracy**, **fledgling industry** — all meaning *too young to judge yet*.

### Where it comes from
From Old English *flycge*, "feathered, fit to fly" — same family as **fly** and **flight**. That's also why **full-fledged** means fully feathered, i.e. ready for anything; a fledgling is the stage just before.

# **fortitude** *(n.)* — **FOR-ti-tyood**

*Also FOR-ti-tood in American speech.*

### Means
Courage of the enduring kind — strength of mind that lets you bear pain, hardship, or fear **over time** without collapsing.

Bravery charges; fortitude *holds*. It's the virtue of the cancer ward, the long trial, the grinding setback — not the burning building. It implies quiet dignity rather than heroics, and it always suggests duration: nobody shows fortitude for ten seconds. Formal and warmly approving, the word a eulogy reaches for.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fort**itude: a **fort** doesn't attack — it withstands the siege.

The fort hidden in the word is defensive, which is exactly the flavour of courage meant — absorbing punishment, not delivering it.

### In sentences
1. Through eighteen months of treatment she bore the illness with a **fortitude** that silenced everyone who had pitied her.
2. Sitting through four hours of his holiday photos took real **fortitude**.

### In the wild
The two marble lions outside the New York Public Library are named **Patience** and **Fortitude** — christened by Mayor La Guardia in the Depression for the qualities he said New Yorkers would need to get through it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fortis** = *"strong."* The root is everywhere: **fort**, **fortress**, **fortify**, **force**, **effort** — and **comfort**, literally *"to strengthen (someone) thoroughly."*

# **fortuitous** *(adj.)* — **for-TOO-i-tuhs**

*British speakers often say for-TYOO-i-tuhs.*

### Means
Happening **by chance**, without design or planning — accidental, unforeseen.

Strictly, the word reports *causation*, not *quality*: a fortuitous event simply wasn't arranged by anyone, and it can be a windfall or a disaster. Popular usage has dragged it toward "happily accidental," and most writers now use it that way, but the GRE tests the older, neutral sense — chance, full stop. On an exam, read it as *unplanned* and let context decide whether the outcome was good.

⚠️ **fortuitous ≠ fortunate.**
- **fortuitous** = by *chance* (says nothing about the outcome)
- **fortunate** = by *good luck* (says nothing about the cause)

A fortunate heir planned nothing and gained much; a fortuitous collision was nobody's plan and helped no one.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fortuitous** rhymes with **circuitous** and **gratuitous** — the *-itous* family all describe *how a thing came about* (by a winding route, without cause, by chance), never how good it was.

Tie the rhyme to "manner of arrival" and the luck drops out of the definition, which is exactly where the exam sets its trap.

### In sentences
1. The discovery of penicillin was **fortuitous**: a contaminated dish that Fleming had every reason to throw away.
2. We ran into each other at the airport — totally **fortuitous**, neither of us knew the other was flying.

### In the wild
Sports commentary leans on it constantly — "a **fortuitous** deflection off the defender's shin" means the goal came from chance, not skill, and often carries a faint sting.

### Where it comes from
Latin **forte** = *"by chance"* (ablative of **fors**, *chance*) → **fortuitus**. Same root as **fortune** and **misfortune** — note that *fortune* itself was originally neutral, the goddess who dealt out good and bad alike.

🚫 **Not from *fortis*, "strong."** That's the other family: *fort, fortify, fortitude, effort*. A fortuitous rescue is a lucky one, not a sturdy one.

# **goad** *(v. / n.)* — **GOHD**

### Means
**Verb:** to provoke or drive someone into doing something, typically by persistent needling.
**Noun:** a spiked stick used to drive cattle — and by extension, anything that spurs you on.

*Urge* appeals to your reason, *spur* pushes a willing horse; **goad** works by irritation. The goaded person acts to make the jabbing stop, which is why they so often act badly — the standard collocation is *goaded into* a fight, a mistake, an unguarded remark. Sympathy usually sits with the goaded, blame with the goader.

### Trick to lock it in
> **go + ad** — an ad that nags you until you finally *go*.

A goad doesn't persuade you, it pesters you into moving.

### In sentences
1. The columnist's jibes were calculated to **goad** the minister into an unguarded reply.
2. Don't let him **goad** you — he just wants you to swing first.

### In the wild
The biblical line *"it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks"* (Acts 9:5) is rendered in modern translations as **"kick against the goads"** — the ox that lashes back at the driver's stick only hurts itself.

### Where it comes from
Old English **gād** = a *spear-point / pointed stick* for driving oxen. The literal object survives inside the metaphor: someone goading you is jabbing you along like livestock.

# **imminent** *(adj.)* — **IM-uh-nuhnt**

### Means
About to happen **at any moment** — hanging over you, ready to land.

The word is about *timing*, not certainty: an imminent collapse is one that could come this hour. It also leans dark by habit — what's described as imminent is nearly always a threat, an arrest, a war, a death, a deadline. You can say "imminent arrival," but the company it usually keeps is bad. Compare **impending** (same nearness, heavier and more dreadful) and **forthcoming** (near, but neutral-to-welcome).

⚠️ **imminent ≠ eminent.**
- **imminent** = about to happen
- **eminent** = distinguished, standing out above others

An eminent judge can face imminent retirement.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Imminent = in a minute.**

The sound of the word is its meaning — whatever it is, it's arriving in a minute, and it's probably not good news.

### In sentences
1. With the levees failing and the storm surge still rising, evacuation was no longer prudent but **imminent** necessity.
2. Don't start a movie — dinner's **imminent**.

### In the wild
The emergency alerts that override your phone's silent mode are officially categorised as **"Imminent Threat Alerts"** — tornado, flash flood, wildfire. The label is doing exactly what the word means: not *possible*, but *about to hit you*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** ("upon") + **minēre** ("to jut, project, overhang") — something jutting out *over your head*.

That root sorts the whole confusing family by direction: **imminent** juts *over* you (in-), **eminent** juts *out above* the crowd (ex-), **prominent** juts *forward* (pro-). Same verb, three prefixes, three GRE words.

# **incontrovertible** *(adj.)* — **in-kon-truh-VER-tuh-buhl**

### Means
**So firmly established that it cannot be argued against** — the case against it simply has nowhere to stand.

This is the top of the certainty scale, and it is a claim about *evidence*, not about feelings. **Undeniable** can describe a mood or a charm; **incontrovertible** wants facts, data, documents, DNA. It's also heavy, formal machinery — courtroom, editorial, scientific paper — so in casual speech it lands as either very serious or slightly pompous. Note too that it grades the *proof*, not the *importance*: a fact can be incontrovertible and utterly trivial.

⚠️ **incontrovertible ≠ uncontroversial.**
- **incontrovertible** = the evidence is so strong that argument is impossible
- **uncontroversial** = nobody happens to be arguing about it

A dull, uncontroversial claim may rest on nothing at all; an incontrovertible one can still start a fight.

### Trick to lock it in
> **incontrovertible ______** — the blank is nearly always *evidence*, *proof*, or *fact*. Never *opinion*.

The word rates how checkable something is, so it only attaches to things that could, in principle, be checked.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal found the documentary record **incontrovertible**: the orders had been signed a full week before the raid.
2. Look, the timestamps are right there — that's **incontrovertible**, mate, stop arguing.

### In the wild
Courtroom staple: the closing-argument line "the evidence is **incontrovertible**" is the prosecutor's move in roughly every legal drama — and in real filings too.

### Where it comes from
Latin **contrā** (*against*) + **vertere** (*to turn*) → *controvert*, "to turn an argument against." Slap **in-** on the front and nothing can be turned against it.

**vertere** is one of the best roots to own for the GRE: **convert, divert, invert, avert, subvert, revert, versatile, adverse, controversy**.

# **itinerant** *(adj. / n.)* — **eye-TIN-uh-ruhnt**

*Also ih-TIN-uh-ruhnt; the stress never moves to the front.*

### Means
**Adj.:** travelling from place to place, especially to do a job. **Noun:** a person who does so.

The word is bound to **work and a route**. An itinerant preacher, judge, labourer or salesman moves because the job moves — there's a circuit, a season, a next town. That distinguishes it from *nomadic* (a whole way of life, often a culture) and from *vagrant* (no work, no home, and an insult). *Itinerant* is descriptive and faintly formal — an administrator's word — though it can carry a whiff of low status, since the people it describes usually don't own the place they're working in.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Itinerant** is just **itinerary** with the end knocked off — someone whose life *is* the itinerary.

The overlap fixes both the spelling and the key nuance: not aimless drifting, but moving along a route, for work.

### In sentences
1. For thirty years he was an **itinerant** judge, holding court in one county town after another as the assizes required.
2. She's basically an **itinerant** hairdresser now — three salons, three cities, one very tired car.

### In the wild
American school districts advertise **itinerant teachers** and **itinerant speech therapists** — staff who serve four or five schools instead of one. If you ever see the word on a job posting, that's what it means.

### Where it comes from
Latin **iter, itineris** = *"a journey, a route"* — itself from **ire**, *"to go."*

That's a productive root: **itinerary** (the route written down), and from *ire* more broadly **transit, exit, circuit, initial, ambition** (originally *going around* canvassing for votes).

# **magnanimous** *(adj.)* — **mag-NAN-uh-muhs**

### Means
Generous, forgiving, and above pettiness — especially toward a rival, a loser, or someone who is at your mercy.

The word needs an occasion for spite. You are magnanimous only when resentment would have been fair and you decline it anyway: the winner who praises the beaten team, the wronged party who drops the charge. Plain *kind* costs nothing; **magnanimous** costs a grudge you were entitled to keep. It's high, slightly grand praise — and it quietly says the person had the power in the exchange.

⚠️ **magnanimous ≠ munificent.**
- **magnanimous** = big of *spirit* — forgives, refuses to gloat
- **munificent** = big of *wallet* — gives lavishly

A munificent donor writes the cheque; a magnanimous rival shakes your hand after beating you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **MAGNA** = big. Too big to be petty.

Magna Carta is the *great* charter, and a magnanimous person is the one whose spirit is too large to squeeze the advantage they're holding.

### In sentences
1. In a **magnanimous** concession speech, she thanked the opponent who had spent six months calling her a fraud.
2. He was **magnanimous** about the dent in his car, which honestly made me feel worse.

### In the wild
Churchill's motto for his war memoirs: *"In War: Resolution. In Defeat: Defiance. **In Victory: Magnanimity**. In Peace: Goodwill."* — victory is exactly the moment the word belongs to.

### Where it comes from
Latin **animus** = *"soul, spirit"* — that half is the payload. It gives you **unanimous** (one spirit), **animosity** (spirit turned hostile), **animated** (given spirit), and the perfect GRE antonym: **pusillanimous** (*pusillus*, "tiny" + *animus*) — tiny-souled, cowardly, petty.

Big soul, small soul. Learn the pair together.

# **meritorious** *(adj.)* — **mer-i-TOR-ee-uhs**

### Means
Deserving of praise, reward, or honour — worthy on the strength of what was actually done.

This is praise handed **downward from an institution**: the army, the court, the citation on a plaque. *Admirable* is what a friend says about you; *meritorious* is what the commendation letter says. It attaches to **acts and service**, rarely to a person directly — meritorious *conduct*, meritorious *service*, a meritorious *career*. It also carries a faint whiff of the dutiful: solid, creditable, worth a medal, but not dazzling.

Legal sense worth knowing: a **meritorious claim** is one with genuine legal substance — the opposite of frivolous.

⚠️ **meritorious ≠ meretricious.**
- **meritorious** = *deserving of reward* (Latin *merēre*, to earn)
- **meretricious** = *gaudy, showily fake* (Latin *meretrix*, prostitute)

Nearly the same letters, opposite verdicts: one earns the medal, the other is cheap glitter.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Merit** + *-orious*, like **glorious** — the merit is the public, medal-pinning kind.

The word wears its root on its sleeve; the *-orious* ending tells you the praise is formal and announced, not a quiet pat on the back.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal restored his pension in recognition of forty years of **meritorious** service.
2. She got a **meritorious** mention for the science fair — solid work, but nobody's calling it genius.

### In the wild
The US military's **Meritorious Service Medal** — awarded for outstanding non-combat service. The word survives mainly on citations and plaques, which is exactly its register.

### Where it comes from
Latin **merēre / meritum** = *"to earn, to deserve."* The same root gives you **merit**, **demerit**, **meritocracy** (rule by the deserving), and **emeritus** — the professor who has *earned* his retirement and keeps the title.

# **mutiny** *(n. / v.)* — **MYOO-tuh-nee**

### Means
Open revolt against lawful authority by the very people bound to obey it — classically sailors against their captain, soldiers against their officers. As a verb, to rise up in this way.

The word is institutional, not political. A population rebels; a *crew* mutinies. It presumes a chain of command, an oath, and a punishment — mutiny is a criminal charge under naval and military law, and historically a hanging offence. That's why it carries such force when borrowed for civilian life: calling the marketing team's revolt a **mutiny** casts the boss as a captain and the staff as insubordinate crew.

### Trick to lock it in
> **MY**-tiny: a mutiny is always *my own* people — the ones sworn to take my orders.

Strangers can riot against you, but only your subordinates can mutiny.

### In sentences
1. Three officers were court-martialled for **mutiny** after they refused to weigh anchor.
2. One more mandatory Saturday and there'll be a full **mutiny** in the design team.

### In the wild
***Mutiny on the Bounty*** — the real 1789 event and the films: Fletcher Christian and the crew seize the ship and set Captain Bligh adrift in an open boat. Textbook case, textbook word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **movēre** = *"to move"* → Old French **mutin**, "rebellious," from *meute*, "an uprising." A mutiny is literally a body of men **set in motion** against their commander.

That root is a GRE workhorse: **motion, motive, momentum, emotion, promote, remove, mobile** — all people or things being moved.

# **paradoxical** *(adj.)* — **par-uh-DOK-si-kuhl**

### Means
Self-contradictory on the face of it — and yet true, or at least standing up to scrutiny.

The word does real work only when both halves survive. Something merely **contradictory** is broken: one side must be wrong. Something **ironic** is a wink at a mismatched outcome. **Paradoxical** keeps both sides alive at once — the drug that causes the symptom it treats, the rule that makes obeying it impossible. Tone is analytical rather than critical: calling a finding paradoxical says *this is worth thinking about*, not *this is nonsense*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Contradictory** means it's wrong. **Paradoxical** means it's wrong *and true anyway*.

Save the word for contradictions that survive inspection — the safety feature that causes accidents, the crowd that leaves you lonelier.

### In sentences
1. The study reached the **paradoxical** conclusion that the most heavily policed districts reported the least confidence in the police.
2. It's a bit **paradoxical**, but the more sleep-tracking apps I install, the worse I sleep.

### In the wild
**Catch-22** is the purest paradoxical rule in fiction: you can be grounded for insanity, but asking to be grounded proves you're sane. The book gave English a phrase for exactly this shape of trap.

### Where it comes from
Greek **para-** (*beside, contrary to*) + **doxa** (*opinion, belief*) → *paradoxos*, "contrary to received opinion."

That **dox** unlocks a set: **orthodox** (*straight* opinion), **heterodox** (*other* opinion), **doxology** (*doxa* also = glory, so: words of praise). And **para-** runs through **parody** (*a song sung alongside*), **parallel**, **paranormal**.

# **perseverance** *(n.)* — **per-suh-VEER-uhns**

*Not "per-SEV-er-uhns" — the noun keeps the VEER sound of persevere.*

### Means
Steady, continued effort at something difficult, kept up despite failure, delay, or discouragement.

**Persistence** is neutral and can grate — a persistent cough, a persistent salesman. **Perseverance** is praise: it presumes real obstacles and a person who deserves credit for outlasting them. It's the virtue on the good side of a line whose bad side is **obstinacy** — same refusal to stop, but pointed at something worth finishing. Note also that it's slow-burn, not intense: perseverance is measured in months of grinding, not one heroic push.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Persevere** hides **severe** — you stay severe with yourself all the way *through* (**per-**).

The hidden word gives you both halves of the meaning: strictness with yourself, sustained for the whole distance.

### In sentences
1. Her **perseverance** through eleven years of rejected manuscripts is the only reason the novel exists at all.
2. He's not the smartest guy on the team, but sheer **perseverance** got him through the exam on the fourth try.

### In the wild
NASA's Mars rover **Perseverance** — named by a middle-schooler in a national contest, then flown 300 million miles to grind away at Martian rock for years. The name is the job description.

### Where it comes from
Latin **per-** (*through, thoroughly*) + **sevērus** (*strict, serious*) → **persevērāre**, "to keep at it strictly."

That second root is the useful one: it also gives **severe**, **severity**, and the GRE word **asseverate** (*to declare earnestly, in dead seriousness*).

# **render** *(v. (n. in design))* — **REN-der**

### Means
Three senses the GRE actually uses:

1. **To cause to become** — always with an adjective after it: *rendered useless, rendered speechless, rendered obsolete*.
2. **To give, deliver, or hand over** what is owed or asked for: *render aid, render a verdict, render an account*.
3. **To represent** — in another medium or another language: an artist *renders* a face; a translator *renders* Homer into English.

Sense 2 is the formal one and carries a whiff of obligation: you *render* what is due, you don't *render* a birthday present. Sense 1 is the everyday workhorse and is stiffer than *make* — "made the treaty void" is plain, "rendered the treaty void" is legal-register. Across all three the core gesture is the same: something is **handed over or turned into** something else.

⚠️ **render ≠ rend.** *Rend* means to **tear violently apart** (*rent* garments, a heart-rending cry). Same first four letters, unrelated word.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sur-render** = give *over* entirely. Strip the *sur-* and **render** is just **give**.

Every sense falls out of *give*: give aid, give a verdict, give back the image — and "render it useless" is giving the thing a new state.

### In sentences
1. A single ambiguous clause **rendered** the entire agreement unenforceable, and the tribunal **rendered** its judgment accordingly.
2. One bad update **rendered** my laptop useless — twenty minutes just to **render** a two-minute video.

### In the wild
The **Render** button in any video or 3D app: you hit it and the machine *gives back* the finished image. Same word, same root, and it also covers "represent in another medium."

### Where it comes from
Latin **reddere** = *"to give back"* (*re-* back + *dare* to give), via Old French *rendre*.

The *give-back* family is worth owning: **surrender** (give over), **rendition** (a handing-over — hence *extraordinary rendition*, and also a performance *given* to an audience), **rendezvous** (French *rendez-vous*, "present yourselves"), **rent** (what you give back for use of a thing).

# **repertoire** *(n.)* — **REP-er-twahr**

*The French *-oire* is "twahr" — never "toy-er" or "twire."*

### Means
The body of pieces a performer is ready to perform — a musician's, a company's, a dancer's — and by extension the full stock of skills, tricks or moves anyone has available.

The word implies **readiness, not history**. A repertoire isn't everything you've ever done; it's what you can produce *now*, rehearsed and on demand. That's why it stretches so easily outside the arts — a bowler's repertoire of deliveries, a chef's repertoire of sauces, a toddler's repertoire of excuses. Slightly elegant, faintly French, but entirely at home in sports commentary and job interviews.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nothing enters your rep**ertoire **until you've done the reps.**

The word opens with **rep** — and a repertoire is exactly the set of things you've drilled enough to deliver cold.

### In sentences
1. The orchestra's **repertoire** ran from Monteverdi to Ligeti, but they could sell out a hall only with the Beethoven.
2. Making an omelette is about the extent of my **repertoire**, so don't get excited.

### In the wild
The Beatles' Hamburg years are the classic case: playing five-hour sets night after night forced them to expand their **repertoire** far beyond the handful of covers they arrived with.

### Where it comes from
Latin **repertorium** = *"an inventory, a list of things found"* (from *reperire*, "to find"). A repertoire is literally your **catalogue of what you can lay hands on** — which is why the same root gives **repertory** theatre, a company holding several plays in stock at once.

# **resilient** *(adj.)* — **ri-ZIL-yuhnt**

*Three syllables in speech — not "ri-ZIL-ee-uhnt."*

### Means
Able to **recover quickly** from shock, hardship, or damage; of a material, **springing back** into shape after being bent or compressed.

The word concedes the blow. *Robust* and *durable* say the damage never lands; **resilient** says it lands, and you come back anyway. That's why it's the standard praise word for people, economies, ecosystems, and markets that get hit and return to form — and why calling someone resilient implicitly acknowledges they've been through something. Compare *stoic*, which is about not showing the pain, not about recovering from it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Re-** like *rebound, return, recover*: resilient things don't resist the hit, they come back from it.

The prefix carries the whole nuance — recovery after impact, not immunity to it.

### In sentences
1. Three years after the earthquake the local economy proved unexpectedly **resilient**, with output back above pre-quake levels.
2. Kids are weirdly **resilient** — he cried for about ten seconds and then went straight back down the slide.

### In the wild
Every cloud provider sells **"resilient architecture"** — and the sales pitch spells out the nuance: the servers *will* fail, the system recovers in seconds. Resilient never meant unbreakable.

### Where it comes from
Latin **resilīre** = *"to leap back"* (**re-** back + **salīre** to leap).

That *salīre* is a workhorse root: **salient** (a point that *leaps out* at you), **assault/assail** (*leap at*), **somersault** (*over-leap*), **sally**, **exult**, and the GRE favourite **desultory** — literally *leaping about*, hence aimless and unmethodical.

# **resolute** *(adj.)* — **REZ-uh-loot**

*Don't let the verb drag it: re-ZOLV gives you re-ZOL-oot, which is wrong.*

### Means
Firmly determined; unwavering in purpose once a decision has been made.

The key is that the deciding is **already over**. *Determined* describes effort, *stubborn* describes refusal, but **resolute** describes a settled mind holding steady under pressure — doubt was weighed and dismissed, and now nothing moves it. It's a word of praise and it carries dignity: obituaries, war memorials, and leaders' speeches are full of it. You call an ally resolute; you call an opponent obstinate for exactly the same behaviour.

⚠️ **resolute ≠ dissolute.** Same *-solute* ending, opposite lives: **resolute** = morally firm and steady; **dissolute** = morally *loosened* — debauched, dissipated, drinking the inheritance away.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Re-solute** = **re-solved**. The argument already happened; resolute is what's left afterwards.

Hear *resolve* inside it and you get the whole nuance — this isn't someone straining to decide, it's someone who decided and won't be moved off it.

### In sentences
1. Facing a hostile chamber, she remained **resolute**, repeating the terms she had set out three months earlier without altering a word.
2. He wanted to quit at mile eighteen, but he was **resolute** about finishing, so he just kept shuffling.

### In the wild
The **Resolute Desk** in the Oval Office — the one behind every US president in every photo — was built from the timbers of HMS *Resolute*. The name is doing exactly the work the word means.

### Where it comes from
Latin **re-** + **solvere** = *"to loosen, release"* — your doubt gets dissolved, and what remains is the settled decision.

*Solvere* is a high-yield GRE root: **solve, resolve, dissolve, absolve, solvent, dissolution** — and **dissolute**, the trap above, which is literally "loosened apart."

# **supple** *(adj.)* — **SUP-uhl**

*Rhymes with couple — the first syllable is "sup," not "soop."*

### Means
Bending and moving easily without breaking or resisting — of a body, a material, or by extension a mind or a prose style.

Praise, almost always. *Limp*, *floppy* and *flabby* describe things that bend because they have no strength; **supple** describes things that bend *and spring back* — the gymnast, the well-worn leather, the fencer's wrist. It also escapes the faint contempt in *pliable* and *malleable*, which hint at a person too easily bent by others: a **supple** negotiator is quick and adaptive, not a pushover.

⚠️ **supple ≠ supine.** Both open with *sup-* and both describe bodies, but they are near-opposites in spirit: **supple** = flexible and strong; **supine** = lying face-up, and figuratively *passively inert, refusing to resist*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sup-ple** ends in the same fold you hear in **pleat** — but a pleat folds and stays folded; supple folds and springs back.

The *-ple* is literally a fold (Latin *plicāre*), and the word's whole value is that the fold is temporary — bend, return, no damage.

### In sentences
1. Decades at the loom had left her hands scarred but still **supple**, capable of the finest work in the room.
2. These boots are stiff now, but give them some oil and a month of wear and they'll go lovely and **supple**.

### In the wild
It's the standard sales word for good leather — every jacket, handbag and car-seat listing promises "soft, **supple** leather," meaning it bends with you instead of cracking.

### Where it comes from
Latin **supplex** = *"bending under, kneeling"* — from *sub* ("under") + **plicāre** ("to fold, to bend").

**plicāre** is a workhorse root: *pliable, pliant, ply, comply, apply, imply, complicated, explicate, duplicate, replicate* — all folding.

**Supplicate** ("to beg") is a true cousin, not a false friend: same *supplex*, the same kneeling body. One branch kept the bending, the other kept the begging.

# **valor** *(n.)* — **VAL-er**

*British spelling: valour. Same sound.*

### Means
Great personal courage in the face of danger — above all, **courage in battle**.

Where *bravery* is the everyday word and *nerve* is cheeky, **valor is ceremonial**. It belongs to citations, eulogies and medals, and it almost always implies combat or a heroic rescue: you show valor storming a position, not asking your boss for a raise. It also carries a whiff of the old-fashioned — used of ordinary modern life, it sounds either grand or mock-grand, which is exactly why it works as a joke.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nobody hands out a **Medal of Bravery** — the medal says **valor**.

Valor is the officially decorated, battlefield grade of courage, not the everyday kind.

### In sentences
1. The citation praised his **valor** under fire: he crossed forty metres of open ground to drag two wounded men back to the line.
2. She opened the fridge, sniffed the leftovers, and ate them anyway — **valor**, of a sort.

### In the wild
Shakespeare's Falstaff, dodging a fight in *Henry IV*: **"The better part of valour is discretion."** The joke only lands because valour means battlefield courage — he's redefining bravery as running away.

### Where it comes from
Latin **valēre** = *"to be strong, to be worth."* Strength and worth are the same idea in this root, which is why it splits two ways: **valiant, valor, prevail, convalesce** (strength) and **value, valid, invalid, evaluate, avail** (worth).

Note the pair **invalid** — *in-VAL-id* (not strong: a sick person) and *IN-vuh-lid* (not valid: a bad argument). One root, two stresses.
