# Group 24

# **assail** *(v.)* — **uh-SAYL**

### Means
To **attack violently and repeatedly** — with fists, with weapons, or (more often on the GRE) with words: *critics assailed the proposal*.

The force of it is in the *repetition*. You attack once; you assail over and over, blow after blow, question after question. It's also the standard verb for being overwhelmed **from the inside**: *assailed by doubts, by nausea, by memories* — the feeling behaves like a mob, and you are outnumbered.

Register is that of a newspaper or a battlefield, never a shrug. Note the useful offspring **unassailable** — an argument or a lead so strong no amount of battering dents it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Assail = "a hail"** — a hail of blows, of questions, of doubts, and it doesn't stop at one.

Hail comes down in volleys, which is exactly what separates *assail* from a single *attack*.

### In sentences
1. Opposition members **assailed** the minister for three hours, each rising in turn to demand her resignation.
2. He'd been fine all week, then at 3 a.m. he was **assailed** by the certainty that he'd sent the email to the wrong person.

### In the wild
Every news report of a mugging calls the attacker an **assailant** — never an "assaulter." If you can hear a police bulletin saying *"the victim did not recognise her assailant,"* you already own the verb.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*at*) + **salīre** (*to leap*) → *assilīre*, "to leap upon." An assailant literally springs at you.

*Salīre* is a GRE goldmine: **salient** (leaping out at the eye), **resilient** (leaping back), **assault**, **insult** (leaping on someone), **exult**, **somersault**, and the **sally** an army makes out of a fort.

# **benevolent** *(adj.)* — **buh-NEV-uh-luhnt**

### Means
Well-meaning and kindly; disposed to do good, and usually in a position to.

The word points at **disposition, not action** — a benevolent person wishes you well; whether they write the cheque is another matter. It carries a faint air of altitude: the benevolent party is the one with the power, the money, or the age. That's why it sits so naturally in *benevolent fund*, *benevolent society*, *benevolent dictator* — and why, applied to a boss or an empire, it can turn quietly sardonic. *Kind* is warm and equal; **benevolent** is gracious and slightly above you.

⚠️ **benevolent ≠ beneficent.**
- **benevolent** = *wishes* good (the intention)
- **beneficent** = *does* good (the deed)

A benevolent millionaire means well; a beneficent one has funded the hospital.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Bene-VOLENT wills you well; MAL-evolent wills you ill.**

The shared `-volent` is *wishing*, so both words are about what someone intends toward you, and only the prefix decides which way.

### In sentences
1. The foundation's **benevolent** intentions were never in doubt; its accounting was another matter entirely.
2. My landlord's in a **benevolent** mood this week — he actually fixed the boiler.

### In the wild
In software, the person who founded a project and still has final say is the **BDFL — Benevolent Dictator For Life**, the title Python's community gave Guido van Rossum. Total control, exercised kindly: the word doing exactly the work it always does.

### Where it comes from
Latin **bene** (*well*) + **volens**, from **velle** (*to wish, to will*).

**bene-** pays out everywhere: *benefit, benefactor, benediction* (well-speaking), *benign, benevolent*. And **velle** gives you *volition, voluntary, volunteer* — all about the will.

Flip the prefix to *mal-* (*badly*) and you get the exact mirror: **malevolent**, *malediction*, *malign*, *malefactor*.

# **berate** *(v.)* — **bih-RAYT**

### Means
To **scold someone harshly and at length** — angry, loud, usually in front of other people.

Length and heat are what make it *berate* rather than anything milder. A **rebuke** can be one cold sentence; **censure** is formal and written down; **admonish** is almost gentle, a warning with care in it. Berating is a tirade — and the word quietly indicts the berater too. Nobody berates with dignity; the verb implies someone who has lost their temper and is enjoying the volume.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Be-RATE** — he's giving you a rating, out loud, at length, and it's one star.

The hidden word *rate* keeps the idea of a verdict being pronounced over you, and "at length" is the part that separates berating from a quick telling-off.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal noted that the officer had **berated** the recruits for a full hour, well past any disciplinary purpose.
2. She **berated** the delivery guy for ten minutes before realising she'd typed the wrong address herself.

### In the wild
Standard sports-desk vocabulary: a manager gets sent off *for berating the referee* — the exact word headlines reach for when someone won't stop shouting.

### Where it comes from
**be-** (thoroughly, all over) + **rate**, an old English verb meaning *to chide* — so, literally, "to scold all over."

That **be-** prefix is worth owning; it means *thoroughly* across a whole family: **belabour** (beat a point to death), **besiege**, **bewilder**, **bemoan**, **bespatter**. It's the prefix of overdoing it.

# **buoyant** *(adj.)* — **BOY-uhnt**

*Americans often say BOO-yuhnt, and call the object a BOO-ee; Brits say BOY throughout.*

### Means
Three linked senses: **able to float** (or to keep other things afloat); **cheerful and light-hearted**; and, of markets or demand, **rising, healthy, doing well**.

The emotional sense isn't loud joy — it's *unsinkable* joy. **Ebullient** bubbles over at you; **buoyant** simply refuses to go down. It implies pressure that should have sunk the person and didn't, so it carries a quiet compliment about resilience rather than volume. A buoyant mood survives bad news; a buoyant economy survives a bad quarter.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's a **buoy** sitting inside **buoyant** — shove one underwater and it pops straight back up.

That pop-back-up is the whole word: not merely happy, but impossible to hold down.

### In sentences
1. Despite the verdict, his spirits remained oddly **buoyant**, as though the worst news had failed to reach him.
2. She came out of the interview **buoyant** — practically skipping to the car.

### In the wild
Financial news lives on this word: "**buoyant** demand," "a **buoyant** housing market" — the sense of an economy bobbing up rather than sinking.

# **buttress** *(v. / n.)* — **BUT-riss**

*Not "buh-TRESS" — the stress lands on the first syllable and the second mumbles, exactly like mattress.*

### Means
**Noun:** a stone or brick support built **against** the outside of a wall to stop it collapsing outward.
**Verb:** to strengthen or prop something up — usually an argument, a claim, a case, a regime.

On the GRE it is almost always the verb, and almost always figurative: you **buttress a claim with evidence**. The word carries its architecture with it — the support comes from **outside**, is added **deliberately**, and quietly implies the thing couldn't stand on its own. *Bolster* can just boost morale; *buttress* is structural and load-bearing. And note the collocation: you buttress a **position**, not a person.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **butt**ress **butts** up against the wall — pushing back from outside so the wall doesn't fall.

The hidden *butt* (to shove against) tells you the support is external pressure applied to something already leaning.

### In sentences
1. The dissertation **buttressed** its central claim with three decades of census data.
2. Don't walk in with a hunch — **buttress** it with numbers or they'll tear it apart.

### In the wild
**Notre-Dame de Paris.** When it burned in 2019, every news report went straight to the *flying buttresses* — the stone arms bracing the walls from outside, the only reason the shell stayed standing.

### Where it comes from
Old French **bouter** = *"to thrust, to push against."* That single root pushes through a useful family: **abut** (to push up against — adjoining land), **rebut** (to push *back* against a claim), **butt** (heads), even **button** (thrust through a hole).

So *buttress, abut, rebut* are all one idea — force applied against something.

- **Meaning cluster** · [Prop up, add to, turn up](groups.md#meaning__prop-up-and-strengthen) — with *bolster, augment, amplify*
- **Second meaning** · [Siege engineering inside an argument](groups.md#second-meaning__siege-engineering-inside-an-argument) — with *bolster, undermine, undercut, assail, barrage, entrenched*

# **condone** *(v.)* — **kuhn-DOHN**

### Means
To **accept or overlook something wrong** — to let an offence pass without protest or punishment.

You can only condone what you already consider bad; condoning a kindness is nonsense. And it stops short of approval: to condone is to *not object*, to look away, to tolerate. That's why it lives overwhelmingly in the negative — *"I do not condone this"* — where the speaker is drawing a line between themselves and someone else's conduct. Silence from someone with the power to act is the classic way of condoning.

⚠️ **condone ≠ condemn.** Near-identical openings, opposite verdicts: **condone** = let it slide; **condemn** = denounce it. If a sentence sounds harsh, the word is *condemn*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **con-DONE** — you treat the wrongdoing as **done**, over with, not worth raising.

The hidden word *done* carries the nuance exactly: not "well done," just done — let it pass.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal held that a government which fails to prosecute such abuses effectively **condones** them.
2. Look, I'm not saying I **condone** what he did — I just don't think he should lose his job over it.

### In the wild
The boilerplate of every corporate statement and celebrity apology: *"We do not condone this behaviour."* It's the phrase institutions reach for when they need to be seen not-looking-away.

### Where it comes from
Latin **condōnāre** = *"to give away, remit, forgive"* — *con-* (thoroughly) + **dōnāre** (to give). You forgive a debt by *giving it up*.

Same *dōnāre* gives **donate, donor, donation**, and **pardon** (*per-* + *dōnāre*, "to give thoroughly").

🚫 **Condemn** is unrelated: *con-* + **damnāre**, "to inflict loss on" — the root of *damn, damnation, indemnity*.

- **Second meaning** · [Innocent-Looking Words That Mean "To Tolerate"](groups.md#second-meaning__homely-words-that-mean-to-tolerate) — with *brook, abide, countenance, forbear*
- **Opposites** · [Clearing blame vs. pinning it on](groups.md#antonym__clearing-of-blame-vs-pinning-it-on) — with *exculpate, exonerate, extenuating, indictment, impugn, stigmatize, reproach, censure*

# **contravene** *(v.)* — **kon-truh-VEEN**

### Means
**To act in violation of** a law, rule, treaty or regulation. In a second, more abstract sense: to conflict with or contradict a principle, claim or finding.

This is the vocabulary of compliance, and it wants a *written* object. You break a promise and you betray a friend, but you **contravene Article 5**. The tone is cool and procedural — no anger in it — because the offence is against the text, and what follows is a penalty, not a scolding.

⚠️ **contravene ≠ circumvent.**
- **contravene** = *break* the rule head-on
- **circumvent** = *get around* the rule without technically breaking it

The bank that lends to a sanctioned firm contravenes the sanctions; the bank that routes the money through a shell company circumvents them.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Contra**vene needs a **contract** — you can only contravene something someone wrote down.

If the thing being broken isn't a clause, code, statute or treaty, the word is wrong.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal held that the deportations **contravened** the state's obligations under the refugee convention.
2. Pretty sure hanging a TV on that wall **contravenes** about four clauses of my lease.

### In the wild
A newsroom stock phrase: **"contravenes the Geneva Conventions."** When a broadcaster reaches for *contravene* instead of *breaks*, it's signalling that a formal legal instrument — not a moral one — has been violated.

### Where it comes from
Latin **contra** (*against*) + **venīre** (*to come*) — literally *"to come against."*

**venīre** is a high-yield GRE root: **intervene** (come between), **convene** (come together), **prevent** (come before), **circumvent** (come around), **advent** (a coming), **venue** (the place you come to).

# **denounce** *(v.)* — **dih-NOWNS**

### Means
To **condemn something publicly and openly** — to stand up and declare a person, act, or idea evil, false, or intolerable. Secondary sense: to **inform against** someone to the authorities ("he was denounced to the police").

Volume is built into the word. You can *disapprove* privately and *criticise* mildly, but you cannot denounce quietly — it's a public act, aimed at an audience, and it puts the speaker's own reputation behind the charge. It also outranks *criticise* in heat: criticism finds fault, denunciation demands that others share the outrage.

⚠️ **denounce ≠ renounce.**
- **denounce** = publicly attack *someone else's* wrongdoing
- **renounce** = formally give up *something of your own* (a claim, a throne, a faith)

A cardinal may denounce heresy; a king renounces the throne.

### Trick to lock it in
> **De-NOUNCE = announce, turned against someone.**

Same *-nounce* as **announce**, so it's inherently a public broadcast — the *de-* just aims it downward at a target.

### In sentences
1. In a speech to the assembly, she **denounced** the new statute as a licence for the powerful to rob the poor.
2. He went online and **denounced** the whole company by name, which felt great for about a day.

### In the wild
Orwell's *1984* runs on the second sense — children are trained to **denounce** their own parents to the Thought Police, and Parsons is turned in by his seven-year-old daughter.

### Where it comes from
Latin **nuntiare** = *"to announce"* (from *nuntius*, a messenger), plus **de-** *"down, against."*

The root is a GRE workhorse: **announce**, **pronounce**, **renounce**, **enunciate**, **nuncio** (the Pope's messenger-ambassador). Note the spelling shift in the noun — *denounce* → **denunciation**, where the Latin *nunti-* resurfaces.

# **despotic** *(adj.)* — **dess-POT-ik**

*The noun is despot = DESS-put, not "DEE-spot".*

### Means
Exercising **absolute power arbitrarily and oppressively** — the rule of one person whose will is the only law.

*Authoritarian* describes a system; *despotic* points at a person. The word carries **whim** as much as cruelty: a despot doesn't just oppress, he oppresses *however he feels like today*, with nobody able to say no. That's why it scales down so easily — a despotic manager, a despotic father, a despotic committee chair. Always an accusation, never neutral, and slightly grander and more literary than *bossy* or *dictatorial*.

### Trick to lock it in
> des-**POT**-ic — the stress lands on **POT**, the same *pot* as **potent** and **potentate**: one person holds the whole pot of power.

Say it out loud and the emphasis itself tells you where the power sits — all of it in one hand.

### In sentences
1. The reforms were less a concession than a performance: **despotic** rule dressed in the vocabulary of consent.
2. He runs that kitchen on **despotic** lines — nobody plates a thing until he's inspected it.

### In the wild
History class gives you the fixed phrase **"enlightened despot"** — Frederick the Great, Catherine the Great — rulers who kept every scrap of absolute power and simply promised to use it well.

### Where it comes from
Greek **despotēs** = *"master of the house"* — literally *house-lord*. That's the nuance in miniature: a despot treats a whole country as his private household, where his word simply is the rule.

The **-pot-** is the power root: **potent, potential, omnipotent, potentate, plenipotentiary**.

# **deviate** *(v. (also n. / adj., rare))* — **DEE-vee-ayt**

### Means
To **turn aside from an established course, standard, or norm** — a route, a plan, a protocol, an average.

The word only works if there *is* a fixed path to leave; it always implies a reference line. That makes it cooler and more measurable than its neighbours: you **stray** aimlessly, you **digress** in speech, but you **deviate** from something specified — the flight plan, the recipe, the mean. Left alone it's neutral, even clinical (engineers and statisticians live in it). The disapproval is imported by context: *deviate from procedure* is an accusation, and the shadow of **deviant** darkens the word whenever the norm being left is a social one.

### Trick to lock it in
> **de-VIA-te** — there's a **via** sitting in the middle: you go *off the via*, off the way.

Spotting *via* reminds you the word needs a road to leave — no established path, no deviation.

### In sentences
1. The pilot **deviated** from the filed route to skirt a line of thunderstorms.
2. Follow the recipe exactly — the one time I **deviated**, it came out like glue.

### In the wild
**Standard deviation** — the stats measure you've met a hundred times. It's literally the average amount your data wanders off the mean.

### Where it comes from
Latin **de-** (*away from*) + **via** (*road, way*) — "off the road."

*Via* is a workhorse root: **devious** (off-road, hence sneaky), **obvious** (*ob-via* — lying in the way, so you can't miss it), **trivial** (*tri-via* — the three-way crossroads where people swapped idle gossip), **envoy**, **voyage**, **viaduct**.

# **disinterested** *(adj.)* — **dis-IN-truh-stid**

### Means
**Impartial** — having no personal stake, no side to gain by, no axe to grind.

This is a compliment. A **disinterested** referee, auditor, trustee or judge may care deeply about the matter and still be trusted, because nothing in the outcome touches their pocket or their pride. It describes a *position*, not a mood: you can be riveted by a case and disinterested in it at the same time. Reach for it wherever fairness depends on the absence of a stake.

⚠️ **disinterested ≠ uninterested.**
- **disinterested** = *neutral*, no stake — a virtue
- **uninterested** = *bored*, no attention — a shrug

The GRE tests this pair constantly, and the "bored" reading of *disinterested* is the wrong answer.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **disinterested** judge has no *money* on the game; an **uninterested** one has no *eyes* on it.

*Dis-* strips away the old sense of *interest* — a financial stake — while *un-* strips away attention.

### In sentences
1. The estate was placed in the hands of a **disinterested** third party, so neither brother could accuse the other of tampering.
2. Don't ask his best mate to score the argument — get someone **disinterested**.

### In the wild
Boardroom language keeps the strict sense alive: when a company approves a deal involving one of its own executives, the bylaws demand a vote of the **disinterested directors** — the ones with no financial stake, who must recuse-proof the decision.

### Where it comes from
*Interest* comes from Latin **interesse**, *"to be between, to concern"* (**inter-** between + **esse** to be) — and in English it first meant a **legal or financial share** in something. So *disinterested* = **holding no share**. The modern "curiosity" sense of *interest* came later, which is exactly why the two words drifted apart.

**esse** also gives you *essence, essential, entity, present*.

# **escalate** *(v.)* — **ES-kuh-layt**

### Means
To **increase by stages** in intensity, scale, or seriousness — or to **push a matter up to a higher authority**.

Two flavours, one shape. A quarrel *escalates* into a brawl, a border skirmish into a war, a repair bill into a mortgage: each step provokes the next, and reversing is far harder than climbing. That step-by-step, self-feeding quality is what separates it from *increase* (flat, quantitative) and *soar* (fast and often welcome). Escalation is rarely good news.

The office sense is bureaucratic rather than violent — "I'll **escalate** this to my manager" means moving the problem up a rung, not making it worse. Same ladder, calmer building.

### Trick to lock it in
> **An escalator has no jump button** — it lifts you one step at a time, and you can't easily get off.

*Escalate* is stepwise, self-continuing intensification — not a sudden leap, and not something you can simply step back down from.

### In sentences
1. Both governments insisted they sought peace, even as troop movements **escalated** the crisis week by week.
2. It was a joke about parking, and somehow it **escalated** into three people not speaking at Christmas.

### In the wild
The **escalator** at any mall is the word made literal — and its corporate cousin, "let me **escalate** this to my supervisor," is what you hear ten minutes into a support call.

### Where it comes from
Backwards from what you'd guess: **escalator** came first (a 1900 trademark, from *escalade* — scaling a wall with ladders), and **escalate** was cut out of it decades later, hitting its stride in the Cold War: *escalation*, *de-escalate*, *escalatory*.

Underneath is Latin **scala** = *"ladder, stairs"* — which also gives **scale** (to climb), **escalade**, and **echelon** (a rung of the hierarchy).

# **exorcise** *(v.)* — **EK-sor-syze**

*Also spelled exorcize; the noun is exorcism (EK-sor-siz-uhm).*

### Means
To **drive out an evil spirit** by ritual, prayer or command — and, far more often on the GRE, to **rid yourself of something that haunts you**: a memory, a guilt, a reputation, an old influence that still has a grip.

The figurative sense carries the ghost with it. You don't *exorcise* clutter or spare furniture; you exorcise what clings — something unwanted, faintly shameful, and hard to shake. It also implies effort and something like ceremony: writing the book, burning the letters, winning the rematch. Compare plain *remove* (mechanical) or *dispel* (an illusion simply evaporates) — exorcising is a **struggle against a thing that resists leaving**.

⚠️ **exorcise ≠ exercise.** One letter apart, opposite worlds: **exorcise** (with an **o**) casts out demons; **exercise** works the body or *uses* a right ("exercise caution", "exercise the option").

### Trick to lock it in
> Look inside: ex**ORC**ise — you're kicking the **orcs** out of the house.

The hidden ORC is only in the demon-word, never in *exercise*, and it captures the sense of expelling something monstrous that won't leave on its own.

### In sentences
1. The memoir was less a confession than an attempt to **exorcise** a childhood she had never been able to speak of aloud.
2. Beating them in the final finally **exorcised** thirty years of losing to that lot.

### In the wild
**The Exorcist** (1973) — the priest is there to *exorcise* the demon, which is exactly the job description.

### Where it comes from
Greek **ex-** ("out") + **horkos** ("oath") → *exorkizein*, literally "to bind by oath" — the exorcist expels the spirit by **swearing an oath at it** ("I command you, in the name of…"). That's why the word implies a formula, not just a shove.

# **finicky** *(adj.)* — **FIN-ik-ee**

### Means
**Of a person:** fussy and hard to please over small details. **Of a thing or task:** temperamental, demanding fiddly attention to work at all — *a finicky carburettor, a finicky recipe.*

Where *fastidious* can be a compliment and *punctilious* sounds dignified, **finicky** never flatters — but it's mild, more eye-roll than insult. It's the word for someone who sends the salad back because the dressing is on it, and the tone is domestic and slightly comic rather than scornful. Note the second sense: things can be finicky too, which is the one the GRE likes.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Finicky** ends in **-icky** — to a finicky eater, everything is *icky*.

The childish sound of "icky" carries the nuance exactly: the objections are petty and small, so we find them faintly ridiculous rather than offensive.

### In sentences
1. The curator was **finicky** about humidity, rejecting three galleries before consenting to lend the panel.
2. Don't bother making him dinner — he's so **finicky** he won't eat anything the sauce has touched.

### In the wild
Recipe writers use it constantly as a warning label: hollandaise, macarons and soufflés are all described as **finicky** — meaning the technique will punish you for the smallest slip.

### Where it comes from
From older **finical**, built on **fine** — someone who insists on excessively *fine* distinctions. Same root branch as *refine, finesse, finery*.

# **foil** *(v. / n.)* — **FOYL**

### Means
**Verb:** to stop a plan, plot, or attempt from succeeding.
**Noun:** a person or thing whose contrast makes another's qualities stand out (Watson is a **foil** to Holmes).

The verb wants an *attempt already in motion* — you foil a robbery, an escape, a coup, not someone's vague ambitions. It's the headline word for last-minute frustration of a scheme ("Police foil plot"), and it carries a faint whiff of the adventure story: the foiler is the good guy, the foiled a bit ridiculous. Compare **thwart** (heavier, more deliberate obstruction) and **frustrate** (drier, often about systems rather than schemes).

The noun sense has nothing to do with blocking. A foil doesn't compete with the hero — it *flatters* by comparison, throwing the other's traits into relief.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Kitchen foil does both jobs: wrap a scheme and it suffocates; slip it under a diamond and the diamond blazes.**

One word, two functions — smother the plot, or sit behind something and make it shine.

### In sentences
1. Customs officers **foiled** the smuggling operation hours before the shipment was due to leave the port.
2. Ravi's whole weekend plan was **foiled** by one email from his boss.

### In the wild
**\"Curses, foiled again!\"** — the stock cartoon-villain line (Snidely Whiplash and every moustache-twirler since). That's the verb's exact flavour: a scheme blocked at the last second, and the schemer left looking silly.

### Where it comes from
Two different words that collided in spelling.

The **verb** is Old French *fouler*, "to trample" — you tread a plan underfoot.

The **noun** is Latin **folium**, "leaf" — a *leaf* of thin metal. Jewellers set a bright foil behind a gem so it caught more light, and that literal trick gave us the literary meaning: the character placed behind the hero to make him sparkle. Same root: **foliage, portfolio, folio, exfoliate, trefoil**.

# **intertwined** *(adj. / v. (past of intertwine))* — **in-ter-TWEYEND**

### Means
Twisted or wound together — literally, like two vines or strands; figuratively, **so closely linked that neither can be understood or dealt with alone**.

The figurative sense is the one the GRE cares about. *Connected* is a line between two separate things; **intertwined** means the boundary is gone — fates, histories, economies, storylines that you can no longer pull apart cleanly. It's neutral in tone: an intertwined pair can be lovers or hostile neighbours. What it always implies is **mutual, inseparable involvement**, not mere proximity.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside it: inter-**TWIN**-ed — two things twinned so tightly you can't say where one ends.

The buried *twin* gives you both halves of the meaning — twoness and inseparability — which is exactly what separates *intertwined* from plain *connected*.

### In sentences
1. The economic fortunes of the two nations became so **intertwined** that neither could impose sanctions without wounding itself.
2. Their friend groups are completely **intertwined** now — you can't invite one person without inviting eleven.

### In the wild
Every diagram of **DNA** is this word made literal: two strands of the double helix intertwined, and you can't read one without the other.

### Where it comes from
**inter-** (*between*) + **twine**, from Old English **twīn** = *"double thread"*, straight from **twā**, "two."

That "two" root runs through more of English than you'd guess: **twin**, **twist**, **twill** (two-thread weave), **between**, and **twilight** (the "two lights" of dusk).

# **inundate** *(v.)* — **IN-un-dayt**

*Also heard as in-UN-dayt; first-syllable stress is the safer default.*

### Means
To **flood** — literally, to cover land with water; figuratively, to swamp someone with far more of something than they can deal with.

The force of the word is **sheer volume arriving from outside**, not difficulty. A hundred routine emails inundate you; one brutal problem doesn't. It usually arrives passive and plural — *inundated with calls, requests, applications, complaints* — which quietly casts you as the shore and the stuff as the tide. Next to *overwhelm* (which can be emotional, or done by a single force) *inundate* is specifically about quantity pouring in.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden in the middle: **UND** — the same wave as *undulate* — and you go **und**er it.

Wave after wave is the point: inundation is measured in volume, not in weight.

### In sentences
1. After the levee failed, three thousand acres of farmland were **inundated** within the hour.
2. I asked one question in the group chat and got **inundated** with replies — forty messages before lunch.

### In the wild
It's the word flood reporting actually uses: BBC and local news say homes are **"inundated"** by floodwater, never just "wet" — the literal sense you hear every storm season is the same one behind "inundated with emails."

### Where it comes from
Latin **inundare** = *"to overflow"* — *in-* (onto) + **unda** (*wave*).

That wave washes through a useful family: **undulate** (move in waves), **abundant** (*ab-undare*, overflowing with), **redundant** (*re-undare*, overflowing **again** — surplus to need), and **surround**.

# **ironclad** *(adj. / n.)* — **EYE-ern-klad**

### Means
**Adj.:** so firm it cannot be broken, challenged, or wriggled out of — **an ironclad alibi, contract, rule, guarantee**.
**Noun (historical):** a 19th-century warship plated in iron.

The word promises *invulnerability to attack*, not mere strength. That's why it clings to things people are actively trying to poke holes in: a lawyer's case, a suspect's alibi, a company's warranty. Say a rule is **strict** and you describe how it's enforced; say it's **ironclad** and you're saying no exception exists to find. Applied to a person's rules or opinions it can tip cold — unyielding where a little give was wanted.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Iron has no holes — so an ironclad promise has no loophole.**

Notice what the word attaches to — alibi, contract, guarantee, rule — always something an opponent is hunting for a gap in.

### In sentences
1. The prosecution's case rested on an **ironclad** chain of custody that the defence could not disturb.
2. Relax, I've got an **ironclad** excuse — I was on a flight with no wifi.

### In the wild
Every "**ironclad** money-back guarantee" in advertising — the phrase exists precisely to tell you there's no small print to hide in.

# **jeopardize** *(v.)* — **JEP-er-dyze**

*Spelled *jeo-*, said "jep" — never "jee-OP." British spelling: jeopardise.*

### Means
To **put at risk** — to expose something to danger, loss, or failure.

The object is almost always a **good you already hold**: a career, a ceasefire, funding, a reputation, someone's safety. *Endanger* points at bodies and lives; *jeopardize* points at stakes and standing. It also implies **your own action** did it — you jeopardize a deal by mouthing off, not by bad luck. And the loss is only threatened, never certain: the thing is still salvageable, which is why the word so often arrives as a warning ("you're jeopardizing everything") rather than an obituary.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hear the **prize** in jeopar-**dize** — you can only jeopardize something you've already won.

The word needs an asset in hand: your job, your visa, your lead, your marriage.

### In sentences
1. A single leaked memo **jeopardized** eighteen months of quiet diplomacy.
2. Don't **jeopardize** your whole scholarship over one stupid party.

### In the wild
The game show **Jeopardy!** is named for exactly this: every answer you give puts money you already have on the line.

### Where it comes from
Old French **jeu parti** = *"a divided game"* — a chess position or wager so evenly balanced that either side could win. An even-odds game became an uncertain outcome, and uncertainty became risk.

**jeu** comes from Latin *iocus*, "jest" — the same root as **joke, jocular, jocose**. **parti** from *partire*, "to divide" — **part, partition, apartment**.

# **mercurial** *(adj.)* — **mer-KYOOR-ee-uhl**

### Means
Liable to sudden, unpredictable changes of mood or behaviour — volatile, quick-changing, hard to pin down.

Tone separates it from its neighbours. **Fickle** implies disloyalty; **volatile** implies danger; **capricious** implies whim exercised over other people. **Mercurial** carries a flicker of glamour — it's the word for the brilliant, quick-witted, temperamental one, so it often attaches to artists, strikers and star CEOs. The person is exhausting, but you're half-impressed.

⚠️ **mercurial ≠ mercenary.** Same *merc-* root, opposite charge: *mercurial* = **moody and unpredictable**; *mercenary* = **coldly motivated by money**. A mercurial manager throws chairs; a mercenary one sells you out.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Mercury in a thermometer** — shoots up, drops back, and never sits still.

The only metal that won't hold a shape gives you both halves of the word: rapid swings, and a bright silvery shine that makes you watch anyway.

### In sentences
1. His **mercurial** temperament made him magnificent in rehearsal and impossible in negotiation.
2. She's brilliant, but she's **mercurial** — text her at ten and you'll get a different answer than at nine.

### In the wild
Football commentary runs on this word: the **"mercurial"** No. 10 — Gascoigne, Ronaldinho, Mbappé — dazzling one week, invisible the next. If you've heard a pundit say it, you've already got the meaning.

### Where it comes from
From **Mercury**, the Roman messenger god — winged heels, fastest of the gods, patron of merchants, travellers and thieves. Hence the metal **mercury** (quicksilver), which rolls away from your finger, and the planet, the quickest around the sun.

The root is Latin **merx, mercis** = *goods, merchandise* — which also gives **commerce, merchant, market, mercenary**. Speed and slipperiness came from the god; money came from the root.

# **oblivious** *(adj.)* — **uh-BLIV-ee-uhs**

### Means
Completely unaware of something — it simply hasn't registered. Usually followed by *to* (or, in older/formal use, *of*).

The word is about a **blank**, not a choice. Someone *indifferent* has noticed and doesn't care; someone *obtuse* has been told and can't grasp it; someone **oblivious** never took it in at all. That's why it usually arrives with a faint sigh of reproach — you weren't malicious, you were just somewhere else while the room filled with smoke.

An older, more literary sense survives in formal prose: *forgetful of*, having let something slip out of mind entirely — "oblivious of past slights."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Obliv**ious lives in **obliv**ion — the fact didn't get ignored, it never arrived.

Oblivion is the void where things are forgotten, and an oblivious person is standing in it while everyone else watches the thing happen.

### In sentences
1. Absorbed in the manuscript, she remained **oblivious** to the storm that had emptied the reading room around her.
2. He kept telling the joke, totally **oblivious** to the fact that the guy it was about was standing right there.

### In the wild
Harry Potter's memory-wiping charm — ***Obliviate!*** — is the same Latin root doing exactly what it says: the memory is erased, and the victim walks off oblivious.

### Where it comes from
Latin **oblivisci** = *"to forget"* — from *ob-* + the root of *levis*, "smooth": to **wipe the wax tablet smooth** so nothing is written on it any more.

That's the whole word in one image, and it also gives you **oblivion** — the state of being wiped clean, forgotten, gone.

# **perpetrate** *(v.)* — **PUR-puh-trayt**

### Means
To **carry out or commit** something — a crime, a fraud, a hoax, an atrocity.

The object is never innocent. You *commit* a crime or *commit* to a plan, but you only **perpetrate** things the speaker disapproves of: no one perpetrates a kindness. It also carries a whiff of officialdom — police reports, court transcripts, journalism — which is why it turns funny when aimed at something trivial: *who perpetrated this haircut?*

⚠️ **perpetrate ≠ perpetuate.**
- **perpetrate** (PUR-puh-trayt) = *do* the bad thing
- **perpetuate** (per-PECH-oo-ayt) = *keep it going* forever

You perpetrate a fraud; you perpetuate a stereotype.

### Trick to lock it in
> Only a **perp** can **perp**etrate.

The cop-show word *perp* is hiding in the first syllable, and it tells you the object is always a wrongdoing — never a favour, never a plan.

### In sentences
1. The commission concluded that the massacre had been **perpetrated** by units acting under direct orders.
2. Someone **perpetrated** a group chat at 2am and now I have 400 unread messages.

### In the wild
**"Perp walk"** — the parade of a handcuffed suspect past the cameras. *Perp* is just a clipped **perpetrator**, and every police procedural you've ever half-watched runs on it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **per-** (*thoroughly*) + **patrare** (*to bring about*) — to see a deed all the way through.

The confusable twin comes from somewhere else entirely: **perpetuate** ← *perpetuus*, "continuous," which also gives **perpetual** and **perpetuity**. Different root, different job.

# **plaintive** *(adj.)* — **PLAYN-tiv**

### Means
Sounding sad in a way that begs for sympathy — mournful, wistful, slightly wounded.

It is almost always about **a sound or an expression**, not a situation: a plaintive cry, a plaintive melody, a plaintive look. *Mournful* is grief with weight behind it; *plaintive* is thinner and higher — it appeals rather than laments. And it carries a hint of quiet complaint, of asking to be pitied, which is why a child's "but *why*?" is plaintive while a widow's silence is not.

⚠️ **plaintive ≠ plaintiff.**
- **plaintive** = sounding sad (adjective)
- **plaintiff** = the person who brings a lawsuit (noun)

Same root, same sound — one whimpers, the other sues.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Plaintive** has **plaint** in it — a complaint, but *sung*, not shouted.

The word is a grievance turned into a sound, which is why it always applies to a tone rather than to a person's circumstances.

### In sentences
1. A **plaintive** oboe carried the melody, thin above the strings, as though it were asking the orchestra a question no one would answer.
2. He gave a **plaintive** little "you never text me anymore" and went back to his phone.

### In the wild
It's a staple of music writing: reviewers reach for **plaintive** for a lone harmonica, a minor-key falsetto, or a Coldplay piano line — the sound that's sad *and* pleading rather than merely slow.

### Where it comes from
Latin **plangere** = *"to beat the breast, to lament loudly."* Via French *plainte*, a lament, it gives a tight family: **complaint**, **plaintiff** (the one who brings the grievance), and **plangent** (a loud, resounding, mournful sound).

Note the split: **plangent** kept the *loudness* of the root; **plaintive** kept only the *sorrow*.

# **poignant** *(adj.)* — **POYN-yuhnt**

*The g is silent — POYN-yuhnt, or plainly POY-nuhnt. Never "POYG-nant."*

### Means
Emotionally piercing — moving in a way that hurts a little, usually because something tender is mixed with loss, distance, or time running out.

Sad is the temperature; **poignant** is the *sharpness*. A funeral is sad. The dead man's dog still waiting at the door is poignant. The word almost always carries a bittersweet double note — sweetness that stings precisely because it's gone or going — and it's a term of praise when applied to art: a poignant film has earned its ache rather than manufactured it. Reserve it for the small, precise detail that pricks, not for general gloom.

⚠️ **poignant ≠ piquant.** Same Latin root, opposite department.
- **poignant** (POYN-yuhnt) = sharp to the **heart** — bittersweet, moving
- **piquant** (PEE-kunt) = sharp to the **tongue** — tangy, zesty, pleasantly stimulating

A piquant sauce; a poignant goodbye.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Poignant** is **point** with a silent g — it doesn't sadden you broadly, it pricks you at one exact spot.

The pinpoint is the whole nuance: poignancy comes from one piercing detail, not from a general fog of sadness.

### In sentences
1. The most **poignant** exhibit was not the wreckage itself but a child's shoe, catalogued and labelled beside it.
2. I was fine until the last five minutes, and then it got so **poignant** I had to pretend something was in my eye.

### In the wild
It's the film critic's default word for a bittersweet drama — scan any Rotten Tomatoes blurb wall and you'll hit "a poignant meditation on…" within three reviews. That's exactly its native habitat: praise for art that aches.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pungere** = *"to prick, to pierce"* — through French *poindre*. High-yield root: **puncture, punctual** (hitting the exact point in time), **punctuate, pungent, expunge, compunction** (a prick of conscience), and **point** itself.

So poignancy is literally a piercing — the emotion goes *in*.

# **quiescent** *(adj.)* — **kwee-ES-uhnt**

*Also heard as kwy-ES-uhnt; both are accepted.*

### Means
Inactive, still, at rest — **temporarily dormant rather than dead**.

The word is about *activity*, not sound: a quiescent volcano isn't silent, it's not erupting. And it almost always carries the hint that the thing could start up again — a quiescent disease, a quiescent market, a quiescent rivalry. **Dormant** suggests sleep waiting for a season; **quiescent** is the pause between bouts. Cool, clinical register — you'll meet it in medicine, geology and physics far more often than in conversation about people.

**quiescent ≠ acquiescent.** Same root, opposite job: *quiescent* = **doing nothing**; *acquiescent* = **going along with it**, agreeing without protest. A quiescent committee holds no meetings; an acquiescent one rubber-stamps everything.

### Trick to lock it in
> **quie(t) + -escent**, like conval**escent** — resting *for now*, not finished.

That **-escent** ending (convalescent, adolescent, effervescent) marks a passing state, which is exactly the temporariness quiescent smuggles in.

### In sentences
1. Between eruptions the volcano lay **quiescent** for two centuries, its slopes green and farmed.
2. The group chat's been **quiescent** since the exams — give it a week and it'll be unbearable again.

### In the wild
Doctors use it constantly: a **quiescent** disease — Crohn's, lupus, leukaemia — is one that's calm right now but still there. It's the word chosen precisely *because* it doesn't mean "cured."

### Where it comes from
Latin **quies, quietis** = *"rest."* It unlocks a cluster: **quiet**, **acquiesce** (come to rest with someone's view), **requiem** (a mass for rest), **tranquil** (*trans-* + *quies*, thoroughly at rest).

# **reiterate** *(v.)* — **ree-IT-uh-rayt**

### Means
To **say something again**, usually to drive it home.

Nearly always about *words*, not actions — you repeat a gesture, but you reiterate a **point**. And it's self-conscious: *repeat* is neutral and mechanical, while *reiterate* is a speaker deliberately restating a position, often with a flicker of impatience that it needed restating at all. "Let me reiterate" means *you should already know this*. Push further and you get **belabour**, which is repetition past the point of usefulness.

⚠️ **reiterate ≠ iterate.**
- **reiterate** = *say it again*
- **iterate** = *go through the cycle again*, refining each time (iterate on a design, iterate over a list)

You reiterate a demand. You iterate a prototype.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Re**iterate already means "again again" — and that redundancy *is* the word.

Latin *iterum* already means "again," so the extra **re-** built into the word mirrors exactly when you use it: the second time, because the first didn't land.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal **reiterated** that no such right exists under the statute — a position it had stated plainly twice before.
2. I'll **reiterate**, since apparently nobody opened the email: the deadline is Friday.

### In the wild
**"Let me reiterate"** is podium language — the stock opener of press briefings, earnings calls and diplomatic statements, where it politely means *I have said this already and I am saying it again*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **iterum** = *"again"* → **iterāre**, "to do again," plus a redundant **re-**.

That root runs straight into tech and maths: **iterate**, **iteration**, **iterative** — one loop after another.

### Also sits in
- **Meaning cluster** · [Say it again](groups.md#meaning__say-it-again) — with *recapitulate, perpetuate, redundant*

# **subside** *(v.)* — **suhb-SYDE**

### Means
To **sink back down** to a lower or normal level — floodwaters, a swelling, ground under a building — and, by extension, for anything intense to **gradually die away**: pain, noise, panic, applause, a fever, a scandal.

Two things define it. First, it needs a **prior rise**: something swelled, surged, or flared, and now it's settling back. Second, it happens **of its own accord**. *Suppress* and *quell* need someone doing the work; *subside* is what a thing does when left alone, on its own timetable. It's also gradual, never abrupt — a riot **subsides** into muttering; it doesn't subside in a snap.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tide** → sub**side**: it goes down on its own, and nobody drains it.

The rhyme fixes the two essentials — something that had risen is now falling, slowly and without help.

### In sentences
1. As the applause **subsided**, the conductor turned back to face the orchestra.
2. Give it two days — the swelling should **subside** on its own.

### In the wild
Straight out of any doctor's or pharmacist's mouth: *"Take these and the pain should subside within a few hours."* Nobody says *"the pain should cease"* — because *subside* is exactly the promise of a slow, unattended fade.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** (*down, under*) + **sīdere** (*to settle, sink down*), a cousin of **sedēre**, *"to sit."*

That sitting root is a GRE goldmine: **sediment** (what settles at the bottom), **reside** (sit back), **preside** (sit in front), **sedentary** (sitting), **assiduous** (sitting at it doggedly), **insidious** (sitting in ambush), **dissident** (sitting apart).

# **subsume** *(v.)* — **suhb-SOOM**

### Means
To **absorb something into a larger category or body**, so that it is treated as a part or instance of that larger thing.

It's a filing verb, not a destroying verb. *Absorb* and *swallow up* suggest the smaller thing dissolves; **subsume** says it still exists — it has simply lost its separate standing and now counts as a sub-case of something bigger. A department gets subsumed into a ministry, a claim gets subsumed under a general principle, three tribes get subsumed under one label. Cool, formal, faintly bureaucratic: the tone of reorganisation charts and philosophy papers, rarely of anger.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sub** + **sum** — your line item gets tucked *under* someone else's **sum**.

Nothing is deleted; it just stops being counted on its own and gets folded into a bigger total.

### In sentences
1. Later theorists **subsumed** these scattered observations under a single law of diminishing returns.
2. Our team got **subsumed** into Platform last quarter — same people, new org chart, no more standalone budget.

### In the wild
The word every acquisition announcement reaches for: the startup isn't shut down, it's *subsumed into* the parent company — brand retired, staff still there, now a line under someone else's P&L.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** (*under*) + **sumere** (*to take*) — literally "to take under."

**sumere** is a GRE workhorse: **assume** (take on), **presume** (take beforehand), **consume** (take up entirely), **resume** (take back up), **presumptuous** (taking too much).

# **surmount** *(v.)* — **ser-MOWNT**

### Means
To **overcome** a difficulty, obstacle, or limitation. (Literally, in architecture: to sit on top of something — *a dome surmounted by a golden cross*.)

What you surmount is an **obstacle, never an opponent** — barriers, odds, prejudice, poverty, a language gap. You *beat* a rival; you *surmount* the thing standing in your way. The image is vertical: the difficulty stays where it is and you get above it.

It's formal and quietly heroic — it concedes the obstacle was real, but claims no gloating. And you'll meet it most often wearing its negative coat: **insurmountable**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sur-** (over) + **mount** (climb) — you don't smash the wall, you end up standing on it.

The word is literally "climb over," which is why it takes obstacles rather than enemies as its object.

### In sentences
1. Having **surmounted** every procedural objection, the defence finally put its case to the jury.
2. She **surmounted** a fear of flying by booking twelve short-haul flights in a row.

### In the wild
Sports commentary lives on the negative form: a team goes three goals down and the lead becomes **"insurmountable."** Same word, same wall — just too high to climb.

### Where it comes from
Old French **sur-** (*over, above*, from Latin **super**) + **monter** (*to climb*, from **mons/montis**, "mountain").

That **sur-** prefix is a GRE workhorse — once you hear it as "over," you get **surpass** (go over), **surplus** (over the amount), **surfeit** (over-supply, to excess), **surcharge**, **surreal** (above the real), even **surname** (the name written over yours).

# **tangential** *(adj.)* — **tan-JEN-shuhl**

### Means
**Only glancingly related to the matter at hand** — touching it at one point and then heading off elsewhere. (Literal geometry sense: a tangent line touches a curve at exactly one point.)

It's a **soft dismissal**. *Irrelevant* denies any connection; *tangential* concedes a real one and then rules it out as beside the point — which is why academics and lawyers reach for it. Calling someone's remark tangential is politer than calling it irrelevant, and just as fatal. It also describes a *manner* of thinking: someone who is tangential wanders off the thread mid-conversation.

### Trick to lock it in
> A tangent **touches the circle once and keeps going** — tangential facts touch your point once, then leave.

The single point of contact is the whole idea: real contact, immediately abandoned.

### In sentences
1. The committee ruled the testimony **tangential** to the charge and struck it from the record.
2. Sorry, this is a bit **tangential**, but did anyone actually book the venue?

### In the wild
The everyday phrase **"going off on a tangent"** is this word doing its job — the speaker touched the topic once and shot off in a straight line away from it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tangere** = *"to touch."* One root, a long list: **tangible** (touchable), **tactile**, **contact**, **contagion** (touching-together disease), **intact** (*not* touched), **contingent** (touching on circumstances).
