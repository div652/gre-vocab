# **abrogate** *(v.)* — **AB-ruh-gayt**

*Noun form: abrogation — ab-ruh-GAY-shuhn.*

### Means
To **formally abolish or repeal** something that was officially in force — a law, a treaty, a constitutional provision, a right.

This is cancellation **from above, on the record**. You can't abrogate a habit or a friendship; the object has to be something that was enacted, and the subject has to be a body with the authority to unenact it — a parliament, a court, a state. Where *revoke* is everyday and *annul* says "it was never valid," *abrogate* says "it was valid, and we have struck it off." The word carries the weight of the instrument being destroyed, which is why it so often shows up when someone is angry about it.

⚠️ **abrogate ≠ arrogate.**
- **abrogate** = *ab-* away → you **cancel** a law
- **arrogate** = *ad-* toward → you **grab** a power that isn't yours (*he arrogated to himself the right to decide*)

One letter, opposite direction of travel.

### Trick to lock it in
> **AB**rogate = **AB**olish. Same two letters, same act, and both need someone in power to do it.

If the sentence's subject couldn't plausibly abolish the thing by decree, the word is wrong.

### In sentences
1. The incoming government moved to **abrogate** the trade treaty its predecessor had signed only eighteen months earlier.
2. You can't just **abrogate** the tenancy agreement because you found a better tenant.

### In the wild
India's **abrogation of Article 370** in August 2019 — the word was in every headline, every debate, for months. Note what it attached to: a constitutional provision, struck off by the state. Textbook usage.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ab-** (*away*) + **rogāre** (*to ask, to propose a law*). In Rome, to propose a bill was *rogāre lēgem*; to repeal it was *abrogāre* — to ask it away.

*Rogāre* is a workhorse root: **interrogate** (question between), **derogatory** (asking down, belittling), **prerogative** (asked first — the privilege claimed ahead of others), **surrogate**, **arrogate**.

# **aghast** *(adj.)* — **uh-GAST**

*British: uh-GAHST. The h is silent — never "ag-hast."*

### Means
Struck motionless by shock and **horror** — the frozen, open-mouthed reaction to something appalling.

Surprise is neutral, shock is clinical, but *aghast* always carries revulsion: you are horrified **at** something, usually something morally or physically ghastly. It also describes the face and the moment — a held expression, not a lasting mood. Grammatically it almost never sits before a noun: people are *aghast at* the news, but you don't meet "an aghast man."

**aghast ≠ agog.** Both are *a-* + one syllable, both describe a wide-eyed stare. **Aghast** = horrified. **Agog** = thrilled with eager anticipation. The crowd was agog for the verdict; the family was aghast at it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **agHASt** hides **GHOST** — you look like you've just seen one.

That face — frozen, drained, mouth open — is exactly what *aghast* names, and it explains the useless silent *h* in the spelling.

### In sentences
1. The committee sat **aghast** as the auditor read out, line by line, where the money had gone.
2. I was **aghast** — he microwaved the fish. In the open-plan office.

### Where it comes from
Old English **gāstan**, *"to terrify"* — same root as **gāst**, *spirit*, which gives us **ghost** and **ghastly**. The silent *h* was smuggled in later purely by imitation of *ghost*.

The Germanic cousin *Geist* extends the family: **poltergeist** (noisy ghost), **zeitgeist** (spirit of the age).

# **apprise** *(v.)* — **uh-PRYZE**

*Rhymes with surprise, not with praise.*

### Means
To **inform** someone — to formally tell them a fact they needed to know.

Register is what marks it. *Tell* is neutral, *notify* is administrative, but **apprise** carries a whiff of duty: the information was owed, and someone discharged the obligation. It almost always takes **of** ("apprised of the risks") and lives happily in the passive — lawyers, officers, doctors and boards apprise people. Nobody apprises you of the football score.

⚠️ **apprise ≠ appraise.**
- **apprise** = *inform* someone (uh-PRYZE)
- **appraise** = *assess the value* of something (uh-PRAYZE)

The extra **a** is the giveaway: ap**praise** hides **praise** — you're putting a worth on it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Apprise rhymes with advise** — and both are things a lawyer does to a client, in writing.

The rhyme locks the sound (PRYZE, not PRAYZE) and *advise* carries the same formal, duty-bound tone.

### In sentences
1. The surgeon **apprised** the family of the risks before asking anyone to sign.
2. Keep me **apprised** — if the client so much as sneezes, I want an email.

### In the wild
**"Keep me apprised."** — the standard bark of every boss, detective and commanding officer in workplace and procedural drama, and of plenty of real managers too.

### Where it comes from
Via French **appris**, past participle of *apprendre* "to learn / to teach," from Latin **apprehendere** = *"to grasp."*

Same root gives you **apprentice** (one being taught), **apprehend** (to grasp — a fact, or a suspect), **comprehend** (grasp fully) and **prehensile** (able to grip). To apprise someone is to hand them something to grasp.

# **beguile** *(v.)* — **bih-GYLE**

*Second syllable rhymes with mile, smile, guile.*

### Means
To **charm or enchant someone** — usually in a way that lowers their guard, and often in order to trick them. A secondary sense: to **while away time pleasantly** (*they beguiled the afternoon with cards*).

*Charm* is harmless; *deceive* is hostile. **Beguile sits in between and refuses to choose.** The charm is doing work on you — it's disarming you while you enjoy it. That's why the word can describe a lovely view, a clever swindler, or a person who is both. On the GRE, expect it where a passage wants pleasure and manipulation in the same breath: you are beguiled *into* things, rarely by accident.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Beguile hides *guile* behind a *smile*.**

The cunning (*guile*) is literally inside the word, but it arrives wrapped in charm — which is exactly why it works.

### In sentences
1. The prospectus **beguiled** hundreds of small investors with projections that no one had audited.
2. He's so charming that you don't notice you've been **beguiled** into paying for dinner again.

### In the wild
Sofia Coppola's film **The Beguiled** (2017) — a wounded soldier taken in by a girls' boarding school, charmed, fussed over, and quietly destroyed. The title *is* the definition: seduction with a knife in it.

### Where it comes from
**be-** (*thoroughly*) + **guile** (*cunning, trickery*) — so literally *"to work guile on someone."*

That root is still a live English word, and it gives you **guileless** (innocent, no cunning at all — a common GRE answer choice) and **wily** / **wile**, its close cousin.

# **boon** *(n.)* — **BOON**

### Means
A **thing that helps a lot**, arriving at just the right moment — a benefit, a blessing, a windfall.

Two things distinguish it. First, a boon comes **from outside**: luck, weather, a policy, a patron. You don't grind for a boon, you *receive* one — which is why "a boon to farmers" sounds right and "a boon I earned" sounds wrong. Second, it's slightly **elevated and headline-ish** — journalists and speechwriters reach for it far more than people do in conversation. Its natural opposite is **bane**, and the pair is often set against each other deliberately.

There's an older, still-live sense too: a **favour asked for and granted** — *"grant me a boon."* That's the original, and it explains the rest.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Boom → boon:** shift one letter and it's still sudden good news you didn't have to work for.

A boom and a boon both drop into your lap from the outside — that unearned, from-elsewhere quality is the whole word.

### In sentences
1. The late monsoon proved a **boon** to a district that had written off the season's harvest.
2. Free coffee in the office kitchen is a genuine **boon** when you're running on four hours' sleep.

### In the wild
In every English retelling of Hindu mythology, the gods **grant a boon** — Ravana asks Brahma for one and gets invulnerability. That's the word's oldest sense sitting in plain sight: a favour requested and given by a higher power.

### Where it comes from
Old Norse **bón** = *"a prayer, a request."* A boon was first the thing you **asked for**; only later did it become the good thing that simply arrives.

🚫 **Not French *bon* ("good")** — despite the look. The one exception is the fossilised phrase **boon companion** (a jolly drinking friend), which really does come from *bon*. Different word, same spelling.

# **callous** *(adj.)* — **KAL-uhs**

*Identical in sound to callus, the patch of hard skin — no audible difference.*

### Means
Hardened against other people's suffering; showing no feeling or concern where feeling is owed.

A **cruel** person wants you to hurt; a **callous** person simply doesn't register that you're hurting. That's the whole charge — not malice but a missing reaction, the shrug where sympathy should have been. It's also a word about *becoming*: people are described as callous after long exposure has worn the softness off them — surgeons, landlords, war reporters. Strong enough to be an accusation, and it usually lands on an act or a remark ("a callous joke") as readily as on a person.

⚠️ **callous ≠ callow.** **Callous** = hardened, unfeeling (too much experience). **Callow** = green, immature, inexperienced (too little). A callow youth becomes a callous veteran.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A callus is skin too thick to feel. A callous person is thickened somewhere else.**

The word hides *callus* whole, and that's exactly the metaphor — not sharpness, just numbness where sensitivity used to be.

### In sentences
1. Years in the claims department had made him **callous** to grief, which he processed at a rate of forty files a day.
2. Texting "k" after she told you her dog died is a bit **callous**, mate.

### In the wild
**"Callous disregard"** is a set phrase in courtrooms and newspapers — the standard wording for indifference so total it counts as wrongdoing. If you've read a verdict summary, you've read this word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **callum / callus** = *"hard skin."* A callus is what forms where the skin is rubbed over and over until it stops feeling anything — and that is the entire meaning of *callous*, transplanted to the heart. Same root gives **callus**, **calloused**, **callosity**.

# **coddle** *(v.)* — **KOD-uhl**

### Means
To treat someone with **exaggerated tenderness** — shielding them from difficulty, effort, or consequences. (Literal sense, still in use: to cook an egg in water kept deliberately *just below* the boil.)

*Pamper* is about luxuries; *coddle* is about protection. You coddle by removing the hard thing — and the word carries the built-in accusation that the removal has made the person soft. It's a judgement, almost always spoken by someone who disapproves: no parent describes their own parenting as coddling. Typical objects are children, star players, and employees.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A coddled egg never meets a rolling boil — and neither does a coddled person.**

The cooking sense is the whole word: heat kept low on purpose, so nothing ever toughens.

### In sentences
1. The report argued that the regulator had **coddled** the banks for a decade, treating every warning sign as a matter for private conversation rather than penalty.
2. Stop **coddling** him — he's twenty-six, he can call the landlord himself.

### In the wild
**The Coddling of the American Mind** — Lukianoff and Haidt's much-argued-about 2018 book on over-protected students. The title is the definition: protect people from every discomfort and you leave them less able to handle any.

# **crescendo** *(n. / v.)* — **kruh-SHEN-doh**

*The sc is Italian "sh" — never kres-SEN-doh.*

### Means
A **gradual increase in loudness** — and by extension, any steady swell in intensity, pressure, or noise.

The word names the **climb, not the summit**. Strictly, a crescendo is the eight bars of getting louder; the loud bit at the top is the climax. So "the argument rose to a crescendo" is the usage careful writers wince at — the crescendo *is* the rising. GRE sentence-completion loves this: a blank wanting *crescendo* wants **building, swelling, mounting**, not *peak* or *culmination*. Note also that it's neutral machinery — a crescendo of applause and a crescendo of complaints are equally idiomatic.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cresc**endo is the **cresc**ent moon — swelling toward full, not the full moon itself.

Same root, same idea: both name growth in progress, which is why the peak is never the crescendo.

### In sentences
1. The murmur of the crowd swelled to a **crescendo** as the doors of the courthouse finally opened.
2. The washing machine does this awful **crescendo** thing right before it starts walking across the kitchen.

### In the wild
The Beatles' **"A Day in the Life"** — that vast orchestral **crescendo** where forty musicians climb from their lowest note to the highest over 24 bars, twice, before the final piano chord. Textbook definition, textbook execution.

### Where it comes from
Italian **crescendo** = *"growing"*, from Latin **crescere**, *"to grow"* — the present participle, literally "in the act of growing."

That root pays out across the GRE: **crescent** (the growing moon), **increase**, **increment**, **accrue**, **excrescence** (an ugly outgrowth), and **recruit** (*re-crescere*, fresh growth for the army).

# **extenuating** *(adj. (from v. extenuate))* — **ik-STEN-yoo-ay-ting**

### Means
Making an offence **seem less serious** by supplying a partial excuse — context that reduces the blame without removing it.

Watch the size of the claim: an extenuating fact does not say *she didn't do it* or *she did nothing wrong*. It says *she did it, and here is why you should judge her less harshly.* Guilt stands; the punishment shrinks. That's why it lives in courtrooms, HR meetings and disciplinary hearings — it's the language of a plea, not a defence. Compare **exonerate** (clears you completely) and **mitigate** (its plain-English twin, used for punishments and risks as well as for blame).

⚠️ **extenuate ≠ attenuate.** Both come from Latin *tenuis*, "thin", and both mean "make thinner" — but **attenuate** thins *anything* (a signal, a virus, a bond), while **extenuate** thins only **blame**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ex-TENU-ating** has *tenuous* buried in it — it makes the case against you **thin**, not nonexistent.

A thinned case is still a case, which is exactly why extenuating circumstances reduce a sentence instead of cancelling it.

### In sentences
1. The judge accepted that his age and coercion were **extenuating** circumstances, and reduced the sentence accordingly.
2. Look, being tired is not an **extenuating** circumstance — everyone else made the meeting.

### In the wild
Universities run on this word: the **"extenuating circumstances" form** you file to get a deadline extended or a failed exam discounted. You admit the work was late — you're arguing the lateness should count for less.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tenuis** = *"thin"* → **extenuare**, "to make thin, whittle down."

The root pays out across the GRE: **tenuous** (a thin argument, barely holding), **attenuate** (weaken, thin out), **tenuity**. Extenuating facts whittle the blame down to something thinner.

# **frenetic** *(adj.)* — **fruh-NET-ik**

### Means
Wildly fast and energetic; hectic to the point of looking out of control.

The word describes **pace and motion**, not feeling. A trading floor, a drum solo, a toddler's birthday party, a newsroom on election night — all frenetic. It can be said with admiration (frenetic energy on stage) or exhaustion (a frenetic schedule), but either way the picture is the same: too much happening, too fast, with no visible governor on it.

⚠️ **frenetic ≠ frantic.**
- **frenetic** = wild **speed and activity** — a frenetic pace
- **frantic** = wild **with fear or urgency** — a frantic search for a missing child

A dance can be frenetic. A parent is frantic.

### Trick to lock it in
> **frenetic = frenzy + kinetic** — a frenzy in motion.

The blend keeps the two halves of the meaning together: *kinetic* is the movement, *frenzy* is the lack of control, and neither half is about fear.

### In sentences
1. The final act unfolds at a **frenetic** tempo, the strings sawing away as though the orchestra were fleeing the hall.
2. Two coffees in, he was typing at this **frenetic** pace and answering three Slack threads at once.

### In the wild
Live sports commentary runs on this word — **"a frenetic first ten minutes"** is standard football-pundit English for a game that opens at breakneck speed.

### Where it comes from
Greek **phrēn** = *"mind"* → **phrenitis**, "inflammation of the brain," i.e. delirium. The word literally meant *brain-fevered*.

That root is worth banking: **frantic** and **frenzy** are the same word arriving by different routes, and **schizophrenia** (*split mind*) and **phrenology** (*mind-reading by skull bumps*) come from the same **phrēn**.

# **fringe** *(n. / adj.)* — **FRINJ**

### Means
**Noun:** the outer edge or margin of something — of a forest, a city, a crowd, a movement. (Also the literal decorative border of hanging threads on a rug or shawl, and in British English the hair cut across the forehead — *bangs*.)
**Adjective:** on the margins; outside the accepted mainstream.

The useful GRE sense is positional and then judgemental. A *fringe* group isn't just small — it sits outside the centre of legitimacy, and the word quietly says the centre is where the sensible people are: **fringe theories**, **the lunatic fringe**, **fringe candidate**. But the same outsider position flips positive when the centre is the establishment: **fringe theatre** means daring and unofficial, not crackpot. Note also **fringe benefits** — perks at the edge of your salary, not part of the main pay.

### Trick to lock it in
> F-**RING**-e — a ring sits *around* the middle, never in it.

The hidden *ring* puts you on the outer band: close enough to touch the thing, never part of its centre.

### In sentences
1. Her proposals, dismissed as **fringe** eccentricities for a decade, are now written into party policy.
2. He's a bit **fringe** — swears the moon landings were filmed in a shed.

### In the wild
The **Edinburgh Festival Fringe** got its name literally: in 1947 eight uninvited companies turned up and performed on the *edge* of the official festival. It's now bigger than the thing it was excluded from — which is the word's whole ambiguity in one story.

# **hapless** *(adj.)* — **HAP-luhs**

### Means
Unlucky — dogged by misfortune, and unable to do much about it.

The tone is pity from a safe distance, usually with a faint smile in it. A *hapless* person isn't doomed or tragic; they're the one who gets rained on, blamed, tripped up — misfortune keeps landing on them while they stand there taking it. That passivity is baked in: hapless people have things *done to* them. It's also almost always said about someone else — a narrator's word, a journalist's word — and it quietly makes the subject slightly ridiculous. Hence the stock pairings: *hapless victim, hapless bureaucrat, the hapless defending champions.*

⚠️ **hapless ≠ helpless.**
- **hapless** = *unlucky* — bad things keep happening to you
- **helpless** = *powerless* — you can't act at all

A strong swimmer can be hapless (three cancelled flights); a hapless swimmer isn't necessarily helpless in the water.

### Trick to lock it in
> **You are never hapless in your own story — only in someone else's.**

Nobody calls themselves hapless, because the word carries an onlooker's amused pity, which is exactly the nuance that separates it from plain *unlucky*.

### In sentences
1. The novel follows a **hapless** clerk whose every attempt at romance collapses into farce.
2. Some **hapless** intern got blamed for taking the whole site down.

### In the wild
Sports commentary runs on this word — the bottom-of-the-table side is always "the **hapless** Bears," never merely the losing one. Pundits reach for it to make defeat sound faintly comic.

### Where it comes from
Old Norse **happ** = *"luck, chance"* → English **hap**, luck. Add *-less*: **without luck**.

That tiny root is hiding in a lot of everyday words: **happen** (what befalls you by chance), **happy** (originally *lucky*, not cheerful), **perhaps** (by chance), **mishap**, **haphazard** — all built on luck, not on feeling.

# **immaculate** *(adj.)* — **i-MAK-yuh-luht**

*The ending mumbles to -luht, never "-layt".*

### Means
Perfectly clean — **not one spot, mark or stain**. By extension: **flawless**, free of any error or blemish (an immaculate record, immaculate timing, an immaculate performance).

It's an absolute, not a degree. *Clean* is a baseline and *tidy* is about arrangement; **immaculate** is a standard *achieved*, and it implies someone worked at it. That's also why it's the highest praise available for a kitchen, a uniform, a restoration, or a career — and why a single crumb on the counter cancels it outright.

### Trick to lock it in
> You can be *fairly* clean. You cannot be *fairly* immaculate.

The word admits no degrees — one crumb, one typo, one missed payment, and it simply stops being true.

### In sentences
1. After forty years on the bench she retired with an **immaculate** record: not one judgment overturned on appeal.
2. His flat is **immaculate** — I've never once seen a dish in that sink.

### In the wild
The **Immaculate Conception** — which, despite the common mix-up, is about *Mary* being conceived free of original sin: conceived **without stain**. The doctrine is literally named after the root.

### Where it comes from
Latin **macula** = *"spot, stain"*, plus **in-** = *"not"*. Immaculate = **un-spotted**.

Same root gives you the eye's **macula** (a small spot on the retina — hence *macular degeneration*) and the rare literary **maculate**, "stained", which is just immaculate's opposite with the *im-* stripped off.

# **obfuscate** *(v.)* — **OB-fuh-skayt**

*Also heard as ob-FUS-kayt.*

### Means
To make something **deliberately unclear** — to muddy a fact, a number, or an argument so nobody can see what's actually being said.

The key is *method and motive*. You don't obfuscate by hiding something; you obfuscate by **piling stuff on top of it** — jargon, qualifiers, footnotes, extra clauses. And it's never accidental: *confuse* can be clumsiness, *obscure* can be a passing cloud, but **obfuscate is always someone's choice**. It's the verb you reach for when a company's earnings report technically discloses everything and reveals nothing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ob-FUSS-cate** — bury the point under a **fuss** of words.

The fuss is the point: obfuscation adds noise rather than removing information, and the person doing it wants the noise there.

### In sentences
1. The report **obfuscated** the losses across nine appendices, each technically accurate and collectively unreadable.
2. Just answer the question — stop **obfuscating**.

### In the wild
Developers ship **obfuscated** JavaScript: the code still runs, but variables become `a`, `b`, `c` and the logic is scrambled so competitors can't read it. Same idea — nothing removed, everything made unreadable on purpose.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob-** (*over*) + **fuscāre** (*to darken*), from **fuscus**, "dark, dusky." Literally *to darken over* — you throw shade across the meaning until nobody can make it out.

# **ossify** *(v.)* — **OSS-uh-feye**

### Means
**Literally:** to turn into bone — cartilage hardening into skeleton.
**Figuratively (the GRE sense):** to become rigid, fixed, and incapable of change.

The word only bites because of what it implies about the *past*: something that ossifies was once soft, living, adaptable — a reform movement, a ritual, a bureaucracy — and has now set hard. *Rigid* describes a state; **ossify** narrates a decline. It's a diagnosis delivered from the outside, almost never about oneself, and it carries a faint contempt: the thing still has its shape, but nothing moves in it anymore.

### Trick to lock it in
> An **ossified** committee is a **fossil** — you can still read the shape, but nothing's alive in it.

*Foss-* and *oss-* rhyme, and the image carries the nuance exactly: not destroyed, just hardened past the point of change.

### In sentences
1. What began as a radical experiment in governance had, within two generations, **ossified** into ceremony.
2. Their whole hiring process has **ossified** — nobody can tell you why the third interview exists, just that it's always been there.

### In the wild
Radiologists date a teenager's age from X-rays by checking how far the growth plates have **ossified** — soft cartilage turning to bone on a predictable schedule. That's the literal sense doing daily work.

### Where it comes from
Latin **os, ossis** = *"bone"*, plus *-ify* (*"make into"*). The root is quietly productive: **osseous** (bony), **ossuary** (a bone-house for stacked remains), **ossicle** (the three tiny bones of the middle ear).

🚫 Not related to **osteo-** words (*osteoporosis*, *osteopath*) — those are Greek *osteon*, also "bone." Two languages, same idea, unconnected spellings.

# **pastiche** *(n. / v.)* — **pa-STEESH**

*Ends in *-eesh*, not *-itch* — it came through French.*

### Means
A work that openly **imitates the style of another artist, period, or genre** — or a piece **assembled out of bits of several sources**, a medley.

The key is that a pastiche hides nothing. **Plagiarism** steals and denies it; **parody** imitates in order to mock; a pastiche imitates *without either* — usually in homage, sometimes just as a stylistic exercise. Which is why the word cuts both ways: call a novel "a loving pastiche of Chandler" and it's praise, call it "mere pastiche" and you've said it has borrowed style and no soul of its own.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pastiche = "paste-ish"** — other people's styles pasted together, openly, with the seams showing.

Paste implies assembly rather than theft or ridicule, which is exactly the space pastiche occupies between plagiarism and parody.

### In sentences
1. His late symphonies are an elegant **pastiche** of Mahler, faithful in every gesture and original in none.
2. The whole bar is a **pastiche** of a 1950s diner — chrome stools, jukebox, the lot.

### In the wild
**Stranger Things** is the cleanest example going: a pastiche of Spielberg, Stephen King and John Carpenter — every source lovingly copied, none of it mocked, none of it hidden.

### Where it comes from
French **pastiche** ← Italian **pasticcio** = *a pie* — a crust stuffed with whatever ingredients were to hand. From Latin **pasta**, "dough."

So the root family is literally food: **pasta, paste, pastry, pâté**. A pastiche is a *pie* of other people's styles.

# **perspicacious** *(adj.)* — **per-spi-KAY-shuss**

### Means
Having sharp insight — able to notice and understand what others miss, especially about people and motives.

This is praise, and formal praise at that. *Shrewd* hints that you're working an angle; *astute* is practical, the eye for a good deal; *perceptive* is the everyday word. **Perspicacious** is the cerebral top of that scale: penetrating judgement, seeing *through* a situation rather than merely noticing it. Noun: **perspicacity**.

**perspicacious ≠ perspicuous.**
- **perspicacious** = the *reader* sees clearly (keen-minded)
- **perspicuous** = the *writing* is clear (lucid, easy to follow)

A perspicacious critic praises a perspicuous argument.

### Trick to lock it in
> **per-SPY-cacious** — hear the **spy**: not just clever, but seeing straight through you.

A spy's whole job is reading people and detecting what's being hidden, which is exactly the kind of intelligence this word names.

### In sentences
1. A perspicacious reader of the market, she sold three months before the crash and never once explained how she knew.
2. One perspicacious question from a junior analyst and the whole business case fell apart.

### Where it comes from
Latin **perspicere** = *"to see through"* (**per-** through + **specere** to look).

That **spec-** root is a GRE workhorse: **inspect** (look into), **conspicuous** (visible from all sides), **circumspect** (looking around before acting), **specious** (looks good, isn't), **prospect, spectacle, specimen**.

# **ponderous** *(adj.)* — **PON-der-uhs**

### Means
**1.** So heavy or bulky that it moves slowly and without grace. **2.** (of speech, writing, style) laboured and dull — weighed down by its own seriousness.

Both senses are complaints. A ponderous thing has mass but no lightness of touch: the freight train, the man in armour, the 600-page novel where nothing happens. Applied to prose or a speaker, it says the effort shows — every sentence arrives like furniture being dragged across a floor. Weight is the literal core; **tedium** is what the GRE usually wants.

**ponderous ≠ pondering.** Same root, opposite compliment: to **ponder** is to think deeply; **ponderous** describes something that merely *feels* heavy — often with no thought in it at all. A ponderous lecture is boring, not profound.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ponder** weighs the idea; **ponderous** just weighs a ton.

Both come from "weight," but only one of them earns it — ponderous is the heaviness without the thinking.

### In sentences
1. The **ponderous** machinery of the appeals court took four years to produce a ruling of two pages.
2. Two hours in and the documentary was still **ponderous** — I gave up and made toast.

### In the wild
It's a stock word in film reviews: a self-important three-hour epic gets called **"ponderous"** almost every time. If a critic uses it, they mean *slow and full of itself* — not *deep*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pondus, ponderis** = *weight*. That one root carries a useful family: **ponder** (weigh an idea in the mind), **preponderance** (the side that outweighs), **imponderable** (something that can't be weighed up at all), and even **pound** — the *lb.* is from Latin *libra pondo*, "a pound by weight."

# **recluse** *(n. / adj.)* — **REK-loos**

*Noun: REK-loos. The adjective form *reclusive* shifts the stress: ri-KLOO-siv.*

### Means
Someone who lives shut away from other people, by choice.

An **introvert** describes how you recharge; a **recluse** describes how you live — it's a whole address, not a mood. A **hermit** withdraws for God or the desert; a recluse just withdraws, usually from a life he already had. That's why the word so often arrives with a backstory: people *become* recluses. It carries a faint chill of eccentricity or damage — call someone a recluse and you've hinted that something happened.

⚠️ **recluse ≠ recuse.**
- **recluse** = a person who shuts himself away from society
- **recuse** *(ri-KYOOZ)* = to remove **yourself** from a case or decision, for conflict of interest

The judge **recused** herself. The novelist became a **recluse**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **re-CLUSE = re-CLOSED.** The door isn't just shut for the evening — it's shut for good.

Shyness is a closed door; being a recluse is a closed door with the lock changed.

### In sentences
1. After the trial, the financier retreated to a shuttered estate in Vermont and lived out his last decade as a **recluse**.
2. He's not antisocial, he's just gone full **recluse** since the breakup — nobody's seen him in months.

### In the wild
The **brown recluse spider** — named for exactly this: it hides in undisturbed dark corners, boxes, attics, and bites only when you corner it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **claudere** = *"to shut"*, plus **re-**: *recludere*, shut away.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **seclude** (shut apart), **exclude** (shut out), **include** (shut in), **preclude** (shut off in advance), plus **closet**, **cloister**, and **claustrophobia** — fear of the shut-in space.

# **retaliate** *(v.)* — **rih-TAL-ee-ayt**

### Means
To **strike back** at someone who has harmed you — returning injury for injury, usually in the same coin.

The word is structurally **second**: it needs a prior blow to exist, so it quietly assigns blame to whoever moved first. Compare its neighbours — **avenge** is moral and elevated (you avenge a murdered brother), **revenge** is personal and hot, but **retaliate** is cool, transactional, almost procedural. It's the verb of tariffs, sanctions, air strikes, and office politics: a measured counter-move, not a passion. Note too that it doesn't need to be *just* — only *responsive*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nobody can retaliate first.**

The word carries its own backstory — a retaliation is always the second blow, which is why it lets the retaliator claim the moral high ground.

### In sentences
1. When the tribunal upheld the sanctions, Beijing **retaliated** within the week, matching the tariff line for line.
2. Push him in a meeting and he won't argue — he'll **retaliate** three months later in your performance review.

### In the wild
Trade-war headlines run on this word: every round of tariffs is met by **retaliatory tariffs**, and HR policies everywhere ban **retaliation** against whistleblowers — the legal term for punishing someone for complaining.

### Where it comes from
Latin **talis** = *"of such kind"* → **retaliare**, *"to return the like."* That's the whole nuance sitting in the root: the response is supposed to **match** the offence.

Same root gives **lex talionis**, the ancient law of "an eye for an eye" — measured, equal payback, not unlimited revenge.

🚫 **Not related to *tally*** (Latin *talea*, a notched stick), even though "settling the score" makes them feel like cousins.

# **rhapsody** *(n.)* — **RAP-suh-dee**

*The *rh* is just an R. The verb shifts the stress: **rhapsodize** = RAP-suh-dyze.*

### Means
An outpouring of extravagant, ecstatic enthusiasm — in speech or writing. Also, in music, a free-form piece that wanders where it likes instead of obeying a set structure.

Both senses share one idea: **form has been abandoned**. A eulogy is organised praise; a rhapsody is praise that has run off its leash — the speaker is carried away, gushing, no longer building an argument. That's why the verb **rhapsodize** (the form the GRE actually tests) usually comes with a faint raised eyebrow: *he rhapsodized about his new grill for forty minutes.* Sincere, yes — but excessive, and slightly funny to watch.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Rhap** = **rap**: a rhapsody is freestyle — stitched together on the fly, no chorus to come back to.

The sound hook carries the nuance: it's not the content that marks a rhapsody but the unstructured, run-on delivery.

### In sentences
1. The review dissolved into **rhapsody** — three paragraphs on the light in a single shot, and not one word on the plot.
2. Don't ask him about his espresso machine unless you've got twenty minutes for the full **rhapsody**.

### In the wild
Queen's **"Bohemian Rhapsody"** is the definition in action: ballad, then opera, then hard rock, no repeating chorus — a song deliberately stitched from mismatched pieces, exactly what the word names.

### Where it comes from
Greek **rhaptein** (*to stitch*) + **ōidē** (*song*) — a "stitched-together song." A *rhapsode* was the travelling reciter who sewed episodes of Homer into one performance.

That **ōidē** is worth banking: **ode**, **melody**, **parody**, **comedy**, **prosody** all carry it.

# **serendipitous** *(adj.)* — **ser-uhn-DIP-i-tuhs**

### Means
Happening by a **lucky accident** — especially when the accident hands you something good that you *weren't looking for*.

Plain luck isn't enough. A serendipitous event needs a **find**: a discovery, a meeting, a solution. And it must be **unsought** — if you were hunting for it, finding it is just success. Penicillin (a contaminated dish) is serendipitous; a lottery win is merely lucky. Tonally the word is warm, a little whimsical, faintly self-congratulatory — it's the word people reach for in wedding speeches and startup origin stories to make a coincidence sound like fate.

⚠️ **serendipitous ≠ fortuitous.**
- **serendipitous** = by chance **and** happily
- **fortuitous** = by chance, full stop — the outcome can be terrible

A fortuitous collision is still a collision.

### Trick to lock it in
> Buried in the middle is **DIP** — you dipped in blind and came up holding treasure.

The *dip* is unaimed, which is the whole point: the good thing found you, not the other way round.

### In sentences
1. The discovery was entirely **serendipitous**: a mislabelled sample, left out over a long weekend, yielded the enzyme three years of directed research had missed.
2. We only met because my train was cancelled — pretty **serendipitous**, honestly.

### In the wild
The 2001 John Cusack film **Serendipity** — two strangers collide over the same pair of gloves in a department store and spend the movie letting chance decide. The title is the plot.

### Where it comes from
Coined by Horace Walpole in **1754**, from the Persian tale *The Three Princes of **Serendip*** — Serendip being the old name for **Sri Lanka**. Walpole's princes, he wrote, "were always making discoveries, by accidents and sagacity, of things they were not in quest of."

That last clause *is* the definition: accident **plus** the wit to notice.

# **shirk** *(v.)* — **SHURK**

### Means
To **avoid or neglect a duty, task, or responsibility** — to slide out of something you were obliged to do.

The object is always an obligation. You can dodge a party or duck a phone call, but you can only *shirk* a **duty** — so the word carries the accusation with it. *Avoid* is neutral, *evade* suggests cunning, but *shirk* suggests laziness or cowardice: the work still exists, and someone else is now carrying it. It's the standard verb when the failure is moral rather than tactical — **"shirk his responsibilities," "never shirked a hard job."**

### Trick to lock it in
> **Shirk** rhymes with **work** and hides **irk** — the work irks you, so you shirk it.

The rhyme fixes the one thing shirking always applies to (work, duty, obligation — never pleasure), and *irk* supplies the motive.

### In sentences
1. The inquiry found that the board had **shirked** its duty of oversight for the better part of a decade.
2. He's not ill — he's **shirking**, and everyone on the late shift knows it.

### In the wild
**"Shirking"** is the actual technical term in economics and management for employees not pulling their weight — whole models of pay and monitoring are built on stopping workers from shirking.

# **sinecure** *(n.)* — **SY-nuh-kyoor**

*Also SIN-uh-kyoor; both are standard.*

### Means
A **position that carries pay, title or status but almost no actual duties**.

The scorn falls on the **post**, not the person — a sinecure is a job that has been hollowed out, usually because someone was rewarding a friend, a donor, or a retiring grandee. You can hold one and still be industrious; there is simply nothing to do. That's what separates it from *idleness* or a *soft job*: the emptiness is built into the office. The word is dry and faintly damning, the vocabulary of political journalism and board minutes rather than complaint.

⚠️ **sinecure ≠ cynosure.** A **sinecure** is a cushy no-work job; a **cynosure** (SY-nuh-shoor) is the centre of attention, the thing all eyes turn to. Both are obscure, both start with a *sy-* sound — the GRE knows it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sign** here, collect the cheque, **cure** nothing.

The holder isn't accused of shirking — the post itself was created with no work attached to it.

### In sentences
1. The ambassadorship to a friendly island nation had long served as a **sinecure** for donors who had given generously and expected something framed on the wall.
2. Head of "Strategic Vision"? That's a **sinecure** — he shows up for the Christmas party and that's it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sine cura** = *"without care."* It began as a church term: a benefice *sine cura animarum* — a paid living **without the cure (care) of souls**, i.e. a parish priest's salary with no parish to look after.

*Cura* is a workhorse root: **curate** and **curator** (one who takes care of), **manicure** (care of the hands), **accurate** (done with care), **procure**, and **secure** — *se-* "free from" + *cura*, literally *without care* too. And *sine* returns in **sine qua non**, "without which, not."

# **sinuous** *(adj.)* — **SIN-yoo-uhs**

### Means
Full of curves; winding, undulating, snake-like in motion or shape.

Two flavours, both curved. Of *things*: a river, a road, a coastline that bends repeatedly. Of *movement*: a dancer, a cat, a melody line that flows without a break. Either way the word admires — sinuous curves are elegant, supple, unhurried. Compare **tortuous**, which is the same twisting seen as an ordeal (a tortuous mountain pass, a tortuous argument). A sinuous path is a pleasure to follow; a tortuous one is a trial. There is a faint serpent under the word too, so it can shade into *sly* or *seductive* depending on what's doing the curving.

⚠️ **sinuous ≠ sinewy.**
- **sinuous** = *curving, winding*
- **sinewy** = *lean and stringy with muscle; tough* (from *sinew*, tendon)

A sinuous dancer flows. A sinewy farmhand could lift your car.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sinuous is an S. Tortuous is a knot.**

Both twist, but the S is a graceful line you'd trace with a finger, while the knot is the twisting you curse at.

### In sentences
1. The road climbed in **sinuous** loops, each bend opening onto the same valley from a slightly higher angle.
2. She had this **sinuous** way of moving through a crowded bar without ever touching anyone.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sinus** = *"a curve, a fold, a bay."*

That one root pays out repeatedly: your **sinuses** (curved hollows in the skull), **insinuate** (literally to *worm your way in* along a curve — which is why insinuating is never straightforward), and the **sine** curve in maths, which got its name through a chain of mistranslations but keeps the shape.

# **sordid** *(adj.)* — **SOR-did**

### Means
Two linked senses: **physically filthy and squalid** (a sordid basement flat), and — far more common on the GRE — **morally grubby**: sleazy, ignoble, driven by low motives.

The word's real charge is *cheapness*. A sordid act isn't grand or dramatic evil; it's small, self-serving and a bit embarrassing — money, lust, spite, a petty betrayal. Calling a scandal sordid says the people in it were shabby, not terrifying. It also carries a whiff of exposure: details become "sordid" the moment they're dragged into daylight. Where **squalid** leans on physical filth, sordid leads with the moral kind and only sometimes means actual dirt.

### Trick to lock it in
> **sor-DID** — the sordid part is never the crime, it's what he **did it for**.

The word hides *did*, and it's the motive behind the deed — money, lust, spite — that earns the label.

### In sentences
1. The biography declines to linger on the poet's finances, treating that sordid business as beneath its subject.
2. Spare me the sordid details of your breakup, I'm eating.

### In the wild
Court and tabloid reporting runs on two fixed phrases: **"a sordid affair"** and **"all the sordid details."** If you've read a scandal write-up, you've read this word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sordēs** = *"filth, dirt"* → **sordidus**, which meant both *dirty* and *stingy, mean, shabby*. That second Latin sense is the one English kept underneath the surface — why sordid implies a **cheap** motive, not just a dirty one.

# **stanch** *(v.)* — **STAHNCH**

*Usually rhymes with launch; some say it to rhyme with ranch. Commonly spelled staunch when used as a verb.*

### Means
To **stop or check the flow** of something — classically blood from a wound, by extension money, losses, or people leaving.

The word presupposes a leak already in progress: you can *stop* anything, but you only stanch what is escaping. It carries triage, not repair — pressure on the wound, a hand over the hole. Something stanched is contained, not cured, which is exactly why finance and politics reach for it: *stanch the outflow of deposits*, *stanch the loss of members*.

⚠️ **stanch ≠ staunch (adj.).** *Stanch* stops a flow; **staunch** means *loyal, unwavering* — a **staunch** ally, a **staunch** defender. The spellings overlap in the verb, but the adjective never means "to stop."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sta**nch makes it **STAND** still — blood, cash, or customers.

The buried *sta-* is Latin *stare*, "to stand": you're not fixing the wound, just making the flow stand still.

### In sentences
1. Field medics were trained to **stanch** arterial bleeding with a tourniquet before any thought of evacuation.
2. Cutting the free snacks isn't going to **stanch** the bleeding — we're losing a million a month.

### In the wild
"**Stanch the bleeding**" is boardroom and business-news boilerplate — every quarterly-loss story about a company trying to stop the cash pouring out.

### Where it comes from
Old French **estanchier**, "to stop the flow," from Latin **stāre** = *"to stand."* Make the blood stand still.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **stagnant** (standing water), **static**, **stance**, **constant**, **stable**, **stanza** (a "standing place" in a poem), **substance**.

# **surfeit** *(n. / v.)* — **SUR-fit**

*Rhymes with *perfect* said lazily — never "sur-FEET".*

### Means
**Noun:** an excessive amount of something — classically food or drink — and the sick, cloyed feeling that follows.
**Verb:** to feed or supply to the point of disgust.

A *surplus* is neutral bookkeeping; *abundance* is a blessing. **Surfeit** always tips past the point where more stops being good: it carries the queasiness of the tenth pastry. Use it and you imply the recipient is full, sated, faintly nauseated — a *surfeit of information* isn't just a lot, it's more than a mind can digest.

⚠️ **surfeit ≠ forfeit.** A **surfeit** is having far too much; to **forfeit** is to lose what you had as a penalty. Same *-feit* ending, opposite direction of traffic.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **sur**plus that no longer **fits**.

The word literally holds *sur-* ("over") and sounds like *fit* — it's the amount that has stopped fitting, in the stomach or in the head.

### In sentences
1. A **surfeit** of choice, the study argued, leaves shoppers less satisfied than a shelf with three options.
2. I had a **surfeit** of cake at that wedding and I'm still recovering.

### In the wild
*Twelfth Night* opens on it: "If music be the food of love, play on... that **surfeiting**, / The appetite may sicken, and so die." Orsino wants so much music that his craving overdoses itself — the word's exact flavour.

### Where it comes from
Old French **surfait** = *sur-* ("over," from Latin *super*) + *faire* ("to do, make," from Latin **facere**). Literally *overdone*.

That *sur-* is a workhorse: **surpass, surplus, surcharge, surmount, surreal** — all "over/beyond."

# **ulterior** *(adj.)* — **ul-TEER-ee-er**

### Means
Lying **beyond what is openly stated** — kept back, undisclosed. Chiefly of motives: the *real* reason, hiding behind the reason you were given.

It does not mean *evil*, only *concealed* — and the charge lands on the hiding, not the aim. A man with an ulterior motive for buying you dinner may only want a job reference. The word is also almost welded to one noun: nine times out of ten you'll meet it as **ulterior motive(s)**, delivered as a raised eyebrow rather than an outright denunciation — polite suspicion, the sort you can voice at a dinner table.

### Trick to lock it in
> Interior, exterior, **ulterior** — the *-erior* that sits **further out** than the reason you were shown.

All three are Latin comparatives of position, and *ulter* means "beyond," so an ulterior motive is simply the one parked further back than the visible one.

### In sentences
1. The committee's sudden concern for procedure was widely read as **ulterior** — a way to bury the report without appearing to.
2. He's been weirdly nice all week, so obviously there's an **ulterior** motive.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ulter** = *"beyond"* — **ulterior** is its comparative (*"farther"*), **ultimus** its superlative (*"farthest"*). That gives you **ultimate** (the farthest point), **ultimatum** (the final demand), **ultra-** (beyond the limit: *ultraviolet*, *ultrasonic*), and, via French *outre*, **outrage** — going beyond all bounds.

# **voluble** *(adj.)* — **VOL-yuh-buhl**

### Means
Talking a great deal, and doing it **easily** — fluent, rapid, unstoppable speech.

The key is *flow*, not volume. **Talkative** counts words; **loquacious** is the neutral bookish label; **garrulous** is a complaint about rambling on. **Voluble** points at the fluency itself — the speaker never gropes for a word, never stalls. That makes it tilt admiring when the person is charming or persuasive (*a voluble host*, *a voluble advocate*), and faintly wary when the flow feels practised or hard to interrupt.

⚠️ **voluble ≠ voluminous.** *Voluble* = pouring out **speech**; *voluminous* = huge in **bulk** (voluminous robes, voluminous case files). A voluble witness talks endlessly; voluminous testimony fills a filing cabinet.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Voluble** rhymes with **soluble** — the words dissolve out, no lumps, no stalling.

It names the smoothness of the delivery, not merely the quantity of it.

### In sentences
1. Normally reserved in committee, the minister turned **voluble** the moment the subject touched his own department.
2. Two coffees in and she was suddenly **voluble**, running through every piece of office gossip without pausing for breath.

### In the wild
Political reporting leans on one collocation constantly: **"a voluble critic of"** — the person who is never short of something to say against the policy.

### Where it comes from
Latin **volvere** = *"to roll"* — speech that rolls out of you.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **revolve** (roll back), **evolve** (roll out), **involve** (roll in), **convoluted** (rolled together, hence tangled), and **volume** (originally a *rolled* scroll).
