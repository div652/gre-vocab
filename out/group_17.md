# **acclaim** *(n. / v.)* — **uh-KLAYM**

### Means
**Noun:** enthusiastic **public** praise. **Verb:** to praise loudly and publicly — or to declare someone something by shouted approval (*he was acclaimed king*).

Praise from one person is a compliment; acclaim is praise from a **crowd, a press, a field**. It's collective, audible, and usually attaches to work rather than to character — a novel, a film, a performance. It also implies the verdict has settled: "widely acclaimed" means the argument is over.

⚠️ **acclamation ≠ acclimation.**
- **acclamation** = election or approval **by shout** (*carried by acclamation* — no ballot needed)
- **acclimation** = getting used to a **climate** or conditions

One is a roar of yes; the other is your body adjusting to altitude.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nobody *claims* their own acclaim — the crowd claims it for you, out loud.**

The hidden word *claim* is the giveaway that this praise is asserted publicly by others, never awarded to yourself.

### In sentences
1. Her second symphony met with an **acclaim** that the first, performed to an empty hall, had never come close to earning.
2. The film flopped at the box office but got so much critical **acclaim** that everyone pretends they saw it.

### In the wild
**"Critically acclaimed"** — the badge on Netflix rows, movie posters, and book jackets. It's the industry's way of saying *the critics loved this even if you haven't heard of it.*

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*toward*) + **clāmāre** (*to shout*) — literally *to shout toward.*

That root is a GRE goldmine: **exclaim** (shout out), **proclaim** (shout forth), **declaim** (shout formally, orate), **clamour** (a crowd's noise), **clamorous**. Every one of them is somebody making noise.

# **ascertain** *(v.)* — **as-er-TAYN**

*Stress on the last syllable, like "obtain" — not AS-er-tain.*

### Means
To **find out for certain** — to establish a fact by checking, asking, measuring or investigating.

*Find out* is casual and can be accidental; you find out by overhearing. **Ascertain** is deliberate and ends in confidence: you went looking, and now you *know*. It's the register of officials, investigators and auditors — police ascertain the cause of a fire, a court ascertains a person's intent. Using it about trivia sounds comically stiff ("I shall ascertain whether we have milk"), and that stiffness is exactly what the GRE is testing you to hear.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ascertain** = **a-** + **certain**: you dig until you *are certain*.

The word wears its answer on its face — the digging is the verb, and certainty is where it has to end up.

### In sentences
1. The auditors were unable to **ascertain** how the missing funds had left the account.
2. Give me ten minutes to **ascertain** whether he's actually on the flight before we panic.

### In the wild
Standard newsroom and police-briefing language: *"Officers are still trying to **ascertain** the cause of the crash."* Whenever an official won't yet commit to a story, this is the verb they reach for.

# **assertive** *(adj.)* — **uh-SER-tiv**

### Means
Confident and direct in stating your views, needs, or claims — and not backing down from them.

Think of a three-point scale: **passive** (says nothing), **assertive** (says it plainly), **aggressive** (says it at your expense). Assertive is the approved middle — firm without hostility, which is why HR departments and therapists love the word. But it isn't purely a compliment: an *assertive* tone in a debate can mean the person is stating things as fact and daring you to disagree. The force is real; only the aggression is absent.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Assert** + **-ive** — not one loud moment, but the *standing habit* of asserting.

The **-ive** suffix turns an act into a permanent trait (*talk → talkative*, *create → creative*), so an assertive person doesn't just assert once — that's simply how they operate.

### In sentences
1. The junior counsel was unexpectedly **assertive**, pressing her objection until the judge conceded the point.
2. You need to be more **assertive** — just tell them the deadline doesn't work instead of quietly redoing your weekend.

### In the wild
**Assertiveness training** is a standard corporate and therapeutic offering — whole workshops built on teaching people to say "no" without apologising for it. The word's everyday home is the performance review.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **serere** (*to join, to bind*) — to lay claim to something by putting your hand on it.

That **serere** root is worth banking: **series** (things joined in a row), **insert** (join something in), **exert** (put force out), **desert** (unjoin, abandon).

# **bogus** *(adj.)* — **BOH-guhs**

### Means
Fake, counterfeit, not genuine — especially something presented as real evidence: **bogus credentials, bogus statistics, a bogus invoice, a bogus charity**.

Two things set it apart from its neighbours. First, what it attacks: *bogus* goes after **claims and documents** — things that assert something and then collapse under checking — where *phony* goes after **people and sincerity**. Second, the register: *spurious* and *counterfeit* are what the report says; *bogus* is what you say **out loud, with contempt**, and it doubles in casual speech as simply "unfair, rubbish" (*that fine is totally bogus*). It's the one word in this family that comes with an eye-roll built in.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Hocus-pocus** is fake magic; **bogus** is fake everything else — and both get said with a sneer.

The shared *-ocus* sound carries the shared attitude: not just false, but *cheaply, insultingly* false.

### In sentences
1. The journal retracted the study after an audit revealed that half the trial data were **bogus**.
2. They slapped a £40 "processing fee" on the ticket — completely **bogus**.

### In the wild
*Bill & Ted's **Bogus** Journey* (1991) — in their slang, *bogus* is the opposite of *excellent*, which is exactly the dismissive, informal flavour of the word.

# **cataclysmic** *(adj.)* — **kat-uh-KLIZ-mik**

### Means
Violently destructive on an enormous scale — a disaster that overturns and remakes whatever it hits.

The word carries **scale plus transformation**. *Catastrophic* names a bad outcome; *cataclysmic* says the old order is gone and the landscape afterwards is unrecognisable — an asteroid strike, a flood, the collapse of an empire. That's why it also works for non-disasters: a **cataclysmic shift** in the industry means everything realigned, not that anyone died. Formal, high-intensity, and easily hyperbolic — reach for it and you've committed to *world-altering*, so a delayed train doesn't qualify.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cataclysm** ≈ **chasm** — it doesn't just damage things, it rips a gap between before and after.

The near-rhyme fixes the key idea: a cataclysm is a rupture in history, not merely heavy damage.

### In sentences
1. The eruption of Thera was **cataclysmic**, ending a civilisation and reshaping the Aegean coastline in a single season.
2. Losing one client isn't **cataclysmic** — calm down, we'll survive the quarter.

### In the wild
*World of Warcraft: **Cataclysm*** earned its name literally — the expansion tore up and permanently redrew the game's existing world map, which is exactly what the word promises.

### Where it comes from
Greek **kataklysmos** = *"a flood, a washing away"* — **kata-** (down) + **klyzein** (to wash). The original cataclysm was the Deluge.

**kata-** pays off across the GRE: **catastrophe** (a turning down), **cataract** (water dashing down — and the film that comes down over the eye), **catapult** (hurling down), **catalyst** (loosening down).

# **circumscribe** *(v.)* — **SER-kum-skrybe**

### Means
To **draw a boundary around** something and thereby **restrict** it — to fix the limits of a power, a role, a freedom, a life.

Where *limit* is plain and *restrict* is practical, **circumscribe** is the formal, almost legal word for limits imposed **from outside and in advance**: a constitution circumscribes a president's powers; poverty circumscribes a childhood. It doesn't claim the limits are unfair — only that they are firm, external, and define the whole permitted space. In geometry it keeps its literal job: to draw a figure *around* another so it just touches.

⚠️ **circumscribe ≠ circumvent.**
- **circumscribe** = *draw* the boundary — this far and no further
- **circumvent** = *get around* the boundary — slip past the rule

A law circumscribes what you may do. A loophole lets you circumvent it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **The circle is drawn *around* you — you don't get to draw it.**

Circumscribed powers, roles and lives are hemmed in by something external — a charter, a class, a disease — not by the person's own choosing.

### In sentences
1. The treaty sharply **circumscribed** the monarch's authority, leaving him a crown and very little else.
2. After the injury her world got **circumscribed** to one room and a flight of stairs she couldn't manage.

### In the wild
School geometry: the **circumscribed circle** drawn around a triangle so it touches all three corners — the outer limit of the shape, made visible.

### Where it comes from
Latin **circum** (*around*) + **scrībere** (*to write, to draw*) — literally *to draw a line around*.

*Scrībere* is a GRE workhorse: **describe** (write down), **inscribe** (write into), **prescribe** (write beforehand — the doctor's order), **proscribe** (write off — outlaw), **transcribe**, **subscribe**, **scripture**.

# **complementary** *(adj.)* — **kom-pluh-MEN-tuh-ree**

*Sounds exactly like complimentary — the difference is only on the page.*

### Means
Serving to **complete** something; combining so that each part supplies what the other lacks.

Two complementary things are **not alike — they're interlocking**. Compatible people merely get along; complementary people cover each other's gaps, and neither is whole alone. That's why it's the standard word for a good partnership ("complementary skills") and for exact technical pairings: complementary angles sum to 90°, complementary colours sit opposite on the wheel, complementary DNA strands pair base for base.

⚠️ **complementary ≠ complimentary.**
- **compl*e*mentary** = *completes* the other thing
- **compl*i*mentary** = praising, or **free of charge**

The hotel gives you a **complimentary** breakfast. The eggs and the coffee are **complementary**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Compl-E-mentary** hides **complete**; **compl-I-mentary** hides **I like it**.

The E-word fills a gap, the I-word pays a compliment — one spelling letter decides which.

### In sentences
1. The two ministers brought complementary strengths to the negotiation: one commanded the detail, the other the room.
2. They're a weirdly complementary pair — he can't cook, she can't drive, so it works.

### In the wild
Every action-film poster is orange-and-teal — the two **complementary colours** on the wheel, chosen because skin tones pop hardest against their opposite.

### Where it comes from
Latin **complēre** = *"to fill up."* Fill something up and it is **complete**; the thing that does the filling is the **complement**.

Same root gives **replete** (filled again, stuffed), **deplete** (un-filled), **plenty**, **plenary**.

# **contentious** *(adj.)* — **kun-TEN-shus**

### Means
Two linked senses:
**Of a thing:** likely to cause argument — disputed, fought over. *A contentious clause, a contentious border, a contentious appointment.*
**Of a person:** eager to argue — quarrelsome, always ready to pick a fight.

*Controversial* just reports that people disagree; **contentious** puts you in the room while they do it — it carries the friction, the raised voices, the fact that this thing *generates* fights rather than merely attracting them. Applied to a topic it's neutral-to-wary; applied to a person it's a straight criticism, and a slightly formal one — you'd write it in a performance review, not shout it across a table.

⚠️ **contentious ≠ contented.** *Contented* = quietly satisfied (Latin *continēre*, "to hold in"). *Contentious* = fighting (Latin *contendere*, "to strain against"). Opposite moods, unrelated roots — the shared "content" is a coincidence.

### Trick to lock it in
> **con-TEN-tious = con + TENSION.** A contentious issue is one loaded with tension.

The sound hook is also the true root — *tension* and *contentious* both come from Latin *tendere*, "to stretch tight."

### In sentences
1. The land reform bill's most **contentious** provision — retroactive compensation — consumed three days of debate on its own.
2. Don't sit him next to my uncle; he gets **contentious** after two drinks and argues about everything.

### In the wild
Law firms split their entire practice this way: **contentious** work means litigation — suing, defending, disputes — while **non-contentious** work is contracts and deals where nobody is fighting. Same for "contentious probate": the will someone is challenging.

### Where it comes from
Latin **contendere** = *"to strain, to strive against"* (*con-* "with/against" + *tendere* "to stretch").

*Tendere* is a workhorse root: **tension, tendon, extend, intense, portend, tent** (stretched canvas) — and its *contendere* branch gives **contend, contention, contest**. A contentious person is permanently *straining against* someone.

# **disingenuous** *(adj.)* — **dis-in-JEN-yoo-uhs**

### Means
Insincere — pretending to know less, or to mean better, than you actually do.

It is not plain lying. A disingenuous person says something **technically defensible** while wearing a mask of innocence: the feigned question, the "I had no idea this would upset anyone," the argument built on a definition they know isn't the one in play. The fakery is in the *guilelessness*, not just the facts.

Register matters: this is the educated, deniable accusation. In a debate or a review you can call an argument disingenuous and stay civil; call it a lie and the room changes.

⚠️ **ingenuous ≠ ingenious.** *Ingenuous* = frank, artless, unguarded (the opposite of this word). *Ingenious* = clever, inventive. One letter, two unrelated qualities — and *disingenuous* is the negative of the first, never the second.

### Trick to lock it in
> **dis-in-genu-ous** — *"this ain't genuine"*, and what's fake is the innocence.

The sound of the word hands you *genuine*, and the *dis-* cancels it — with the specific fraud being an act of not-knowing.

### In sentences
1. The minister's claim that he had never read the report was **disingenuous** at best; his own annotations ran down its margins.
2. Don't be **disingenuous** — you knew exactly how that would sound when you sent it.

### In the wild
Listen to any political interview or press briefing: *disingenuous* is the standard weapon there precisely because parliamentary and broadcast conventions forbid calling someone a liar outright.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ingenuus** = *"freeborn"* — and a freeborn Roman was assumed to speak frankly, so the word slid into "candid, open." Add **dis-** and you get someone faking that openness.

Underneath sits **gignere / genus** (*to beget, to be born*), which pays for itself across the exam: **genuine, genus, congenital, indigenous, progeny, genesis**.

🚫 **Not *ingenious*.** That's Latin *ingenium*, "inborn talent" — cleverness, not candour.

# **divulge** *(v.)* — **dih-VULJ**

*Also duy-VULJ; stress is on the second syllable either way.*

### Means
To **reveal something that was meant to be kept private** — a secret, a source, a salary, a confidence.

You can only divulge what was *withheld*. Announcing tomorrow's weather isn't divulging; naming your anonymous source is. The word carries a whiff of breached trust — even when the divulging is voluntary, there was a seal on the thing. It's also noticeably formal: police, lawyers and press officers *divulge*; your friend just *tells* you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Divulge = make it vulgar** — drag the private thing out to the *vulgus*, the common crowd.

The secret stops being yours the moment it becomes everybody's.

### In sentences
1. The bank is legally forbidden to **divulge** a client's account details to any third party.
2. Don't **divulge** how much you paid for it — he'll never let it go.

### In the wild
The stock line in every press conference and corporate email: *"We're not able to divulge further details at this time."* If it's a stonewall, the verb is almost always this one.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dīvulgāre** = *"to spread among the people,"* from **vulgus** = *the common crowd*.

Same root gives you **vulgar** (of the crowd → coarse) and the **Vulgate** (the Bible put into the people's Latin). Divulging is the act of handing a private thing to the public.

# **dogmatic** *(adj.)* — **dog-MAT-ik**

### Means
Laying down opinions as if they were settled fact — rigidly attached to a doctrine and unwilling to hear evidence against it.

The insult isn't that the person is wrong; it's that they're **closed**. An *opinionated* person argues hard, a *dogmatic* one refuses to argue at all, because for them the question was decided before you spoke. It's almost always an accusation, and it carries a faint whiff of the pulpit or the party line — belief handed down rather than worked out.

### Trick to lock it in
> **DOGMA + auto*MATIC*** — the belief comes out automatically, with no thinking in between.

The *-matic* ending is the whole point: positions asserted on reflex, never re-examined.

### In sentences
1. The review dismissed his monograph as **dogmatic** — confident assertion where the evidence demanded a question mark.
2. Don't be so **dogmatic** about it; you've never even tried pineapple on pizza.

### In the wild
Steve Jobs, Stanford 2005: *"Don't be trapped by dogma — which is living with the results of other people's thinking."* That line defines the noun the adjective comes from.

### Where it comes from
Greek **dogma** = *"an opinion, a decree"*, from **dokein** = *"to seem, to think."*

That root pays off across the exam: **orthodox** (*ortho-* straight + *doxa* opinion = correct belief), **heterodox** (other belief), **paradox** (*para-* beyond belief), **doxology**.

# **fallacious** *(adj.)* — **fuh-LAY-shuhs**

### Means
Based on **faulty reasoning**; containing a logical error, and therefore misleading.

The charge is against the *argument*, not the arguer's honesty — you can be entirely sincere and still fallacious. That's what separates it from **false**: "false" means the claim is untrue; "fallacious" means the *path to* the claim is broken, so the conclusion is unearned even in the odd case where it happens to be true. It's a cool, academic word — the language of debate judges, philosophers and editorials, not of shouting matches.

⚠️ **fallacious ≠ fallible.**
- **fallacious** = this reasoning *is* wrong
- **fallible** = this person *could be* wrong

Every expert is fallible; only some of their arguments are fallacious.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Falla**cious hides **FALL** — a hole in the logic you fall into, and an argument that falls apart.

The flaw is structural, so the whole thing collapses under weight rather than simply being untrue.

### In sentences
1. The court dismissed as **fallacious** the notion that a signature obtained under duress constitutes consent.
2. His whole plan rests on the **fallacious** idea that if it worked once, it'll work forever.

### In the wild
Any internet argument where someone replies *\"that's a logical fallacy\"* — straw man, slippery slope, ad hominem. Those are the catalogued species of fallacious reasoning.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fallere** = *"to deceive, to trip up."* One root, a big family: **fallacy, fallible, infallible, false, fail, fault, default**. Notice the through-line — every one of them is about something giving way underfoot.

# **foolhardy** *(adj.)* — **FOOL-har-dee**

### Means
Bold in a way that ignores the danger — daring without judgment.

The daring is real; that's what separates it from plain stupidity. A *foolhardy* person genuinely has nerve, they just have no brakes. **Reckless** is careless about consequences generally; **audacious** admires the nerve; **foolhardy** grants the courage and then condemns it as wasted. It's the word you use for the climber who went up without ropes — half admiration, mostly disapproval, and a strong hint that it will end badly.

### Trick to lock it in
> **fool + hardy** — the *hardy* half is real toughness; the *fool* half is the missing brain.

Both words are sitting right there in plain sight: courage present, sense absent.

### In sentences
1. The general's **foolhardy** advance across open ground cost him half a division before noon.
2. Swimming out there at night was **foolhardy** and you know it — brave, sure, but you could've drowned.

### In the wild
Alexander Pope's line **"fools rush in where angels fear to tread"** — later the Elvis/Ricky Nelson standard *Fools Rush In* — is foolhardiness in one sentence: charging in exactly where the wiser party won't.

### Where it comes from
**hardy** here doesn't mean *tough-plant* hardy — it's Old French **hardi**, *"bold, daring."* So the word is literally **"foolishly bold."** Same root gives **hardihood** (audacity) and the older sense of **foolhardiness**.

# **hinder** *(v.)* — **HIN-der**

### Means
To **get in the way of** something — to slow it, delay it, or make it harder.

Hinder is the mild end of the scale. *Thwart* and *foil* kill an attempt outright; hinder only adds friction, and the thing usually still limps forward. It's also often impersonal — rain, paperwork, bad lighting, a sore knee. Note the standard exam phrasing **"hinder progress"** and the negative construction **"nothing hindered them"**: hinder lives most naturally where movement or advancement is the thing being slowed.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Hind**er keeps you **behind** — it doesn't keep you *out*.

The hidden word *hind* fixes both the meaning and the strength: you're pushed to the back of the pack, not removed from the race.

### In sentences
1. Chronic underfunding **hindered** the excavation for a decade, though the team never quite abandoned the site.
2. Look, I'm not trying to **hinder** you — just don't expect me to help carry it.

### In the wild
**Hinder** is the American rock band behind the 2006 radio monster **"Lips of an Angel."** Odd name for a band, useful for you.

### Where it comes from
Old English **hindrian**, "to hold back" — built on **hind**, *behind*. Same family: **behind**, **hindsight**, a horse's **hind legs**, and **hinterland** (German *hinter*, "behind" + *Land* — the country lying back from the coast).

So the root meaning is spatial: hindering pushes you toward the *back*. It doesn't stop the race; it puts you further down the field.

# **impair** *(v.)* — **im-PAIR**

### Means
To **weaken, damage, or diminish** something — usually a faculty, ability, or asset — so that it still works, but works worse.

The key is *partial*. You destroy a bridge; you **impair** someone's hearing, judgment, mobility, credit rating, or a treaty's effectiveness. The thing survives in degraded form. It also takes abstract objects almost exclusively — faculties and capacities, not chairs and windows — which gives it a clinical, legal, medical flavour: *cognitive impairment*, *impaired assets*, *driving while impaired*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Impaired, not broken** — the drunk driver still drives, just worse.

Impair degrades a faculty that continues to function; it never means destroy outright.

### In sentences
1. Chronic sleep deprivation **impairs** judgment more severely than moderate intoxication, yet no one legislates against it.
2. One late night and my focus is **impaired** for the rest of the week.

### In the wild
Every TV listing and caption track marked **"for the hearing impaired"** — the standard label, chosen precisely because it means *reduced*, not *absent*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pejor** = *"worse."* Late Latin *impejorare* = "to make worse" → Old French *empeirier* → **impair**. The word is literally *to worsen*.

Same root: **pejorative** (a word that makes something sound worse), and its superlative *pessimus* ("worst") gives **pessimist**.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *pair*.** The spelling is a coincidence of French sound-shift, not a reference to two of anything.

# **impugn** *(v.)* — **im-PYOON**

*The g is silent — never "im-PUG-n."*

### Means
To **attack something as false or dishonest** — to call into question a person's motives, integrity, or the validity of a claim.

The word is a frontal challenge, and it lands on *honesty*, not quality. You **criticise** a plan for being weak; you **impugn** it for being a lie. Note also that it stops short of proof: to *refute* an argument is to defeat it, but to **impugn** it is only to cast doubt on it — which is why the word carries a faint whiff of the accuser overreaching. Formal, and heavily legal and political: what usually gets impugned is a witness's credibility, a rival's motives, a court's independence.

⚠️ **impugn ≠ impute.**
- **impugn** = *attack* the honesty of a claim or person
- **impute** = *attribute* something (usually bad) to someone

You impute a selfish motive to him; you impugn his account of what happened.

### Trick to lock it in
> **imPUGN** hides **pug** — as in **pug**ilist, a boxer. To impugn is to throw a punch at someone's honesty.

It's a swing at credibility, not a knockout — the doubt is landed, the case is not yet won.

### In sentences
1. No one **impugned** her competence; it was her motives for taking the contract that the committee quietly questioned.
2. Say I got the numbers wrong, fine — but don't **impugn** my honesty in front of the whole team.

### In the wild
Standard courtroom vocabulary: counsel who tries to **impugn the credibility** of a witness — attack the person's truthfulness rather than the evidence — and the near-fixed political phrase *"I do not impugn my colleague's motives,"* which is almost always said right before doing exactly that.

### Where it comes from
Latin **impugnāre** = *"to fight against"* — *in-* (against) + *pugnāre* (to fight), from **pugnus**, *"fist."*

That fist unlocks a cluster: **pugnacious** (spoiling for a fight), **pugilist** (a boxer), **repugnant** (fighting *back* against — hence revolting).

# **incessant** *(adj.)* — **in-SES-uhnt**

### Means
Going on and on **without a pause**, especially something repetitive — noise, rain, questions, complaints.

*Constant* and *continuous* are neutral measurements; **incessant** is a complaint. Calling something incessant tells you the speaker is worn down by it and wants it to stop. That's why it attaches almost exclusively to irritants — *incessant chatter*, *incessant beeping*, *incessant demands* — and almost never to things we enjoy.

**incessant ≠ insistent.**
- **incessant** = it never *stops* (a matter of time)
- **insistent** = it won't take *no* (a matter of will)

A toddler's questions can be both — insistent because he demands an answer, incessant because he never runs out.

### Trick to lock it in
> **If it were *pleasant*, you'd never call it *incessant*.**

The rhyme locks in the built-in irritation: nothing you enjoy ever gets this word.

### In sentences
1. Three weeks of **incessant** rain had turned the field into a swamp.
2. I couldn't hear myself think over the **incessant** pinging of his group chat.

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** (*not*) + **cessāre** (*to stop, to cease*) — literally *not-ceasing*. You can hear **cease** sitting inside it.

*Cessāre* comes from **cēdere**, "to go, to yield," which is a workhorse GRE root: **cease, cessation, deceased, concede, recede, secede, accede, precedent**.

# **inclined** *(adj.)* — **in-KLYND**

### Means
Having a tendency, leaning, or willingness toward something (*inclined to agree*); also, literally sloping (*an inclined plane*).

On the GRE it's almost always the mental sense — and it is a **hedge**. Saying "I'm inclined to think so" commits you to a lean, not a position; the exit door stays open, which is why it's the language of committees and diplomats. Set it against its neighbours: *determined* or *bent on* is locked in, *prone* and *apt* describe a statistical tendency usually toward something bad (*prone to error*). *Inclined* is mild, neutral, and voluntary — a preference, not a flaw and not a decision.

### Trick to lock it in
> An **incline** is a slope, not a cliff — being *inclined* is a tilt of the mind, not a fall into commitment.

The word keeps its physical sense intact: you're tipped in one direction, but nothing has been settled.

### In sentences
1. The board is **inclined** to approve the merger, though it has asked for one further round of figures.
2. I'm **inclined** to say yes, but let me sleep on it.

### In the wild
Everyday English already uses the exact GRE sense in **"musically inclined," "mathematically inclined"** — someone whose natural bent tips that way, without any claim that they've trained for it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **inclinare** = *"to lean toward"* (*in-* + *clinare*, to bend or lean).

That root pays off across the exam: **recline** (lean back), **decline** (lean away, or downward), **proclivity** (a leaning-forward — a natural tendency), **declivity** / **acclivity** (a downward / upward slope), and even **climate** (Greek *klima*, the "slope" of the earth toward the sun).

# **inveterate** *(adj.)* — **in-VET-uh-ruht**

### Means
Fixed in a habit by long practice — doing something so persistently, for so long, that it has hardened into character. An **inveterate** gambler, liar, meddler, tea-drinker.

Two things set it apart from *habitual*. First, **time**: the habit has to be old, not just frequent. Second, **tone**: *habitual* is clinical, *chronic* belongs to disease, but *inveterate* is faintly amused — it's the word a profile writer uses for a charming vice. It can also attach to the habit itself rather than the person (*inveterate hostility*, *an inveterate prejudice*), and there it turns cold: too old to be argued away.

⚠️ **inveterate ≠ invertebrate.** One letter's difference in the eye, none in a hurried read — *invertebrate* is spineless (jellyfish, or a cowardly official). An inveterate coward has been one for decades; an invertebrate one simply has no backbone.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in-VETERAN-ate** — an inveterate liar is a *veteran* liar: years of service, decorated in the field.

The word literally hides *veteran*, and both come from Latin *vetus*, "old" — the habit has seniority.

### In sentences
1. Forty years on the bench had not softened his **inveterate** distrust of expert witnesses.
2. He's an **inveterate** snoozer — nine alarms, and he still rolls in at ten past.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vetus, veteris** = *"old"* → *inveterātus*, "made old, grown chronic."

Same root gives you **veteran** (an old hand) and **veterinary** (originally the medicine of *veterīnae*, old working beasts). If you can see *veteran* inside it, you have the meaning: aged into the job.

# **miserly** *(adj.)* — **MY-zer-lee**

*First syllable rhymes with *eye*, not with *miss* — despite looking like *misery*.*

### Means
Extremely unwilling to spend or give — hoarding money out of greed, and living meanly in the process.

This is not thrift. **Frugal** is a compliment, **thrifty** is neutral, **miserly** is contempt: the person has more than enough and still won't part with it, denying others *and themselves*. The word carries a whiff of the pitiful — a miser sitting cold in a dark room to save on coal. It also travels beyond money: you can be *miserly with praise*, *miserly with detail*, which means grudging and withholding, never merely economical.

### Trick to lock it in
> **MISER**LY has **MISERY** buried in it — stinginess that makes the hoarder wretched too.

The overlap is real, not a coincidence: both words come from Latin *miser*, "wretched," which is exactly the judgment the word passes on the hoarder.

### In sentences
1. He left a fortune of nine million pounds and a house with no heating — a **miserly** end to a **miserly** life.
2. Don't be **miserly** with the cheese, mate, it's a pizza not a rice cracker.

### In the wild
**Ebenezer Scrooge** in *A Christmas Carol* is the miser the word was made for — rich, freezing, refusing Bob Cratchit an extra lump of coal.

### Where it comes from
Latin **miser** = *"wretched, pitiable."* Note what that means: the root doesn't describe hoarding at all — it describes the *state* of the hoarder, which is the built-in verdict.

Same root: **misery**, **miserable**, **commiserate** (*to be wretched together* = to sympathise), and the **Miserere** psalm ("have mercy").

# **patent** *(adj. (also n. / v.))* — **PAT-uhnt**

*British speakers usually say PAY-tuhnt for the "obvious" sense, keeping PAT-uhnt for the invention.*

### Means
**Adj.:** obvious, plain for anyone to see — needing no proof or argument. (The familiar noun — an exclusive right to an invention — is the same word, explained below.)

Register is formal-to-legal, and the company it keeps is telling: a **patent lie**, **patent nonsense**, a **patent absurdity**, **patently unfair**. Almost nobody calls a pleasant fact patent. Where *obvious* is neutral and *evident* suggests something you inferred from evidence, *patent* means the thing is lying wide open — and the speaker is usually exasperated that anyone pretended otherwise.

⚠️ **patent vs. latent** — exact opposites, one letter apart.
- **patent** = lying **open**, in plain sight
- **latent** = lying **hidden**, not yet showing

A latent flaw becomes patent the day the bridge falls.

### Trick to lock it in
> You never hear a *patent truth* — only a **patent lie**.

The word attaches almost exclusively to falsehood and absurdity, so learn it as the adjective you use when something false is insultingly easy to see through.

### In sentences
1. The defence rested on a claim of ignorance that the documents rendered **patently** false.
2. Come on — that excuse is **patent** nonsense and you know it.

### In the wild
Newsrooms and courtrooms run on the adverb: **"patently false," "patently absurd," "patently unfair"** turn up constantly in reporting and judgments — you have almost certainly read it this month without registering it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **patēre** = *"to lie open."* A medieval king's grant came as *litterae patentes*, **"letters patent"** — an open document, unsealed so anyone could read it. That single image runs both senses: what is patent lies open to view, and a patent is your claim published openly for all to see.

Its mirror is **latēre**, *"to lie hidden"* → **latent**. Open vs. hidden, one letter apart.

# **petulant** *(adj.)* — **PET-yuu-luhnt**

### Means
Sulky and bad-tempered in a **childish** way — snapping, pouting, or huffing over something small.

The word is a put-down of the *scale* of the anger, not just its heat. **Irascible** or **truculent** grant the anger some force; **petulant** denies it any dignity — it says a grown adult is behaving like a thwarted six-year-old, and the offence they're nursing wasn't worth it. You can be petulant about a cold coffee; you cannot be petulant about an injustice.

### Trick to lock it in
> **PET**ulant — sulking over **pet peeves**, not real wrongs.

The hidden *pet* keeps the key limit in view: the grievance is small and personal, which is exactly why the sulking looks childish.

### In sentences
1. The minister's **petulant** refusal to answer the question told the chamber more than any answer would have.
2. He went all **petulant** because we started the film without him.

### In the wild
Football commentary owns this word: a needless retaliatory kick at an opponent is always "a **petulant** kick" — the standard verdict on Beckham's red card against Argentina in 1998.

### Where it comes from
Latin **petere** = *"to seek, to make for, to attack."* A *petulans* was someone forever lunging at things — impudent, unable to sit still under a slight.

The root is everywhere on the GRE: **petition** (seek a favour), **appetite** (seek food), **impetus** and **impetuous** (rushing at), **compete** (seek alongside), **repeat** (seek again), **centripetal** (seeking the centre).

# **pithy** *(adj.)* — **PITH-ee**

*th as in *thin*, not *this*.*

### Means
Short **and** loaded — few words carrying real substance.

Its neighbours are all colder. *Concise* only promises nothing is wasted; *terse* hints at bluntness bordering on rude; *curt* has crossed that line; *laconic* describes a person who habitually says little. **Pithy** is the one that promises the line was *good* — you cannot be pithy and dull. It's the word for a great one-liner, a proverb, an epigram, a summary that beats the report it replaced. Almost always praise, and usually praise for the writing or speaking, not the person.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pith** is the core running down a plant stem — *pithy* means **all core, no padding**.

Strip a sentence of everything that isn't load-bearing and what's left is the pith — which is why "pithy" compliments the content, not just the length.

### In sentences
1. Her dissent ran to a single **pithy** paragraph that was quoted more often than the ninety-page majority opinion.
2. Keep the toast **pithy** — two lines, one joke, sit down.

### In the wild
Hamlet, mid-"To be or not to be": *"enterprises of great **pith** and moment"* — pith there meaning weight, substance, the stuff that matters.

# **pliant** *(adj.)* — **PLY-uhnt**

### Means
**Literally:** bending easily without breaking — willow branches, leather, young wood.
**Of people:** easily influenced, yielding to whoever is pushing.

Two very different temperatures depending on the object. Applied to a material it's pure praise (*pliant* leather is good leather). Applied to a person it's rarely a compliment: *flexible* says you adapt well, *pliant* says you have no resistance in you — you take the shape of whoever's hands you're in. Softer and more passive than **docile** (which implies obedience to authority) and nowhere near as grovelling as **obsequious**; a pliant person isn't fawning, just shapeable. It's the word a manipulator would use about a mark.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pli**ant = **ply**wood — thin layers that bend instead of snapping, because there's nothing solid inside.

The material sense and the human sense are the same fact: it gives way under pressure rather than holding its shape.

### In sentences
1. The old bookbinder chose a pliant calfskin that would fold around the spine without cracking.
2. They wanted a pliant deputy, not someone who'd argue back in meetings.

### Where it comes from
Latin **plicāre** = *"to fold."* That one root folds out into a huge family: **ply**, **pliers**, **pliable**, **apply**, **comply**, **imply**, **reply**, **complicated** (folded together), **duplicate** (folded twice), and ballet's **plié** — a bend at the knee.

Note **comply** especially: comply and pliant are the same fold, which is exactly why the human sense drifts toward "does what he's told."

# **sanctimonious** *(adj.)* — **sank-tuh-MOH-nee-uhs**

### Means
Making a **showy display of moral superiority** — preaching virtue in a tone that says *and you fall short*.

The word is a verdict on the performance, not the piety. A person can be genuinely devout without being sanctimonious; what earns the label is the **parade** — the sighing, the lecture, the visible halo. It also carries a strong hint of hypocrisy: the sanctimonious usually moralise about a sin they quietly commit. Note who uses it — **nobody ever calls themselves sanctimonious.** It is always an accusation, thrown at someone whose righteousness has become unbearable.

⚠️ **Don't hear *parsimonious*.** Same **-monious** ending, unrelated word: *parsimonious* = **stingy with money**; *sanctimonious* = **preachy about morals**. Miserly wallet vs. superior tone.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sanct** (saint) + **-monious** (like *cere**monious***) = **sainthood performed as ceremony.**

The saintliness is staged for an audience, which is exactly why the word stings.

### In sentences
1. The editorial struck a **sanctimonious** tone, condemning a corruption its own proprietor had profited from for a decade.
2. Spare me the **sanctimonious** speech about recycling — you flew to Ibiza three times last year.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sanctus** = *"holy, consecrated."* One root, a whole GRE cluster: **sanctify** (make holy), **sanctuary** (holy place, hence refuge), **sacrosanct** (untouchable), **sanctum**, **saint**.

*Sanctimonious* once simply meant *holy* — it soured into sarcasm, and the sneer is now permanent.

# **sound** *(adj. / v.)* — **SOWND**

### Means
**Adj.:** free from defect, damage or error — **whole, healthy, structurally solid**. A *sound* building, a *sound* argument, a *sound* night's sleep, *of sound mind*.

**Verb (the GRE's other trick):** to **measure the depth** of water — and by extension to probe cautiously. You *sound out* a colleague before proposing something; you *sound the depths* of a problem.

The praise in *sound* is deliberately unglamorous. Calling a plan *sound* is not calling it brilliant, imaginative or bold — it's saying you have looked for the crack and there isn't one. It's the verdict of an engineer or an auditor, not an admirer: reliability, not flair. Hence the sting hidden in "a sound, if unexciting, choice." The negative, **unsound**, is much harsher than it looks — *unsound reasoning* means broken at the foundation, and *of unsound mind* is a legal finding of incapacity.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sound** sleep, **sound** mind, **sound** argument — the word never means *good*, it means *nothing in it is broken*.

Run the collocations and you can hear that *sound* is always certifying an absence of flaws rather than a presence of merit.

### In sentences
1. The auditors found the firm's accounting **sound**, though its strategy struck them as timid.
2. That's **sound** advice — boring, but he's never once been wrong about this stuff.

### In the wild
Taylor Swift's **"Safe & Sound"** (from *The Hunger Games*) — *sound* there isn't noise, it's the old meaning: unharmed, whole, nothing broken.

### Where it comes from
Old English **gesund** = *"healthy, uninjured"* — the same word as German **gesund**, which is why you say **Gesundheit!** when someone sneezes: *health to you*. Nothing to do with noise.

🚫 **Three unrelated "sounds."** *Sound* (noise) is Latin **sonus** (*sonic, sonorous, resonate*). *Sound* (to measure depth) is Latin **sub + unda**, "under the wave" (*sounding, sound out*). *Sound* (a channel of water, Puget Sound) is Norse **sund**. Only the healthy one is *gesund*.

# **tarnish** *(v. / n.)* — **TAR-nish**

### Means
**Verb:** to lose (or cause to lose) shine — metal going dull and discoloured; by extension, to damage a reputation.
**Noun:** the dull film itself.

The damage is **surface-level and gradual**. A scandal doesn't tarnish a career the way it *ruins* one — tarnish means the gleam is gone, not the thing. It also implies there *was* a shine: you can only tarnish something that was admired. And unlike *sully* or *besmirch*, which sound morally outraged, tarnish is cool and factual — the word a newspaper uses.

⚠️ **tarnish ≠ burnish.** Same ending, opposite jobs: **burnish** = rub *to* a shine (and figuratively, to *boost* a reputation); **tarnish** = lose the shine. A PR firm burnishes an image; a leak tarnishes it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Varnish** goes on and adds gloss; **tarnish** creeps in and takes it away.

One letter apart, exact opposites — and both are about a thin surface layer, never the substance underneath.

### In sentences
1. The verdict was overturned, but the accusation had already **tarnished** a reputation forty years in the making.
2. Don't put the good spoons in the damp cupboard — they'll **tarnish** in a week.

### In the wild
In *Elden Ring*, you play as **the Tarnished** — an exile stripped of the golden grace they once had. Fallen from shine, not destroyed: exactly the word.

# **tepid** *(adj.)* — **TEP-id**

### Means
**Literally:** lukewarm — neither hot nor cold. **Figuratively:** showing little enthusiasm, warmth, or conviction.

The figurative sense is the one the GRE wants, and its sting is that it isn't hostility. A tepid response doesn't attack you; it just fails to care. Cold means rejection, hot means passion — tepid is the flat middle where nobody could be bothered either way. That's why "tepid applause" and "a tepid endorsement" land harder than outright criticism: praise with the heat taken out reads as polite dismissal.

⚠️ **tepid ≠ torpid.**
- **tepid** = *lukewarm*, unenthusiastic
- **torpid** = *sluggish*, dormant, barely moving (a hibernating bear is torpid)

A tepid audience claps limply; a torpid audience is asleep.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tepid** rhymes with **insipid** — and both mean *meh*.

Insipid food has no flavour and tepid feeling has no heat, so the shared "-pid" ending files them together as the vocabulary of underwhelm.

### In sentences
1. The proposal met with a **tepid** reception from the board — no objections raised, and no hands offered either.
2. He said he "liked" my haircut in this **tepid** little voice, which honestly was worse than saying nothing.

### In the wild
"**Tepid** reviews" is standard critic-speak — the word that surfaces when a film isn't a disaster, just a shrug, and the Rotten Tomatoes score parks somewhere around 45%.

# **upbraid** *(v.)* — **up-BRAYD**

### Means
To **scold someone severely**, reproaching them face-to-face for a specific fault.

You always upbraid someone *for* something — the offence gets named. That's what separates it from **berate** (sheer volume), **reprimand** (official, on the record), and **chide** (mild, almost fond). Upbraiding is *reproach*: moral disappointment with an edge, delivered by someone who assumes they hold the high ground. It's also bookish — people don't upbraid each other in a kitchen argument, they write about it afterwards.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Up + braid** — your faults get dragged **up** and **braided** together into one long rope.

Upbraiding isn't one sharp bark, it's a sustained recital of everything you did wrong, woven strand by strand.

### In sentences
1. The abbot **upbraided** the young monk for his idleness, item by item, until the boy stared at the flagstones.
2. She **upbraided** him for forgetting her birthday — again — and he had absolutely no defence.

### In the wild
The King James Bible, James 1:5: God *"giveth to all men liberally, and **upbraideth** not"* — i.e. gives without throwing it back in your face later. That line is why the word still sounds pulpit-formal.

### Where it comes from
Old English **upbrēdan** = *up* + **bregdan** ("to pull, snatch, weave" — the ancestor of **braid**). The original sense was to *throw something up against* a person as a reproach. The fault is lifted up and flung in their face.

# **vexation** *(n.)* — **vek-SAY-shuhn**

### Means
**Irritation and frustrated annoyance** — or the *thing* causing it ("the leak was a constant vexation").

Scale matters: fury is hot and outward, vexation is low-grade and inward — the teeth-grinding of someone stuck with a petty, repeated obstacle they can't fix. It carries a whiff of helplessness that *anger* doesn't, and it's the more bookish word: you write of a character's vexation, you shout in rage. Law keeps the harsher old sense alive in **vexatious litigation** — lawsuits filed purely to harass.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Vex rhymes with hex** — a small curse that nags at you, not a lightning bolt.

Vexation is the persistent low-level torment of a minor jinx, not the explosion of real rage.

### In sentences
1. Her letters betray a quiet **vexation** at being praised for her husband's work.
2. Three trips to the DMV in one week — you can imagine my **vexation**.

### In the wild
The King James Bible, Ecclesiastes: *"all is vanity and **vexation** of spirit."* Everything is pointless, and it's irritating.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vexāre** = *"to shake, jolt, harass"* — literally to be rattled about. Gives **vex**, **vexatious**, and the **vexed question** (one shaken back and forth without resolution).
