# **abrasive** *(adj. / n.)* — **uh-BRAY-siv**

### Means
**Literally:** capable of scraping or wearing down a surface — sandpaper, grit, steel wool. As a noun, the scratchy substance itself.
**Of a person or manner:** harsh, rough-edged, irritating to deal with.

The metaphor does the work: an abrasive person doesn't wound you, they *wear* on you. The fault is texture, not malice — blunt, tactless, no social cushioning. Which is why it's the standard HR euphemism for someone effective and unpleasant: "brilliant, but abrasive." Compare *caustic* (burns, and means to) or *vicious* (aims to hurt); abrasive is friction, often unintentional and usually chronic.

### Trick to lock it in
> **abrasive** ≈ *a-BRACE-ive* — you **brace** yourself before the meeting.

Not because they'll destroy you, but because half an hour of that scraping leaves you raw.

### In sentences
1. His feedback was accurate in every particular and **abrasive** in every syllable; the junior staff stopped submitting drafts.
2. Don't take it personally — she's like that with everyone, just a bit **abrasive** before her second coffee.

### In the wild
Read the back of a bathroom cleaner or a whitening toothpaste: **"non-abrasive"** — i.e. it won't scratch the enamel. That's the whole word in one label, and abrasive people scratch the same way.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ab-** ("away") + **rādere** ("to scrape") — to scrape away.

*Rādere* is a quiet workhorse: **erase** (scrape out), **razor** (the scraper), **raze** (scrape a building to the ground), **abrade**, **rash**. Learn the root once, get five words.

# **artful** *(adj.)* — **AHRT-fuhl**

### Means
Clever and skilful — but on the GRE, almost always **skill bent toward getting one over on you**: cunning, wily, sly.

Two live senses, and the sly one dominates. An *artful* dodge, an *artful* question, an *artful* change of subject: the person is good at it, and that's exactly the problem. Compare *cunning* (colder, more predatory) and *devious* (flatly disapproving) — *artful* keeps a note of grudging admiration, because the craft really is impressive. The tamer sense survives in phrases about arrangement — an *artful* display of flowers — where it just means tastefully contrived.

⚠️ **artless is not the opposite you expect.** *Artless* doesn't mean *clumsy* — it means **guileless, naively sincere**. The opposite of *artful*'s cunning, not of its skill.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Con artist.** The *art* is in the con, not on the canvas.

An artful person has genuine skill, and is using it to work you.

### In sentences
1. His resignation letter was an **artful** piece of work: three paragraphs of praise that left everyone certain he had been pushed.
2. She gave an **artful** little shrug and somehow I ended up paying for dinner.

### In the wild
**The Artful Dodger** in *Oliver Twist* — Dickens's teenage pickpocket, all charm and sleight of hand. The nickname *is* the definition.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ars, artis** = *"skill, craft"* — which gives you **artisan, artifice, artifact, artificial**, and even **inert** (*in-* + *ars* = "without skill," hence sluggish, doing nothing).

Note the slide: English **craft** did the same thing, going from *skill* to *crafty*. Skill words drift toward trickery, because a trickster is only dangerous if he's good.

# **avert** *(v.)* — **uh-VERT**

### Means
Two senses, one gesture.
**1.** To **turn away** — usually the eyes or the face: *she averted her gaze.*
**2.** To **ward off** something bad that was already coming: *the pilot averted a collision.*

Sense 2 is where the GRE lives, and it is narrower than *prevent*. You prevent anything — rust, boredom, a goal. You **avert** only a looming harm, and the word carries the sense of a last-minute deflection: the disaster was on its way and got turned aside. *Forestall* means you acted early; *avoid* means you personally sidestepped it; *avert* means the danger was real, close, and pushed off course. Sense 1 keeps the same flinch — averting your eyes is admitting there's something you can't look at.

⚠️ **avert ≠ aver.** *Aver* (uh-VUR) means to **state firmly as fact** — "he averred his innocence." One letter, opposite energy: *avert* turns away from something, *aver* plants a flag on it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **You only ever avert two things: your eyes, and a disaster.** Both get turned away at the last second.

Lock the collocation and you get the nuance free — the object is always something bad you refuse to meet head-on.

### In sentences
1. Only a hastily brokered ceasefire **averted** a wider war.
2. He **averted** his eyes when she mentioned the money he still owed.

### In the wild
**\"Crisis averted.\"** — the stock phrase every news anchor and every group chat reaches for when a near-miss ends well.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ab-** (*away*) + **vertere** (*to turn*) — literally *turn away*.

*Vertere* is one of the highest-yield roots on the exam: **divert** (turn aside), **invert** (turn upside down), **convert** (turn together, change), **subvert** (turn from beneath), **revert** (turn back), **vertigo** (the room turning), **versatile** (turns to any task), **adverse** (turned against you).

# **avid** *(adj.)* — **AV-id**

### Means
Keenly enthusiastic; devoted to a pursuit to the point of appetite. Older, sharper sense: **greedy for** something — *avid for power*.

Two settings, and the GRE tests both. Followed by a noun, it's warm and almost domestic — an **avid reader**, an **avid golfer**, someone whose hobby is a habit. Followed by **for** or **of**, it turns hungry: *avid for praise*, *avid for revenge* — that's craving, not fondness. **Eager** is a burst of feeling before one event; **avid** is a standing appetite.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Avid** and **avarice** are the same craving — avarice wants your money, avid wants one more chapter.

Both come from Latin *avēre*, "to crave," so avid is greed with the appetite pointed at something harmless.

### In sentences
1. Two decades in opposition had left him **avid for** office, and the caution of his younger self was gone.
2. She's an **avid** baker — there hasn't been a weekend without flour on the counter since March.

### In the wild
The stock phrase of every CV hobbies line and dating-app bio: *avid reader, avid hiker, avid traveller.* Nobody writes "avid" any other way — which is exactly the collocation the GRE expects you to know.

### Where it comes from
Latin **avēre** = *"to crave, to long for"* → **avidus**, "greedy, eager." The same root gives **avarice** and **avaricious** — the money-hungry cousins. Knowing they're relatives is what preserves the hungry edge of *avid for*.

# **brevity** *(n.)* — **BREV-uh-tee**

### Means
Shortness — either of **expression** (few words) or of **duration** (a short span of time).

Applied to speech or writing, it's almost always praise: brevity is a *skill*, the discipline of cutting to the point. That's what separates it from **terseness** (short to the point of rudeness) and **curtness** (short because annoyed). *Conciseness* is its closest neighbour, but conciseness stresses density of content, brevity simply stresses shortness — a one-word answer has brevity even if it says little.

The time sense runs the other way emotionally: **the brevity of life**, of a friendship, of summer — there it's wistful, a complaint that the good thing ended.

⚠️ **brevity ≠ levity.** One letter apart, both GRE staples: **brevity** = shortness; **levity** = lightness, not-taking-it-seriously (Latin *levis*, "light" — same root as *alleviate*).

### Trick to lock it in
> **brev + -ity** — the *-ity* turns "brief" into a **quality you possess**, like *clarity* or *purity*.

That suffix is why brevity reads as a virtue rather than a measurement: a memo isn't just short, it *has* brevity.

### In sentences
1. The judge thanked counsel for her **brevity**, noting that the afternoon's argument had run to four hours on the same point.
2. Keep it short — **brevity** is your friend when nobody reads past the first line of an email.

### In the wild
Polonius in *Hamlet*: **"Brevity is the soul of wit"** — a line he delivers in the middle of a long, rambling speech, which is the joke.

### Where it comes from
Latin **brevis** = *"short."* It's hiding in a big family: **brief**, **abbreviate**, **abridge**, **breviary** (the shortened prayer book), and the musical **breve**.

# **bromide** *(n.)* — **BROH-mide**

### Means
A **trite, comforting remark** offered as if it were wisdom — "everything happens for a reason," "time heals all wounds." By extension, **a tediously conventional person** who deals in such remarks.

What separates it from *cliché* or *platitude* is the sedative effect. A cliché is merely worn out; a bromide is worn out **and deployed to soothe** — to smooth over grief, conflict, or hard questions so nobody has to think. Calling something a bromide accuses the speaker of dodging, not just of being unoriginal. The word itself is bookish; it shows up in criticism, obituaries of speeches, and political columns far more than in speech.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **bro**mide is what your **bro** says when he has nothing useful: *"it is what it is, mate."*

The hidden **bro** flags the key nuance — it's meant kindly and lands as comfortingly empty, not merely stale.

### In sentences
1. The minister's statement offered nothing but **bromides** about resilience and lessons learned, and not one word about who had signed off on the contract.
2. Don't give me the "everything happens for a reason" **bromide** — I just want someone to admit it went wrong.

### In the wild
The modern specimen everyone recognises: **"thoughts and prayers"** after a disaster — soothing, automatic, and criticised precisely because it substitutes for action. That is a bromide in one phrase.

### Where it comes from
Greek **brōmos** = *"stench."* It named **bromine**, a foul-smelling element — and **potassium bromide** was the standard Victorian **sedative**, dosed out for nerves, insomnia and hysteria.

So a bromide is literally a **tranquilliser in verbal form**: a phrase administered to calm you down. Once you see the drug, the meaning never slips.

# **circumstantial** *(adj.)* — **ser-kuhm-STAN-shuhl**

### Means
Depending on **circumstances** rather than on direct proof — and so, of evidence, **indirect**: it points to a conclusion by inference instead of demonstrating it.

A second, quieter sense: **incidental, not essential** (*circumstantial details* of a plan). A third, older one runs the opposite way — *a circumstantial account* means one crammed with particulars.

The word almost always carries a downgrade. Calling a case **circumstantial** concedes that nobody saw it happen; the facts sit around the crime, fitting it neatly, without ever touching it. Note the strange tension the word holds: circumstantial evidence can be abundant, minute and utterly convincing — and still, by definition, unwitnessed.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sub**stantial evidence stands *under* the claim and holds it up; **circum**stantial evidence only stands *around* it.

Both words are built on Latin *stare*, "to stand" — the prefix decides whether the facts support the conclusion or merely surround it.

### In sentences
1. The prosecution's case was wholly **circumstantial**: a receipt, a tyre track, and a man who could not say where he had been.
2. Look, they saw his car outside — that's **circumstantial**, it doesn't prove he did anything.

### In the wild
The stock line of every courtroom drama and true-crime podcast: *"It's all circumstantial."* Said by a defence lawyer, it means *you can't prove it*; said by a juror, it usually means *but we all know*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **circum** (*around*) + **stare** (*to stand*) → *circumstantia*, "the things standing around" — the surroundings of an act.

Both halves pay off elsewhere: **circum-** gives *circumvent, circumscribe, circumspect, circumference*; **stare** gives *substantial, status, stance, stationary*.

# **cloying** *(adj.)* — **KLOY-ing**

### Means
So sweet, rich, or sentimental that it becomes **sickening** — pleasure that curdles from too much of itself.

The word is only ever an insult, and it carries a physical aftertaste: the third slice of cake, perfume that fills the lift, a film that keeps telling you to cry. Sweetness alone isn't cloying — *excess without a break* is. Note the built-in time signature: something becomes cloying, usually after it was briefly pleasant.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cloying** rhymes with **annoying** — sweetness that stays until it annoys.

The rhyme carries the whole nuance: the problem isn't the sugar, it's the *not stopping*.

### In sentences
1. The novel's final chapter dissolves into **cloying** sentiment, as though the author distrusted his readers to feel anything unprompted.
2. Her Christmas playlist is fine for a day, but by the fourth of January it's just **cloying**.

### In the wild
It's a staple of film criticism — the standard word reviewers reach for when a movie's emotional score, voiceover, and slow-motion hugs are all working on you at once.

### Where it comes from
From **cloy**, older sense *"to clog, to block up"* — via Old French *encloer*, from Latin **clavus** = *"nail."* To cloy a gun was to nail its touch-hole shut.

That's the useful image: cloying sweetness **clogs**. Same *clavus* gives you **clove** — the spice literally named for looking like a little nail.

# **counterfeit** *(adj. / n. / v.)* — **KOWN-ter-fit**

*Last syllable is "-fit," not "-feet" or "-fyte," despite the spelling.*

### Means
**Adj.:** made in exact imitation of something genuine **in order to deceive**. **Noun:** the fake item itself. **Verb:** to make such an imitation (or, more loosely, to feign a feeling).

Two things have to be true: there's a real original, and someone means to pass the copy off as it. A *replica* is honest, an *imitation* may be harmless, a *fake* is the everyday catch-all — **counterfeit** is the cold, legal-sounding one, the word that appears on a seizure notice. It attaches most naturally to money, passports, medicines, luxury goods: things whose whole value is that they're authentic.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Counterfeit** money has to cross a **counter** — the fake only counts if someone takes it as real.

That built-in "counter" keeps the essential part in view: a counterfeit isn't just a copy, it's a copy made to be *passed*.

### In sentences
1. Customs officers destroyed some forty thousand **counterfeit** vials, each stamped with a pharmaceutical logo it had no right to bear.
2. That's a **counterfeit** — look at the stitching, no way that bag came out of a Prada factory.

### In the wild
The felt-tip pen a cashier swipes across your banknote is a **counterfeit-detection pen** — it darkens on ordinary wood-pulp paper, but not on the cotton stock real currency is printed on.

### Where it comes from
Old French **contrefait** = *"made against"* — Latin **contra** (against) + **facere** (to make). Something manufactured in opposition to the real thing.

That **facere** shows up disguised as **-feit / -fit** in a small family worth knowing: **forfeit** (something "done wrong," hence surrendered as a penalty), **surfeit** (over-done, an excess), **benefit** (well-done). Same root, undisguised, in **fact, factory, manufacture, artifice**.

# **denigrate** *(v.)* — **DEN-ih-grayt**

### Means
To attack someone's reputation or belittle their worth — to run them down unfairly.

The unfairness is baked in. You can *criticise* a book fairly, but to **denigrate** it is to talk it down beyond what it deserves, and the word quietly convicts the speaker as much as the target. It also tends to be a campaign rather than an outburst: a steady drip of belittling remarks, often about a person's character, competence, or achievements. Register is formal — it's the word of editorials, HR policies and eulogies-in-reverse, not of a pub argument.

**denigrate ≠ deprecate.** To *denigrate* is to blacken someone else's name; to *deprecate* is to express mild disapproval — which is why *self-deprecating* is charming (you're gently mocking yourself) and self-*denigrating* would be bleak.

### Trick to lock it in
> **de-NIG-RATE** — you *de*-rate them: knock their rating down below what it's worth.

The word ends in the sound of *rate*, and denigrating is exactly that — marking someone down unfairly rather than judging them accurately.

### In sentences
1. The memoir is generous to his rivals, refusing to **denigrate** even the men who engineered his downfall.
2. Stop **denigrating** your own work — you spent two years on that thing.

### Where it comes from
Latin **de-** (*completely*) + **niger** (*black*) → *denigrare*, "to blacken." You blacken a name, and the reputation is what's stained.

Same *niger* gives French **noir** — as in **film noir**, the genre literally named "black film."

# **disciple** *(n.)* — **di-SEYE-puhl**

*Stress on the middle, not the first syllable — never DISS-ih-pull.*

### Means
A **committed pupil of a particular teacher** — someone who learns a doctrine directly from a master, lives by it, and passes it on.

The word is heavier than *follower* and more personal than *adherent*. You can be a follower of a trend or an adherent of a doctrine, but a disciple is bound to a **person**: there is a master, there is submission, and the loyalty runs deeper than agreement. In secular use it keeps a faint religious perfume, which is why journalists reach for it when a business or political figure has a circle who repeat his lines a bit too faithfully — "a disciple of Milton Friedman" can be respect or a raised eyebrow.

### Trick to lock it in
> A follower follows a **cause**; a disciple follows a **teacher**.

The word always implies a named master standing at the other end of the devotion.

### In sentences
1. Freud's early **disciples** split into rival schools within a decade of his death, each claiming to hold the true method.
2. He's such a **disciple** of that fitness guy that he won't eat before noon anymore.

### In the wild
The **Twelve Disciples** — and the label is exact: as *disciples* they were the ones being taught, and only once sent out did the same men get called *apostles* (Greek *apostolos*, "one sent out"). Disciple = learner, apostle = messenger.

### Where it comes from
Latin **discipulus** = *"pupil,"* from **discere**, *"to learn."*

That gives you **discipline** — originally the *training given to a pupil*, only later the punishment that enforces it. Its twin root **docēre**, *"to teach,"* covers the other half of the classroom: **docile** (teachable), **doctor** (teacher), **doctrine**, **document**.

# **extol** *(v.)* — **ik-STOHL**

### Means
To **praise highly and enthusiastically** — usually out loud, at length, and in public.

*Praise* is the plain word; **extol** turns the volume up and adds a podium. You commend an employee in a review, but you extol a hero at a ceremony. The register is elevated, almost sermon-like, which is why it carries a faint risk of overdoing it: when a politician **extols the virtues of** hard work, the word is doing half the eye-rolling for you. Note that collocation — *extol the virtues/merits/benefits of* is where this verb lives about 80% of the time.

⚠️ **extol ≠ exhort.** To **extol** is to *praise* someone; to **exhort** is to *urge* them to act. A coach exhorts the team at half-time and extols them afterwards.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ex-TOLL** — you ring the bells for them.

Bells are loud, public and celebratory, which is exactly how extolling differs from quietly praising.

### In sentences
1. The obituary **extolled** her four decades of service without once mentioning the scandal that ended them.
2. Dad won't shut up about that air fryer — he's been **extolling** it to strangers in the queue.

### In the wild
Psalm 145 opens *"I will **extol** thee, my God, O king"* — the King James phrasing that fixed this word in its worshipful, ceremonial register.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** ("up") + **tollere** ("to lift, raise") — to extol is literally to *raise someone up*, onto the pedestal.

Same lifting root gives you **elate** (spirits raised) and the **-lation** words built on *tollere*'s partner form *lātus*: **relate, translate, prelate, superlative**.

# **forebode** *(v.)* — **for-BOHD**

### Means
To be an **omen of something bad**; or, of a person, to **feel in advance** that something bad is coming.

The word runs one way only. *Predict* and *portend* are directionally neutral — you can portend a golden age. Nothing ever forebodes good luck: the verb carries the dread built in, so it needs no adjective to darken it. Note also that the bare verb is now rare and faintly literary; the living forms are the noun **foreboding** ("a sense of foreboding") and the adjective ("a foreboding silence"), and those are what the GRE will usually put in front of you.

⚠️ **foreboding ≠ forbidding.**
- **foreboding** = *signalling doom to come* — a foreboding hush before the verdict
- **forbidding** = *harsh, unwelcoming to look at* — a forbidding grey fortress

A forbidding cliff is merely ugly and steep; a foreboding cliff is about to have someone fall off it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Bode** takes *well* or *ill* — **forebode** only ever takes *ill*.

Test it by collocation: if you can't slot in *disaster*, *doom* or *ruin* after it, you want *portend* or *predict* instead.

### In sentences
1. The stillness of the birds, the fishermen said, **foreboded** a storm none of the instruments had yet registered.
2. I don't know why, but I had this **foreboding** all morning that he was going to quit.

### In the wild
The two low strings under a horror-film trailer, before anything has happened on screen — that's a **foreboding** score, telling you the bad thing is coming while the character is still fine.

### Where it comes from
**fore-** (*before*) + Old English **bodian** (*to announce*), from **boda** = *"a messenger."* A forebode is literally a messenger who arrives ahead of the event — and this one only ever brings bad news.

Same Germanic root sits in **bid** in its old sense of *announce, command* — which is where **forbid** comes from.

# **ideologue** *(n.)* — **EYE-dee-uh-log**

*Also heard as ID-ee-uh-log; the "-logue" rhymes with dog.*

### Means
Someone who follows a system of ideas — political, economic, religious — **rigidly and on principle**, fitting the facts to the doctrine rather than the doctrine to the facts.

It is almost always thrown, not claimed. Nobody calls themselves an ideologue. The word's whole force is the accusation of **inflexibility**: the person has stopped thinking and started applying. Its natural opposite in political writing is *pragmatist* — the one who'll take the deal that works. Having strong beliefs doesn't make you an ideologue; refusing to update them does.

⚠️ **ideologue ≠ idealist.**
- **idealist** = holds high, hopeful principles — usually admiring, sometimes gently mocking ("naive")
- **ideologue** = holds rigid doctrinal principles — always a charge of dogmatism

An idealist wants the world to be better. An ideologue already knows exactly how it must be.

### Trick to lock it in
> An ideo**logue** gives a mono**logue**, never a dia**logue**.

Same ending, same behaviour — he talks the doctrine at you and nothing comes back the other way.

### In sentences
1. The reformers were sidelined by **ideologues** who preferred a doctrinally pure defeat to a compromised victory.
2. He's not evil, he's just an **ideologue** — every problem gets the same answer before he's even heard it.

### In the wild
Standard political-press vocabulary: a nominee is graded as either "a pragmatist" or "an ideologue," and only one of those is a compliment.

### Where it comes from
**idea** + Greek **logos** = *"word, speech, reasoning."* Coined in Revolutionary France for the *idéologues*, philosophers who claimed to have a science of ideas — Napoleon used it as a sneer for impractical theorists, and the sneer stuck.

Mind the two endings — they're different roots and worth separating for good:
- **-logue** = *speaking* (logos): **monologue, dialogue, prologue, epilogue**
- **-gogue** = *leading* (Greek *agein*): **demagogue** (leads the mob), **pedagogue** (leads the child), **synagogue** (leads together)

So an **ideologue** talks the doctrine; a **demagogue** leads the crowd.

# **impediment** *(n.)* — **im-PED-uh-muhnt**

### Means
Something that **gets in the way** of progress — a hindrance, a drag on movement toward a goal.

An *obstacle* sits in front of you; an **impediment** clings to you. It's the formal, slightly clinical word, and it usually names a condition rather than an event — a **speech impediment**, a legal impediment to a marriage, an impediment to trade. It slows and encumbers rather than absolutely blocks: impediments are *removed* or *overcome*, never smashed through.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's a **PED** in it — an impediment is at your **feet**, not in your path.

It's the thing tangling your ankles as you walk, which is why it drags on progress instead of halting it outright.

### In sentences
1. The committee identified visa delays as the single greatest **impediment** to recruiting foreign researchers.
2. His only real **impediment** was that he couldn't shut up in meetings.

### In the wild
The wedding line every film interrupts: the celebrant asks whether anyone knows *"any just cause or impediment"* why the two should not be married — then, *speak now or forever hold your peace*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **impedīre** = *"to shackle the feet"* — *in-* (in) + *pes, pedis* (foot). Its exact opposite is **expedite**: *ex-* + *ped-*, to **free** the feet, get things moving.

The root pays out everywhere: **impede, expedite, pedestrian, pedal, pedestal, biped**.

🚫 Not the *ped-* in **pediatric, pedagogue, pedant** — that's Greek *pais, paidos*, "child." Same spelling, different body part.

# **imperative** *(adj. / n.)* — **im-PERR-uh-tiv**

### Means
**Adj.:** absolutely necessary, not optional. **Noun:** an essential duty or an order that must be obeyed (*the moral imperative to act*).

Note the force behind it: *necessary* states a fact, *urgent* states a deadline, but **imperative** speaks with the voice of command — something is demanding this of you, and refusing carries consequences. It is the register of policy papers, boardrooms and generals, which is why "it is imperative that we act now" lands harder than "we should act now." Grammar borrows the same force for the **imperative mood** — the form of a verb that gives orders: *Go. Stop. Listen.*

⚠️ **imperative ≠ imperious.** Same root, different target: **imperative** = the *thing* that must be done; **imperious** = a *person* who is arrogantly bossy. A safety check is imperative; the manager barking about it is imperious.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Imper**ative = an **emper**or's order — you don't negotiate with it.

The near-identical opening syllable is the real link, and it carries the nuance: this isn't a suggestion that happens to be important, it's a command with no reply.

### In sentences
1. It is **imperative** that the vaccine be kept below four degrees at every stage of transit.
2. Look, it's not a nice-to-have — getting the deposit in by Friday is **imperative**.

### In the wild
Every language class you've ever sat in: the **imperative mood** is the command form — *Sit. Wait. Don't touch that.* The grammar term is the meaning.

### Where it comes from
Latin **imperāre** = *"to command,"* which gives **imperium** (command, dominion) — and from there a whole family: **emperor**, **empire**, **imperial**, **imperious**, **imperative**. Learn the root once and five words come with it.

# **impulsive** *(adj.)* — **im-PUL-siv**

### Means
Acting on a sudden urge, with no gap between wanting and doing — no weighing, no plan, no second thought.

Notice how mild the disapproval is. *Reckless* means you knew the risk and went anyway; *impulsive* means the thought never got as far as risk. It describes a **temperament** more than a single act, and it can even be affectionate — the impulsive friend who books a flight at midnight. On the GRE it usually sits on the negative side, but it's the *unreflective* end of negative, not the *malicious* end.

⚠️ **impulsive ≠ compulsive.**
- **impulsive** = acts on **one** sudden urge, unrepeated and unplanned
- **compulsive** = driven to repeat the **same** act, unable to stop

An impulsive gambler bets once on a whim; a compulsive gambler cannot leave the table.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Hiding inside it is *pulse* — and your pulse never asks permission before it beats.**

The impulsive person acts the way a heartbeat happens: from the inside, instantly, with the thinking part never consulted.

### In sentences
1. The court treated the crime as **impulsive** rather than premeditated, and sentenced him accordingly.
2. Don't let her near a sale — she's so **impulsive** she'll come home with a kayak.

### In the wild
Retailers put chocolate and batteries by the till precisely to trigger the **impulse buy** — an entire industry built on the assumption that you won't stop to think.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pellere / pulsus** = *"to drive, to push."* An *impulse* is a push **in**to you from within.

That root pays off across the exam: **compel** (drive together, force), **propel** (drive forward), **expel** (drive out), **repel** (drive back), **dispel** (drive away), plus **pulse** and **repulsive**. Spot *-pel-* or *-puls-* and you're looking at a push.

# **interminable** *(adj.)* — **in-TER-mi-nuh-buhl**

*Not "inter-MINE-able" — the stress lands early, on TER.*

### Means
Endless — or rather, **so long that it feels endless**.

This is a word of complaint, not measurement. Nothing is literally interminable; calling a speech interminable tells you about the listener's suffering, not the clock. *Lengthy* is neutral, *protracted* is faintly clinical, but **interminable** is exasperated — the speaker has been trapped and wants you to know it. It attaches to things you must sit through: meetings, sermons, delays, queues, someone's story about their car.

⚠️ **interminable ≠ indeterminable.**
- **interminable** = has no *end* (endless)
- **indeterminable** = can't be *determined* (unknowable, unmeasurable)

An interminable wait is agony; a wait of indeterminable length is merely a mystery.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in-TERMIN-able** — it will not **terminate**, and you desperately want it to.

The buried verb *terminate* gives you the meaning, and the fact that you're wishing for it gives you the tone of complaint.

### In sentences
1. The trial dragged through an **interminable** third week of expert testimony, each witness contradicting the last.
2. Her uncle told an **interminable** story about a fishing trip, and nobody could work out how to escape.

### In the wild
**The Terminator** is the thing that ends you — same root. An interminable meeting is the one nobody can terminate.

### Where it comes from
Latin **terminus** = *"boundary, end"*, plus **in-** = *"not."* Literally *unable to be ended*.

That root is everywhere: **terminate**, **terminal** (the end of the line — airport, illness, or cable), **terminus**, **exterminate**, and **determine** (to set the limits of a question, i.e. settle it).

# **intuitive** *(adj.)* — **in-TOO-i-tiv**

### Means
Two linked senses. **Of a person or judgement:** arrived at by immediate insight, without conscious reasoning — you knew before you could say why. **Of a thing (an app, a tool, a layout):** so well made that you understand it without being taught.

Applied to objects it is straightforward praise — the highest compliment a product review can pay. Applied to a *person's* reasoning it cuts both ways: it credits the speed of the insight while quietly noting the absence of proof. Compare the neighbours — **instinctive** is biological and involuntary (blinking, flinching), **impulsive** is a failure of self-control, while **intuitive** is genuine understanding that simply skipped the working-out.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Intuitive** is what you need *no* **tuition** for.

The two words share a root, so let the contrast fix the meaning: if it has to be taught, it wasn't intuitive.

### In sentences
1. Her diagnosis was **intuitive** rather than deductive — she named the illness on sight, then spent an hour assembling the evidence for it.
2. The new remote is so **intuitive** my grandmother had it working before I'd found the manual.

### In the wild
It's the single most overused word in product reviews and app-store blurbs — "a clean, **intuitive** interface." Any time software is praised for needing no instructions, this is the word doing the work.

### Where it comes from
Latin **intuērī** = *"to look upon, gaze at"* — *in-* (upon) + **tuērī** (to watch, guard). Intuition is knowledge you get by *looking straight at* something, with no steps in between.

That root **tuērī** quietly runs a whole family of watching-over words: **tutor** (one who watches over you), **tuition**, **tutelage**, **tutorial**.

# **lassitude** *(n.)* — **LAS-i-tood**

*Also LAS-i-tyood in British English.*

### Means
A state of **weariness and listlessness** — no physical energy, and no will to summon any.

Tiredness with a flat battery is *fatigue*; lassitude is tiredness that has **soaked into the mood**. It's heavy, passive, faintly melancholy — the languor of a sickbed, a heatwave, a long convalescence, an empire in decline. Crucially it isn't laziness: laziness is a character flaw you're blamed for, lassitude is a condition that happens *to* you. The register is formal and literary; nobody says it about a Monday morning without irony.

⚠️ **lassitude ≠ latitude.** One letter apart in the middle: **lassitude** = exhausted listlessness; **latitude** = room to manoeuvre (or a line on a map). Being *given latitude* is freedom; being *seized by lassitude* is collapse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-itude** names a *state you live in* — sol**itude**, servi**itude**, grat**itude**. Lassitude is tiredness you're living in, not tiredness you're feeling.

Fatigue passes after a nap; lassitude is the thing you wake up still inside.

### In sentences
1. The heat pressed down until a general **lassitude** settled over the whole household, and even conversation seemed like too much effort.
2. Fan's broken, it's thirty-five degrees, and nobody in this office has moved in an hour — total **lassitude**.

### In the wild
Read the side-effects leaflet folded inside almost any box of medication: **"drowsiness, lassitude, nausea"** is standard drug-labelling language — the pharmaceutical industry's word for *this will flatten you*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **lassus** = *"weary, faint."*

The sneaky payoff: **alas** is the same root — Old French *ha las*, literally *"ah, weary!"* So every time someone sighs *alas*, they're announcing their lassitude.

# **omit** *(v.)* — **oh-MIT**

*Many speakers mumble the first syllable to uh-MIT; both are standard.*

### Means
To **leave something out** — to not include it — or to **fail to do** something you were supposed to do.

*Delete* removes what was there; **omit** means it never made it in. That's why an omission is so often a *fault of absence*: nobody sees the gap unless they know what should have filled it. And something can only be omitted if it had a claim to be there — you can't omit an irrelevance. The word carries a faint whiff of accountability (an accountant who *omits* an asset, a witness who *omits* a detail) without ever quite accusing anyone of lying.

⚠️ **omit ≠ emit.** One letter apart, same Latin root, opposite directions: **omit** = leave *out*; **emit** = send *out* (a chimney emits smoke).

### Trick to lock it in
> **oh-MIT** = *"oh, missed it"* — the thing that should have been there isn't.

The pronunciation is literally the reaction to an omission: you notice the gap only afterwards.

### In sentences
1. The report was accurate in every particular but **omitted** the one figure that would have embarrassed the board.
2. I **omitted** the part where I cried in the car, obviously.

### In the wild
Strunk & White's most quoted rule of writing: **"Omit needless words."**

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob-** (*away*) + **mittere** (*to send*) — "to send away, let go."

**mittere** is one of the highest-yield GRE roots: *mission, missile, transmit, emit, remit, submit, admit, permit, commit, intermittent, remiss*. Once you hear "send" inside a word, the rest falls out.

# **ornate** *(adj.)* — **or-NAYT**

### Means
Elaborately decorated — covered in carving, gilding, curls, flourishes.

The word tilts. Applied to a Baroque altarpiece it can be admiring; applied to *anything modern*, and to writing above all, it's a polite complaint — **ornate prose** is prose smothered in adjectives and subclauses, where a plain sentence would have done the job. Compare **elaborate** (complex, no verdict) and **opulent** (expensive, not necessarily fussy): ornate is specifically about surface decoration, and it always hints that someone couldn't stop adding.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ornate** = **ornament** + **-ate**: it's been *ornament-ed* — and one curl past enough.

The **-ate** ending marks something done *to* the object, so an ornate thing is never plain-born — it was decorated, and the word quietly asks whether it needed to be.

### In sentences
1. The chapel's **ornate** ceiling — every inch gilded, every rib carved — took four decades and three bankrupt patrons to finish.
2. His emails are so **ornate** that by the end of paragraph two you still don't know what he actually wants.

### In the wild
It's the default adjective of property listings and auction catalogues: *\"ornate cast-iron fireplace,\" \"ornate Victorian mirror.\"* Nobody ever writes *ornate* about a thing built after 1930.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ornāre** = *"to equip, to adorn"* → **ornātus**, "adorned." Straight to **ornament**, **adorn**, **ornamental**.

The useful bonus: **suborn** is the same root (*sub-* "secretly" + *ornāre* "equip") — to secretly *fit someone out* to lie for you, i.e. bribe a witness. A GRE word hiding inside a decorating verb.

# **paltry** *(adj.)* — **PAWL-tree**

*Also PAL-tree (rhyming with poultry) in American speech.*

### Means
Ridiculously small in amount or worth — so small it's insulting. Most often attached to money, wages, offers, turnout, percentages.

The word does two jobs at once: it measures *and* it sneers. **Meagre** is pitiable, **trifling** is merely unimportant, **modest** is polite — but calling a sum **paltry** says the giver should be ashamed. There's an implied comparison behind it: paltry *next to what was owed, promised, or expected*. That's why "a paltry £5" works and "a paltry mountain" doesn't.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Paltry** sounds like **poultry** — a paltry sum is **chicken feed**.

"Chicken feed" is already English's phrase for an amount too small to be worth accepting, and that dismissive shrug is exactly what *paltry* adds on top of "small".

### In sentences
1. After eighteen months of litigation the family was awarded a **paltry** sum, barely covering their legal costs.
2. They want a full rewrite by Friday and they're offering a **paltry** two hundred quid for it.

### In the wild
Business journalism runs on it: *"shares rose a paltry 0.3%"*, *"a paltry 12% turnout"*. The number alone is neutral — *paltry* is the reporter telling you it's an embarrassment.

# **pejorative** *(adj. / n.)* — **puh-JOR-uh-tiv**

*Also heard as PEJ-uh-ray-tiv (older, more common in Britain).*

### Means
**Adjective:** expressing contempt or disapproval; belittling.
**Noun:** a word that does the belittling.

This is a label you put on a **word**, not on a person — you say *"'bureaucrat' is a pejorative"*, not *"he was pejorative to me."* That gives it a cool, almost clinical register: the person saying "pejorative" is calm; the word being described is not. It's also wider and weaker than *slur* — a pejorative can be a mere sneer buried in a suffix (*poetaster*, *starlet*) rather than an outright attack.

⚠️ **pejorative ≠ prerogative.** A **prerogative** is an exclusive right you hold (*"that's the boss's prerogative"*). Similar rhythm, opposite worlds — one insults, one entitles.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Deliberately mis-say it — pe-JEER-ative. A pejorative jeers.**

The jeer is baked into the word itself, which is why you label the *term* pejorative rather than the speaker.

### In sentences
1. Once neutral, the word drifted into **pejorative** use and now appears in the style guide's list of terms to avoid.
2. He called my playlist "eclectic," but the tone was pure **pejorative** — he meant it was a mess.

### In the wild
Wikipedia boilerplate: scroll to almost any article on a nickname or slang term and you'll hit the sentence *"the term is often used as a pejorative."* It's the internet's standard way of saying *this word carries a sneer.*

### Where it comes from
Latin **peior** = *"worse"* → **pejorare**, *"to make worse."* A pejorative word makes its target worse than it is.

Same root, disguised: **impair** (via Latin *impejorare*) literally means *to make worse* — which is exactly what impairing something does.

# **prefigure** *(v.)* — **pree-FIG-yer**

*Also heard with front stress: PREE-fig-yer.*

### Means
To **show the shape of something before it exists** — to be an early version, model, or hint of what later comes to pass.

Foreshadow lives inside stories and leans ominous: a stray remark that hints at the murder. *Prefigure* is cooler and more structural — the earlier thing simply **has the form** of the later one, and typically has no idea it's doing so. There's no prophet and no omen; the resemblance is spotted afterwards, by an observer, which is why the word belongs to critics, historians and theologians rather than fortune-tellers.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pre-FIGURE** — the *figure*, the shape, was already drawn; nobody predicted it, we just recognise the outline later.

The hidden word *figure* keeps you on "form, not forecast" — prefiguring is resemblance noticed in hindsight, not prediction made in advance.

### In sentences
1. The 1919 general strike **prefigured** the labour upheavals that would convulse Europe a decade later.
2. Burnt toast, dead battery, lost keys — Tuesday morning pretty much **prefigured** the whole week.

### In the wild
Music criticism runs on this word: some obscure 1977 record is forever said to have **prefigured** punk, or grunge, or synthpop, by a decade — meaning nobody involved was predicting anything, the sound was just already there.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae-** *(before)* + **figura** *(shape, form)*, from **fingere** *(to shape, to mould)*.

That root is worth banking: **figure, configure, disfigure, effigy** (a moulded likeness), **figment** and **fiction** (things shaped out of nothing), **feign** (to shape a false front).

# **presage** *(v. / n.)* — **PRESS-ij**

*The verb is also said pri-SAYJ; the noun is always PRESS-ij.*

### Means
**Verb:** to be a sign or warning of something that is coming. **Noun:** the omen itself, or the feeling of foreboding it gives you.

The subject is usually a *thing*, not a person — falling share prices, a cough, a red sky. A forecaster **predicts**; the storm clouds **presage**. And the thing coming is nearly always bad: you can presage disaster, collapse, or war, but "presaged a lovely afternoon" reads as a joke. Weightier and gloomier than *foreshadow*, which is what a novelist does on purpose; presage is what the world does to you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pre-sage** — the **sage** knew *before* it happened, and he looked worried.

The word literally has *sage* sitting inside it, and the worried face is the reminder that what's foreseen is bad news.

### In sentences
1. The tremors of that spring **presaged** the collapse of the whole banking order.
2. That weird grinding noise from the engine **presaged** a very expensive Tuesday.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae-** (*before*) + **sagīre** (*to perceive keenly, to sense*). A Roman **praesāgium** was a foreboding — a thing sensed before it arrived.

That second root is the payoff: *sagīre* also gives you **sagacious** (keen-scented, shrewd) and **sage** (one who perceives keenly). Learn one, get three.

# **prescribe** *(v.)* — **pri-SKRYBE**

### Means
To **lay something down as a rule or order** — with the authority to make it binding. Medically: to order a specific treatment for a patient.

The word assumes **standing**. You *recommend* if you're a friend, you *prescribe* if you're a doctor, a statute, a style manual. It's not aggressive the way *dictate* is — it's calm, official, and not up for discussion. Watch for the GRE's favourite non-medical use: **prescriptive** rules tell you how you *ought* to write or speak, as opposed to **descriptive** ones, which just report how people actually do.

**prescribe ≠ proscribe.** Near-identical on the page, near-opposite in force:
- **prescribe** = order it *done* ("the doctor prescribed rest")
- **proscribe** = forbid it outright ("the regime proscribed the party")

One letter flips a command into a ban.

### Trick to lock it in
> **PRE**scribe writes it **in**; **PRO**scribe writes it **off**.

The *pre-* word puts an instruction on the page for you to follow; the *pro-* word puts a name on a list to be got rid of.

### In sentences
1. The charter **prescribes** the precise order in which the successors must be consulted.
2. She **prescribed** me two weeks off and said she'd know if I was still checking email.

### In the wild
The entire legal category of **prescription drugs** vs over-the-counter rests on this word: someone with authority has to write the order down before the pharmacy is allowed to hand it over.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae** (*before*) + **scrībere** (*to write*) — literally "written down in advance." That root pays for itself: **describe, inscribe, subscribe, transcribe, script, scripture, conscription**.

And it explains the trap. **Proscribe** is *pro-* (*publicly, out front*) + *scrībere*: in Rome, a **proscription** was a list of names posted in the Forum — men declared outlaws, to be hunted and killed. Written up in public = condemned.

# **prevail** *(v.)* — **pri-VAYL**

### Means
Three linked senses:

1. **To win out** — to prove stronger and come out on top after a struggle (*justice prevailed*).
2. **To be widespread or currently dominant** — usually as **prevailing** (*the prevailing wind, the prevailing view*).
3. **prevail upon/on someone** — to persuade them, by pressing until they give way.

Nothing prevails *quickly*. You **win** a race in ten seconds; you **prevail** in a war, a lawsuit, an argument that has gone on too long — the word carries the weight of resistance overcome, not just a result. Sense 2 falls straight out of sense 1: whatever won out is now the thing in force everywhere. And note it's intransitive with **over** or **against**, never "prevail someone."

⚠️ **prevail ≠ prevaricate.** *Prevail* = win out. *Prevaricate* (pri-VAR-i-kayt) = dodge the question, speak evasively. Identical first four letters, opposite postures — one stands firm, one squirms.

### Trick to lock it in
> **You *win* a race; you *prevail* in a war — and whatever prevailed becomes the *prevailing* wind.**

Prevailing always implies a contest of strength or endurance, which is exactly why the participle came to mean "dominant, in force everywhere."

### In sentences
1. After four years of litigation the smaller firm **prevailed**, and the patent was struck down.
2. Everyone wanted to keep arguing, but common sense **prevailed** and we went home.

### In the wild
Faulkner's 1950 Nobel Prize speech, one of the most quoted lines in American letters: *"I believe that man will not merely endure: he will prevail."* The contrast is the definition — enduring is surviving; prevailing is coming out on top.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae-** (*before, ahead*) + **valēre** (*to be strong, be worth*) — literally *to be stronger than*.

*Valēre* is a workhorse root: **valid** (strong enough to hold), **value**, **valour**, **invalid**, **equivalent**, **convalesce** (to grow strong again), and **prevalent** — prevail's own adjective.

# **propitiate** *(v.)* — **pruh-PISH-ee-ayt**

*The middle is "pish", not "pit" — noun form: pruh-pish-ee-AY-shuhn.*

### Means
To win back the favour of someone offended — to appease a power that is angry with you, usually by giving something up.

Where *placate* and *mollify* just soothe a mood, **propitiate** assumes a hierarchy: gods, kings, inspectors, your furious head of department. You are the smaller party, and you pay — an offering, a concession, a sacrifice. It also carries a faint ritual smell, so using it of an ordinary person quietly mocks them as a petty deity demanding tribute.

⚠️ **propitiate ≠ expiate.**
- **propitiate** = appease the offended **party**
- **expiate** = atone for the offending **act**

You expiate your sin; you propitiate the god who saw it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pro-pity** — you work *for* the pity of someone who outranks you.

The hidden "pity" carries the whole nuance: you're the one begging upward, and it costs you something.

### In sentences
1. The villagers **propitiated** the mountain with an offering of grain before any climb began.
2. He brought her coffee and a muffin, trying to **propitiate** her after forgetting the meeting entirely.

### In the wild
**Propitiation** is a load-bearing word in Christian theology — the blood sacrifice that turns away divine wrath (Romans 3:25). That's where the word's built-in sense of *paying to calm an angry power* comes from.

### Where it comes from
Latin **propitius** = *"favourable, gracious"* — originally an augur's word for birds flying the right way. To **propitiate** is literally *to make favourable*.

Same root gives you **propitious** = *favourable, well-omened* ("a propitious moment to ask"). Learn the pair together: you **propitiate** the god so the omens turn **propitious**.

# **recapitulate** *(v.)* — **ree-kuh-PICH-uh-layt**

*Also heard as ree-kuh-PIT-yoo-layt in British speech.*

### Means
To **restate the main points** of something in condensed, ordered form — a summary that goes back over the ground already covered.

Not mere repetition: to recapitulate is to *compress and re-order*, hitting the headline points and dropping the detail. It sits at the end of things — a closing argument, a lecture's final slide, the last paragraph of a report. Faintly formal; a colleague says "let me recap," a professor says "to recapitulate."

Second sense worth knowing: to **repeat the stages of** an earlier development — as in the old biology slogan *ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny*, the embryo replaying evolutionary history.

⚠️ **recapitulate ≠ capitulate.** *Capitulate* = **surrender**. The *re-* does not mean "surrender again" — the two words split long ago and share only a root.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Recap** is just this word with the middle knocked out — and the joke is that a word meaning *"keep it short"* is five syllables long.

Same meaning, opposite register: you *recap* in a meeting and *recapitulate* in a thesis.

### In sentences
1. To **recapitulate**: the funding lapsed in March, the team dispersed in April, and no one told the board until June.
2. Before you ask another question, can someone **recapitulate** what we actually decided?

### In the wild
The **"Previously on…"** montage at the start of a TV episode is a recap — literally this word, clipped short, doing exactly what it says.

### Where it comes from
Latin **caput, capitis** = *"head"* → **capitulum**, *"little head"* = the **heading of a chapter**. To *recapitulate* was to run back through the chapter headings.

That root is a goldmine: **capital, captain, chapter, decapitate, per capita, capitulate** (to draw up terms of surrender under headings), and **cap** itself.
