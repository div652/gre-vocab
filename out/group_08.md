# **accentuate** *(v.)* — **ak-SEN-choo-ayt**

### Means
To **make something already present stand out more** — to heighten, sharpen, throw into relief.

The key limit: you can only accentuate what's **already there**. A belt accentuates a waist that exists; a recession accentuates inequality that existed. Compare *emphasise*, which is about what a speaker chooses to stress, and *exaggerate*, which inflates past the truth — accentuate stays honest, it just turns the contrast up. It works on features (a cheekbone, a curve), on effects (a flaw, a divide, a colour), and it cuts both ways: what gets accentuated can be a virtue or a defect.

⚠️ **accentuate ≠ attenuate.** Two syllables apart, exact opposites: **accentuate** turns it *up*; **attenuate** thins it *down* (an attenuated signal, an attenuated virus).

### Trick to lock it in
> **accent**uate = put an **accent** on it. The accent mark adds no letter — it just makes one syllable louder.

That's the whole nuance: nothing new is created, something existing is just made to stand out.

### In sentences
1. The low evening light **accentuated** every crack in the plaster.
2. That jacket really **accentuates** your shoulders — wear it.

### In the wild
The 1944 standard **"Ac-Cent-Tchu-Ate the Positive"** — *"You've got to accentuate the positive, eliminate the negative"* — still the phrase most people have heard the word in.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **cantus** (*song*) → *accentus*, literally "song added to speech" — the rise and fall that marks a stressed syllable. The Romans heard stress as **singing**.

That *cantare* root is a GRE goldmine: **chant**, **enchant** (sing a spell onto someone), **incantation**, **recant** (sing it back), **cantata**, **descant**.

# **conjectural** *(adj.)* — **kuhn-JEK-chuh-ruhl**

*The *-tur-* is "chuh", as in *nature* — not "tyoor".*

### Means
Based on **guesswork or inference rather than evidence** — plausible, but unproven.

The word doesn't say *wrong*; it says *unsupported*. Where "speculative" hints at wishful reaching and "baseless" is an insult, **conjectural** is the cool academic label a historian or scientist attaches to a reconstruction they can't document: reasonable, honestly flagged, still air. Call someone's argument conjectural and you haven't refuted it — you've quietly demoted it from finding to hunch.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Con-JECT-ural — a guess in a tweed JACKet.**

The formal dress is the whole point: it's still a guess, just one wearing scholarly clothes.

### In sentences
1. The dating of the manuscript remains **conjectural**; no colophon survives, and the scribe's hand matches nothing else in the archive.
2. Everything after "he probably left around midnight" is **conjectural** — you're making it up as you go.

### In the wild
**Goldbach's Conjecture**, **the Poincaré Conjecture** — in mathematics a *conjecture* is precisely a statement everyone believes and nobody has proved. Goldbach's has stood unproven since 1742.

### Where it comes from
Latin **conicere** = *"to throw together"* (**com-** *together* + **jacere** *to throw*) — you fling scraps of evidence into a pile and see what shape they make.

**jacere** is a GRE workhorse: **project** (throw forward), **eject**, **inject**, **reject** (throw back), **trajectory**, **dejected** (thrown down), **adjacent** (thrown near).

# **convivial** *(adj.)* — **kuhn-VIV-ee-uhl**

### Means
**Cheerful, sociable and warm** — specifically in the way of people gathered around food and drink.

*Friendly* describes a disposition; **convivial** describes an occasion. It carries the table with it: a shared meal, a loosened tie, wine going round, everybody talking at once. You can be friendly to a stranger in a lift, but you cannot be convivial alone — the word needs company, and usually needs a host. It's a compliment with a faint glow of indulgence: a convivial evening was long, and nobody was in a hurry to leave.

⚠️ **convivial ≠ congenial.**
- **congenial** = *agreeable, well-suited to you* — a congenial colleague, a congenial climate
- **convivial** = *festive, good company* — a convivial dinner

A congenial person suits your temperament; a convivial person pours you a drink.

### Trick to lock it in
> **con + VIV** = *living it up, together* — same **viv** as *vivid*, *revive*, *survive*.

The **viv** supplies the life and the **con** supplies the crowd, which is why a solitary cheerful person is genial but never convivial.

### In sentences
1. The negotiations, frosty all week, ended in a surprisingly **convivial** dinner at which both delegations sang.
2. He's a nightmare in meetings but properly **convivial** the moment someone lights a barbecue.

### In the wild
Restaurant and hotel reviews live on this word — "a **convivial** atmosphere," "**convivial** shared plates." It's reviewer-speak for *loud, warm, and you'll stay too long*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **convivium** = *"a banquet"* — literally **con-** (*together*) + **vivere** (*to live*). A *convivium* was a dinner party, and the guests were *convivae*, "those who live together."

**vivere** is a workhorse root: **vivid**, **vivacious**, **revive**, **survive**, **viable**, **vivisection** — and **bon vivant**, the person who lives well and dines better.

# **decadent** *(adj.)* — **DEK-uh-duhnt**

### Means
Marked by **luxury and self-indulgence that signals moral or cultural decline** — an empire in its late, pleasure-soaked years; art that has gone soft and ornamental; a person who lives for comfort.

The word carries an accusation inside the compliment. *Luxurious* praises the thing; *decadent* praises it while implying something is rotting underneath — that the pleasure is what people do **instead of** building, fighting, or believing.

That charge has been mostly drained out by advertising, where "decadent" now just means *very rich and slightly naughty* (chocolate, spa days). On the GRE, expect the older, harsher sense: **declining, degenerate, past its peak**.

⚠️ **decadent ≠ decedent** *(dih-SEE-duhnt)*. A **decedent** is a dead person in legal language — "the decedent's estate." One is a rich dessert; the other is a corpse in a will.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Deca**dent desserts are **deca**y you can taste.

Both words start from the same falling-apart idea, so even the menu version keeps a whiff of rot — pleasure on the way down.

### In sentences
1. Gibbon portrays a **decadent** Rome, its citizens numbed by spectacle while the frontiers quietly gave way.
2. Three desserts and a nap at 2pm — a **decadent** Sunday, and I regret nothing.

### In the wild
Dessert menus. "Decadent chocolate torte" is the one place marketers cheerfully advertise moral collapse — proof of how far the word has been softened.

### Where it comes from
Latin **cadere** = *"to fall."* *De- + cadere* = to fall away, fall off.

That root falls all through English: **cascade** (falling water), **decay**, **deciduous** (trees whose leaves fall), **cadence** (the falling of the voice), **accident** and **coincidence** (things that befall you), **occident** (the West, where the sun falls).

# **egregious** *(adj.)* — **ih-GREE-juhs**

*The ending is one syllable — "juhs," not "jee-uhs."*

### Means
Outstandingly bad — bad in a way that **stands out**, conspicuous enough that people notice and name it.

Not a synonym for "very bad." *Egregious* is a verdict handed down: judges, editors, referees and inspectors reach for it, and it lands on the **offence**, not the person — an egregious error, an egregious lie, an egregious breach. It implies the fault was so plain that missing it was itself a failure. Say it about a small private mistake and you sound absurd.

Historical twist worth knowing: it once meant outstandingly **good**, and flipped to sarcastic use around the 1500s. That reversal is why the root looks so oddly complimentary.

### Trick to lock it in
> Sounds like **"a grievous"** — and an egregious fault is a grievous one that *everybody can see*.

The word never covers small quiet faults — only ones bad enough to point at, which is why it pairs almost automatically with *error*, *lie*, *foul*, *violation*.

### In sentences
1. The auditors described the omission as an **egregious** breach of fiduciary duty, and the board resigned within a week.
2. Forgetting the client's name in the pitch meeting was bad; calling him by his rival's name was **egregious**.

### In the wild
It's the standard escalation word in law and sport: courts speak of **"egregious misconduct"** as a threshold that triggers real sanctions, and a commentator saying **"that's an egregious foul"** means the card is coming out.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ē-** (*out of*) + **grex, gregis** (*flock, herd*) — literally *standing out from the herd*. Originally a compliment: distinguished, head and shoulders above the crowd. Sarcasm dragged it downhill until only the bad kind of standing out survived.

The flock unlocks a whole set: **gregarious** (happy in the herd), **congregate** (flock together), **segregate** (flock apart), **aggregate** (herded into one mass).

# **evanescent** *(adj.)* — **ev-uh-NES-uhnt**

### Means
Fading out of existence almost as soon as it appears — mist, a scent, a chord, a mood.

Where *ephemeral* is about **lifespan** (a mayfly, a fad — it lasts a day and then it's over), *evanescent* is about **the vanishing itself**: the thing is thinning, dissolving, already half-gone while you're still looking at it. It's the softest word in this family — no tragedy, no waste, just delicate disappearance — and it's almost always used of something lovely and insubstantial rather than something merely short.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-escent** means *in the process of becoming* — adol**escent**, conval**escent**, lumin**escent**. **Evan-escent** = in the process of **vanishing**.

The suffix supplies the key idea that this isn't gone yet — it's going, right now, in front of you.

### In sentences
1. The **evanescent** glow on the water lasted the length of a held breath, then the cloud closed over the sun.
2. Her enthusiasm for the gym proved **evanescent** — three weeks, tops.

### In the wild
The band **Evanescence** — the noun form, straight up. "Bring Me to Life" is one long fade-into-nothing, and the name was chosen for exactly that meaning.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** ("out") + **vanescere** ("to vanish"), from **vanus** = *"empty."*

That root gives you **vanish**, **vain** (empty of substance), **vanity**, **evanescent** — all built on the idea of nothing being there.

# **flamboyant** *(adj.)* — **flam-BOY-uhnt**

### Means
Showy, colourful, theatrical — dressing, speaking, or behaving in a way designed to be noticed.

Tone is what to watch. *Ostentatious* sneers at vulgar wealth; *garish* means it hurts to look at; **flamboyant** is usually half-admiring — the person has flair and knows it, and the display is more fun than offensive. Applied to people it points at style and personality (a flamboyant conductor, a flamboyant winger); applied to things it means bold colour and swagger (a flamboyant waistcoat, a flamboyant signature). It only turns critical when the context makes the showiness inappropriate — a flamboyant eulogy.

### Trick to lock it in
> **flam-BOY-ant = FLAME + buoyant** — it burns bright *and* floats up where everyone can see it.

The flame is the colour and heat of the display, and the buoyancy is the lightness that keeps it charming rather than crass.

### In sentences
1. His **flamboyant** courtroom manner — the pauses, the sweeping gestures — won juries that the evidence alone might not have.
2. She turned up to a beach wedding in a **flamboyant** purple suit and completely got away with it.

### In the wild
Freddie Mercury is the word's default illustration — practically no profile of him is written without "flamboyant" in the first paragraph, and it's always meant as a compliment.

### Where it comes from
French **flamboyer** = *"to blaze, to flame"*, from Latin **flamma**. Late Gothic architecture is literally called **Flamboyant** for its flame-shaped window tracery.

Same fire: **flame, inflame, flambé, flammable** — and **flamingo**, the flame-coloured bird.

# **forestall** *(v.)* — **for-STAWL**

*Two syllables, not three — there's no "forest" in it.*

### Means
To **head something off by acting first** — to prevent an anticipated event by taking steps in advance.

The word carries **foreknowledge and timing**. *Prevent* just reports the outcome; *forestall* says you saw it coming and moved early enough to block it. It's also quieter than *preempt* — preempting is a seizure, striking first to take what the other side wanted; forestalling is defensive, a door bolted before the knock. What you forestall is almost always unwelcome: a crisis, a lawsuit, an objection, a rival's next move.

### Trick to lock it in
> **fore + stall** — you put a **stall** in its path **before** it arrives.

The stall only works if you set it up ahead of time, which is exactly the foreknowledge the word insists on.

### In sentences
1. To **forestall** any challenge to the succession, the regent had the rival claimants married off before the old king was buried.
2. I texted him first to **forestall** the whole "you never call me" speech.

### In the wild
Standard central-bank and headline language: rates are raised **to forestall inflation**, bailouts arranged **to forestall a run on the bank**. If you learn one collocation, learn *forestall a crisis*.

### Where it comes from
Old English **foresteall** = *an ambush* — literally a "standing (*steall*) in front of" someone on the road. Medieval English law used **forestalling** for buying up goods before they reached the market, cornering the supply before anyone else could bid.

**fore-** is a whole free family: *forecast, foresee, forewarn, foreboding* — all of them "before."

# **gainsay** *(v.)* — **GAYN-say**

*Also heard as gayn-SAY; both are accepted.*

### Means
To **deny, contradict, or dispute** something — to declare that a statement or claim is not true.

It is a stiff, bookish word, and it keeps strange company: in modern English it lives almost entirely inside a negative. You will read *"no one can gainsay her achievement"* or *"there is no gainsaying the numbers"* far more often than a plain *"he gainsaid me."* That habit carries the nuance — the word usually appears precisely to say that the thing is **beyond dispute**. Where *contradict* is a neutral everyday act, *gainsay* implies challenging the validity or truth of an assertion, often with a whiff of futility.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nobody ever *gainsays* — things are only **"beyond gainsaying."**

Lock the word to its negative frame and you get both the meaning (to say against) and the way it is actually used — to declare something undeniable.

### In sentences
1. Whatever one thinks of his methods, there is no **gainsaying** the scale of what he built.
2. Sure, the food's overpriced — but I'm not going to **gainsay** anyone who says it's the best in town.

### In the wild
The King James Bible, Luke 21:15: *"I will give you a mouth and wisdom, which all your adversaries shall not be able to **gainsay** nor resist."* That biblical weight is exactly why the word still sounds so formal.

### Where it comes from
Pure Old English, and transparent once split: **gēan** (*"against"*) + **say**. It's the same *gēan* buried in **again** and **against** — so *gainsay* is literally *say-against*.

🚫 Nothing to do with **gain** (profit). That's from French *gaaignier*; the resemblance is an accident of spelling.

# **galvanize** *(v.)* — **GAL-vuh-nyze**

*British spelling: galvanise.*

### Means
To **shock someone into sudden action** — to jolt a person, crowd, or movement out of apathy and into doing something.

The force is a *jolt*, not a coaxing. You *encourage* a team over weeks; you **galvanize** them in a moment, usually with a speech, a crisis, or an outrage. It also implies the target was **inert beforehand** — you can't galvanize people who were already busy. And it's warm: the galvanizer is admired, whereas an **incite**-r is blamed. Politics, activism and business love it — *galvanize support*, *galvanize the base*, *galvanized into action*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nobody is ever galvanized and then just sits there.** The word is almost always followed by the doing: *galvanized into action*, *galvanized the crowd to march*.

Galvanize never describes a mood — only a trigger, so if nothing visibly happens next, it isn't the right word.

### In sentences
1. The verdict **galvanized** a protest movement that had been dormant for a decade.
2. One furious email from the client **galvanized** the whole team — we shipped the fix by lunch.

### In the wild
**Galvanized** steel — buckets, nails, roofing sheets — is iron given a zinc coat by an electrical process named after the same man. The metal in your hardware store and the crowd in the speech share a root.

### Where it comes from
From **Luigi Galvani**, the 18th-century Italian anatomist who made a dead frog's leg kick by touching it with metal — proof that electricity moves flesh. That twitch *is* the word: something lifeless, a current, sudden movement.

Same man gives you **galvanic** and the **galvanometer**.

# **indiscriminate** *(adj.)* — **in-di-SKRIM-uh-nuht**

*The ending is "-nuht," not "-nayt" — the verb discriminate ends dis-KRIM-uh-nayt, the adjective doesn't.*

### Means
Making no distinctions — choosing at random, or not choosing at all; sweeping up the innocent with the guilty, the good with the junk.

The word carries blame. *Random* is neutral, even mathematical; **indiscriminate** implies someone who *should* have sorted and didn't. That failure is the whole accusation — an indiscriminate reader devours anything with pages, indiscriminate praise is worthless because it's handed to everyone, and indiscriminate shelling is a war crime precisely because it doesn't ask who's standing there. Strongest in contexts of harm, but usable lightly for anyone with no filter.

**indiscriminate ≠ indiscreet.** *Indiscriminate* = makes no distinctions (takes everything). *Indiscreet* = keeps no secrets (says everything). Both are lapses of judgement, but one is a bad filter on intake, the other a bad filter on output.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **discriminating** palate picks; an **indiscriminate** one eats whatever's on the table.

Strip the *in-* and you get the connoisseur who sorts carefully — so *indiscriminate* is the refusal, or inability, to sort at all.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal condemned the **indiscriminate** shelling of the district, which spared neither hospital nor school.
2. He's an **indiscriminate** swiper — right on everyone, then acts surprised when the dates go badly.

### In the wild
The standing phrase in war reporting and UN statements: **"indiscriminate attacks on civilians."** It's the technical charge — the weapon couldn't distinguish a soldier from a child, and was fired anyway.

### Where it comes from
Latin **cernere** = *"to sift, to separate"* — the grain from the chaff. Add *dis-* (apart) and you get **discernere**: to tell one thing from another.

That sieve runs through a whole family: **discern** (tell apart), **discrete** (separate, distinct), **secret** (*se-cernere*, sifted aside), **concern**, **certain**. So *indiscriminate* is literally **unsifted** — everything left in the pile together.

# **innocuous** *(adj.)* — **ih-NOK-yoo-uhs**

### Means
Harmless — incapable of causing injury or offence.

*Harmless* is plain description; **innocuous** almost always arrives with suspicion attached. You reach for it when harm was possible, expected, or later discovered: *an innocuous-looking package*, *an innocuous remark that started the whole fight*. It is the word for things that pass inspection.

Its second life is dismissive. Call a novel, a pop album, or a policy proposal **innocuous** and you mean it is so inoffensive it is boring — nothing in it could hurt you, and nothing in it could move you either.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in-NOC-uous — it can't *knock* you.**

The stressed middle syllable *sounds* like **knock**, and the *in-* cancels it: no knock, no damage — but note that you only say it about something that *could* have landed a blow.

### In sentences
1. The compound proved **innocuous** in every trial, though the committee kept testing it for another two years.
2. It was an **innocuous** question — I genuinely didn't know it would set him off.

### In the wild
Standard vocabulary in security briefings: phishing training warns you about the **innocuous-looking** attachment or the **innocuous-looking** link — harmless on the surface, which is the entire point.

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** (*not*) + **nocēre** (*to harm*) — literally "not harming."

*Nocēre* is worth banking: it gives **noxious** (harmful), **obnoxious** (originally *exposed to harm*, now the harm itself), **nuisance**, and **innocent** — someone who has done no harm.

# **momentary** *(adj.)* — **MOH-muhn-ter-ee**

### Means
Lasting only a moment — a few seconds, gone almost as soon as it arrives.

It is the *shortest* word in its family: **temporary** can run for months, **transient** for a season, **ephemeral** carries a whiff of beauty and loss. **Momentary** is plain and clinical about duration — and it usually does quiet defensive work, framing something as too brief to count against you: *a momentary lapse, a momentary hesitation, a momentary flash of anger*. Say a failure was momentary and you are asking for it to be waved off.

⚠️ **momentary ≠ momentous.**
- **momentary** = lasting a moment (*how long*)
- **momentous** = weighty, historic (*how much*)

A momentary pause before signing; a momentous decision to sign.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-ary** measures the clock; **-ous** measures the weight.

Momentary tells you a thing lasted one moment, momentous tells you it was *full of* moment — heavy, consequential.

### In sentences
1. The truce proved **momentary**; artillery resumed before the envoys had left the room.
2. There was a **momentary** panic when I couldn't find my passport, then I remembered it was in my jacket.

### In the wild
Pink Floyd's 1987 album ***A Momentary Lapse of Reason*** — and "momentary lapse" is the phrase the word lives in most naturally.

### Where it comes from
Latin **momentum** — originally the tiny weight that *tips* a balance scale (from *movēre*, "to move"). That one image split two ways: the instant it takes to tip → **moment**, **momentary**; the weight doing the tipping → **momentous**, **momentum**.

# **mundane** *(adj.)* — **mun-DAYN**

### Means
Ordinary, everyday, unremarkable — and by extension **dull because it is routine**. A second, older sense survives in formal writing: **of this world**, earthly rather than spiritual.

The dullness of *mundane* is the dullness of laundry, expense reports, and the school run. Compare its neighbours: **banal** sneers at something stale and unoriginal, **tedious** says it actively wears you out, **prosaic** says it lacks poetry. *Mundane* passes no such judgement — it simply places a thing at ground level, among the chores. Which is why it can be used with no contempt at all ("the mundane details of running a household") or with a quiet sigh, depending entirely on what it's set against.

### Trick to lock it in
> **MUN**dane starts like **Mon**day — the world's most routine, ground-level day.

Monday isn't offensive or exhausting, just relentlessly ordinary — exactly the flavour of dull that *mundane* carries.

### In sentences
1. After a decade of war reporting, he found the **mundane** rhythms of village life almost unbearable in their gentleness.
2. I had big plans for the weekend and spent it on **mundane** stuff — bins, laundry, a dentist form.

### In the wild
In fantasy fiction, ordinary non-magical humans get called **mundanes** — Cassandra Clare's *The Mortal Instruments* and the *Shadowhunters* series use it constantly. It's the word's original sense fossilised: people of *this* world, not the other one.

### Where it comes from
Latin **mundus** = *"the world."* So *mundane* first meant **worldly** — the earthly realm as opposed to the heavenly one. Once you're stuck down here with the taxes and the dishes, "worldly" slides naturally into "everyday," and "everyday" slides into "boring."

Same root in **extramundane** (beyond this world) and **demimonde** (French *monde*, "world" — the half-world of the socially disreputable).

# **nettlesome** *(adj.)* — **NET-uhl-suhm**

### Means
Irritating, vexing, causing persistent low-grade trouble.

Scale matters: a *nettlesome* problem is not a crisis, it's a nagging one — it keeps coming back, it stings, and it refuses to be solved cleanly. *Infuriating* is hot and loud; *nettlesome* is cool and bookish, the word a judge, an editorial writer, or a diplomat reaches for. It attaches most naturally to **problems, questions, issues, details** — the annoyances of the desk rather than of the heart — though a person who won't stop poking at you can be nettlesome too.

⚠️ **nettlesome ≠ mettlesome.** One letter apart, opposite feeling: **mettlesome** (from *mettle*, spirit) = **spirited, plucky, full of courage** — a compliment, often for a horse or a fighter. Nettlesome irritates you; mettlesome impresses you.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **nettle** doesn't wound you — it *stings*, over and over, and the **-some** means you get a whole lot of that.

The word encodes its own intensity: repeated small stings, not a single deep cut.

### In sentences
1. The treaty foundered on the **nettlesome** question of who would police the border.
2. Setting up the printer was fine — it's the **nettlesome** little driver updates every other week that get me.

### In the wild
Pure courtroom-and-op-ed vocabulary: American judges and political columnists write **"a nettlesome question"** constantly, precisely because it flags a problem as thorny and unresolved without calling it a disaster.

### Where it comes from
Straight from the **nettle**, the weed that stings your ankles — to *nettle* someone has meant "to irritate" since the 1500s.

The payoff is the suffix: **-some** = *"tending to produce."* Lock it once and you get **burdensome, troublesome, cumbersome, meddlesome, quarrelsome, loathsome, fulsome, noisome**.

# **nullify** *(v.)* — **NUL-uh-fye**

### Means
To make something **legally void** — to strip it of force so it counts as if it never held any. Also: to **cancel out completely**, so the net effect is zero.

Weaker words let something survive in reduced form: *undermine* leaves it standing but shaky, *reduce* leaves a remainder. **Nullify leaves nothing.** It carries the flavour of an authority striking a thing off the record — courts, referees, legislatures — or of two forces meeting and cancelling exactly. Note the arithmetic edge: a raise **nullified** by inflation isn't a smaller raise, it's no raise.

### Trick to lock it in
> **null + -ify** — not *weakened*, **zeroed**.

The word wears its meaning on its face: whatever gets nullified is set back to null, the way a form field goes blank rather than merely small.

### In sentences
1. The Supreme Court **nullified** the statute, holding that the legislature had never possessed the power to pass it.
2. Two weeks of dieting, **nullified** by one wedding buffet.

### In the wild
Football commentary lives on this word: *"the goal is nullified — the flag is up for offside."* Not a goal that counted less; a goal wiped off the scoreboard entirely.

### Where it comes from
Latin **nullus** = *"not any"* (from *ne* + *ullus*, "not even one") + **-ficāre**, *"to make"* — literally *make-it-none*.

Same root gives you **null**, **nullity**, **nil**, and — hiding behind a prefix — **annul** (*ad-* + *nullus*), which is exactly what a court does to a marriage: not end it, but declare it never legally existed.

# **obviate** *(v.)* — **OB-vee-ayt**

### Means
To **make something unnecessary** — to head off a difficulty so completely that it never has to be dealt with.

The key is *pre-emption, not repair*. You don't obviate a problem you're already in; a clever design obviates the **need** for a fix before anyone reaches for one. *Prevent* stops a thing from happening; *obviate* goes further and cancels the requirement itself — the meeting isn't rescheduled, it's called off. Dry, formal, and overwhelmingly used with an abstract object: *the need, the requirement, the difficulty, the objection*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Obviate** makes a need **obsolete** — same *ob-*, same result: no longer required.

It doesn't answer the question, it makes the question stop applying.

### In sentences
1. The new evidence **obviated** any need for a second trial; the charges were dropped outright.
2. If you just put the spare key under the mat, that **obviates** this whole conversation.

### In the wild
Live in engineering and patent prose as a fixed phrase: **\"obviates the need for\"** — a new sensor obviates the need for manual calibration. If you see *obviate*, the words *the need for* are almost certainly next.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob-** (*in the way of*) + **via** (*road*) — to plant yourself across the road so the trouble never arrives.

**via** is a workhorse root: **deviate** (off the road), **devious** (off-road, hence sneaky), **trivial** (*tri- + via*, the three-way crossroads where anyone might be standing — hence commonplace), **viaduct**, **previous**, **obvious**.

**obvious** is the same *ob + via* — but there it means lying in the road where you can't miss it. Same root, opposite jobs: *obvious* is what's in your way; *obviate* is what gets in **its** way.

# **omnipresent** *(adj.)* — **om-nee-PREZ-uhnt**

### Means
Present everywhere at once — or, loosely, present so constantly that you can't get away from it.

The word starts in theology: **omnipresence** is a property of God, alongside omnipotence and omniscience. That grandeur never fully washes off, which is why everyday use is usually half-joking or half-annoyed — an omnipresent advert, an omnipresent boss, an omnipresent summer song. *Ubiquitous* means found in many places; **omnipresent** means there the whole time, inescapably, watching. One is a distribution; the other is a presence.

### Trick to lock it in
> **God is omnipresent. So are the ads.**

The word only has two homes — the divine and the exasperated — and nobody has ever been pleased that something was omnipresent.

### In sentences
1. In the medieval imagination, an **omnipresent** deity made privacy theologically impossible: there was no room without a witness.
2. That chorus was **omnipresent** last summer — supermarket, taxi, gym, my own head at 3 a.m.

### Where it comes from
Latin **omnis** = *"all"* + **praesens** = *"present, at hand."*

*Omnis* is one of the best-value roots on the GRE: **omniscient** (all-knowing), **omnipotent** (all-powerful), **omnivore** (eats all), **omnibus** (*"for all"* — a bill bundling everything, and the horse-drawn vehicle for everyone, which we shortened to **bus**).

# **oust** *(v.)* — **OWST**

### Means
To **force someone out of a position** — an office, a throne, a job, a board seat — or out of possession of property.

What gets ousted is usually a *role*, not a room. The removal is involuntary and final, but the method is left open: a palace coup, a shareholder vote, and a quiet 3am memo all count. Compare the neighbours — *evict* is about property and paperwork, *expel* is about being thrown out of an institution, *depose* is reserved for rulers. *Oust* is the newsroom's all-purpose verb, and it lives in the passive: nobody ousts themselves, they **are ousted**. The noun is **ouster** (the act of removal, not the person doing it).

### Trick to lock it in
> **OUST = OUT + S(eat)** — you're out of your seat, and someone else is in it.

Ousting is always about a seat — of power, of office, of the board — so the person doesn't just leave, they are *replaced*.

### In sentences
1. The junta **ousted** the elected president before dawn, and the state broadcaster was reading the new decrees by breakfast.
2. Two bad quarters and the founder was **ousted** by his own board.

### In the wild
November 2023: every headline on the planet ran the same verb when OpenAI's board **ousted** Sam Altman — and then un-ousted him five days later.

### Where it comes from
Anglo-French **oster** ("to remove"), from Latin **obstāre** = *ob-* ("against") + **stāre** ("to stand"). To oust is to remove someone from where they *stand* — same *stāre* behind **obstacle**, **obstruct**, **station**, and **status**.

# **palpable** *(adj.)* — **PAL-puh-buhl**

### Means
Literally: **able to be touched or felt** with the hands. Almost always used figuratively: a feeling or atmosphere so intense it seems to have physical presence — **palpable tension, palpable relief, palpable dread**. A third sense survives in phrases like *a palpable lie*: so obvious it might as well be solid.

The word marks **intensity, not just visibility**. Something *obvious* is merely easy to see; something *palpable* presses on the room. That is why it attaches to collective moods — a crowd, a courtroom, a silence — rather than to one person's private mood.

⚠️ **palpable ≠ palatable.** *Palatable* (PAL-uh-tuh-buhl) is from *palate* — tasty, or acceptable enough to swallow: *a palatable compromise*. One is felt, the other is tasted.

### Trick to lock it in
> **PALP**able — you could lay your **palm** on it.

The word insists the thing is dense enough to touch, which is exactly why it outranks "obvious."

### In sentences
1. As the verdict was read, the tension in the courtroom was **palpable**.
2. He said he was fine, but the sulk coming off him was **palpable** from across the kitchen.

### In the wild
Live sports and news commentary run on it — "you can feel it, the tension in this stadium is **palpable**" is the reflex line every commentator reaches for before a penalty shootout.

### Where it comes from
Latin **palpāre** = *"to touch, to stroke."* Same root gives you **palpate** — what a doctor does pressing your abdomen — and **palpitate**, a heart beating hard enough to feel.

# **perfidy** *(n.)* — **PER-fi-dee**

*Adjective form: perfidious — per-FID-ee-uhs, stress shifts.*

### Means
Deliberate **breach of faith** — treachery by someone who was trusted, and who traded on that trust.

You cannot be perfidious to a stranger. The word requires a bond first — an ally, a spouse, a treaty, an oath — and it condemns the *violation of the bond*, not merely the lie. Next to it, *dishonesty* is petty and *deceit* is technique; perfidy is a moral verdict, delivered in a raised register. It belongs to statesmen, historians and courtroom rhetoric, and it carries real contempt: a lawyer says *perfidy* when *betrayal* isn't damning enough. In the laws of war it's a defined crime — feigning surrender in order to shoot.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Perfidy is fidelity with a *per-* bolted on** — the faith has to exist before it can be broken.

Spot the **fid** buried in the middle and you get both the meaning and the condition: no prior trust, no perfidy.

### In sentences
1. The treaty was signed in the morning and the border crossed by night — an act of **perfidy** the smaller nation never forgave.
2. He smiled, took the credit for her slides, and called it teamwork; the **perfidy** was breathtaking.

### In the wild
**"Perfidious Albion"** — the centuries-old French sneer at England, meaning *the island that signs treaties and breaks them*. Still trotted out in European newspapers whenever Britain reneges on something.

### Where it comes from
Latin **per-** (*through, to destruction*) + **fidēs** (*faith, trust*) — literally *faith wrecked*.

That root is a goldmine: **fidelity**, **infidel**, **confide**, **bona fide**, **affidavit**, **fiancé** (one pledged), and the Marines' **Semper Fi**. Even the classic dog's name **Fido** is Latin for *"I trust."*

# **profuse** *(adj.)* — **pruh-FYOOSS**

*Ends in a hiss — rhymes with loose, not lose.*

### Means
Given out or flowing in **very large amounts** — abundant to the point of overflow, even extravagance.

The word carries the sense of something **pouring out**, unstoppable and slightly beyond measure. *Plentiful* is a calm fact about quantity; **profuse** is liquid and uncontrolled. That's why the things it attaches to are things that stream: sweat, blood, tears, apologies, thanks, praise. Applied to a person's manner, it tips towards *too much* — profuse thanks are the kind that make the room uncomfortable.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Profuse things POUR** — sweat, blood, tears, apologies. Nobody has profuse chairs.

The word only fits what can stream out of something, so if it can't flow, it can't be profuse.

### In sentences
1. The manuscript's margins were covered in **profuse** annotations, each hand more cramped than the last.
2. He turned up an hour late with a bunch of flowers and **profuse** apologies, none of which explained anything.

### In the wild
You've met the adverb far more than the adjective: in every hospital drama and first-aid manual, someone is **bleeding profusely**, and in every awkward email, someone **apologises profusely**.

### Where it comes from
Latin **profundere** = *"to pour forth"* — **pro-** (forth) + **fundere** (to pour). The past participle *profusus* is literally *poured out*.

*Fundere* is a workhorse root: **fuse**, **infuse**, **transfusion**, **diffuse**, **effusive** (gushing with feeling), **refund** (pour money back), **confuse** (poured together into a mess).

🚫 **Not related to *profound*.** That one is *pro-* + **fundus**, "bottom" — depth, not flow.

# **pugnacious** *(adj.)* — **puhg-NAY-shuhs**

*The g is silent in the related verb impugn (im-PYOON), but fully sounded here.*

### Means
Eager to fight; combative, quarrelsome, always up for a scrap.

This is a **standing disposition, not a mood**. Someone *angry* has been provoked; someone **pugnacious** goes looking. Compare the neighbours: *belligerent* belongs to armies and drunks and implies real violence; *truculent* is sullen and menacing; **pugnacious** is usually verbal and often faintly admiring — the columnist, the trial lawyer, the small striker who won't be bullied. It carries energy and relish, so a pugnacious person is a nuisance you might also enjoy watching.

### Trick to lock it in
> **PUG**nacious — a pug is a squashed-faced little dog *and* old boxing slang for a fighter. Small, scrappy, spoiling for it.

The "pug" sitting at the front is no accident — boxers were called *pugs* from the same fist-root, and it pins the word's flavour of eager scrappiness rather than genuine menace.

### In sentences
1. His pugnacious style of cross-examination unsettled witnesses long before it ever produced a confession.
2. Three minutes into the meeting he was already picking fights — pugnacious little man, isn't he?

### Where it comes from
Latin **pugnus** = *"fist"* → **pugnāre**, *"to fight."*

One root, a useful cluster: **pugilist** (a boxer — a fist-fighter), **impugn** (to attack a claim *at* it, with words), **repugnant** (literally *fighting back* against you — hence repellent).

# **sagacious** *(adj.)* — **suh-GAY-shuhs**

### Means
Having keen judgment and **foresight** — able to read a situation, or a person, and see where it is heading.

This is wisdom with a practical edge. **Wise** suggests depth and moral weight; **shrewd** suggests self-interest and a sharp eye for advantage; **sagacious** sits between them — respectable, admiring, and always about *judgment applied to the future*. You call a decision sagacious when it looked unremarkable at the time and turned out to be exactly right. The word is formal and slightly grand, so it flatters: it's the adjective for elder statesmen, canny investors, and — a long literary tradition — unusually clever dogs.

**sagacious ≠ salacious.** *Salacious* (suh-LAY-shuhs) means lewd, indecently sexual — *salacious gossip*. One letter and one vowel apart, opposite worlds: praise a colleague's *salacious* judgment and you have said something very different.

### Trick to lock it in
> **SAGA**cious — he sees the whole **saga** before it's played out.

The hidden word carries the key nuance: this isn't just knowing things, it's seeing how the story ends.

### In sentences
1. The treaty was the work of a **sagacious** diplomat who had anticipated the alliance's collapse a decade before it came.
2. Gran's **sagacious** enough to know that when the two of them go quiet, something's been broken.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sagax** = *"keen-scented,"* from **sagīre**, *to perceive sharply* — originally the nose of a hunting hound picking up a trail nobody else can detect. That's the image inside the word: sensing what's there before there's evidence.

Same root gives **presage** (*prae-* "before" + *sagire*) — to sniff out what's coming. Note that **sage** is *not* a relative: that's Latin *sapere*, "to taste, to be wise" (**sapient, insipid, savour**).

# **sanguine** *(adj.)* — **SANG-gwin**

*Ends in "-gwin," not "-gwine" — it does not rhyme with wine.*

### Means
Cheerfully confident that things will turn out well — **especially when there's good reason to think they won't**.

Optimism is the general word; **sanguine** is optimism with the volume down. It describes a settled temperament rather than a mood, and it almost always shows up against a backdrop of trouble: you are sanguine *about* the deficit, the diagnosis, the deadline. That's what gives it its edge — depending on the writer, it's either admirable composure or a polite way of saying someone hasn't grasped the danger. Formal, and common in finance and journalism ("investors remain sanguine").

An older, near-dead sense means *blood-red* or *ruddy-complexioned*, which is the bridge back to the root.

⚠️ **sanguine ≠ sanguinary.** Same blood root, opposite temperature: **sanguine** = cheerful and unworried; **sanguinary** = bloodthirsty, gory. A sanguine general expects to win; a sanguinary one enjoys the killing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sanguine** starts with **sang** — while everyone else panics, he **sang**.

The calm cheerfulness is the whole point: sanguine isn't excitement, it's humming through a crisis.

### In sentences
1. Despite two failed harvests, the old man remained **sanguine** about the estate's prospects, as though ruin were merely a rumour.
2. Markets have gone haywire and he's still **sanguine** about his pension — I wish I had his nerves.

### In the wild
It's the standard word of market commentary: any week you can find a headline saying investors are "**sanguine** about inflation" — always meaning *calm so far, possibly foolishly*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sanguis, sanguinis** = *blood*. Medieval medicine held that four bodily humours set your personality; the man with blood dominant was ruddy, warm and cheerful — the **sanguine** type. The temperament outlived the theory.

Same root: **sanguinary** (bloodthirsty), **consanguineous** (related by blood), and the red wine punch **sangria**.

# **scant** *(adj. (also v.))* — **SKANT**

### Means
**Barely enough — and pointedly less than what was needed, expected, or deserved.**

*Scarce* describes supply: the thing is hard to come by. *Scant* describes a **shortfall against a standard** — there is some, just not enough to do the job. That's why it almost always sits directly in front of a noun and carries a note of reproach: *scant evidence*, *scant regard*, *scant attention*, *scant comfort*. Saying a report gave the objections *scant attention* is a criticism of the report, not a statement about how much attention exists in the world.

It also does quiet duty as a precise measure: *a scant cup* means fill it and then take a little back.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Scant** rhymes with **can't** — there's scant flour, so you *can't* finish the cake.

Scant isn't "none" and it isn't "little" — it's the amount that leaves you unable to do the thing.

### In sentences
1. The commission devoted three hundred pages to procedure and **scant** attention to the victims.
2. We had a **scant** ten minutes to make the connection, so we ran.

### In the wild
Baking recipes use it as a technical measure: **"a scant cup of sugar"** means fill the cup, then tip a little out — deliberately just under.

# **skullduggery** *(n.)* — **skul-DUG-uh-ree**

*Usually spelled skulduggery (one l) in dictionaries and British use; both are correct.*

### Means
Underhanded, sneaky behaviour — trickery, scheming, dishonest dealing done out of sight.

Tone does the work here. *Fraud* and *malfeasance* belong in an indictment; **skullduggery** belongs in a newspaper column, and it half-winks while it accuses. It suggests backroom deals, rigged committees and quiet double-crossing rather than violence or grand evil — serious enough to condemn, small and colourful enough to enjoy. Because it sounds faintly comic, it's also the word people reach for when they suspect wrongdoing but can't prove it.

### Trick to lock it in
> Skull-**DUG**-gery: somebody's been **digging** where they shouldn't — under the table, behind your back.

The buried "dug" gives you the secrecy, and the clownish bounce of the word gives you the register — shady scheming, not atrocity.

### In sentences
1. The inquiry uncovered a decade of quiet **skullduggery** in the awarding of state contracts.
2. There's no way he won that raffle three years running — that's **skullduggery** if I ever saw it.

### In the wild
Derek Landy's bestselling **Skulduggery Pleasant** series — a wisecracking skeleton detective. The title is a pun on *skull*, not the real origin, but it does lock the spelling in.

### Where it comes from
🚫 **Nothing to do with skulls.** No graverobbing, no bones. It's an altered form of Scots **sculduddery**, meaning obscenity or lewdness — origin otherwise unknown. English mangled the spelling toward *skull*, and the false image has stuck ever since.

# **trivial** *(adj.)* — **TRIV-ee-uhl**

### Means
So small, ordinary or inconsequential that it isn't worth attention or effort.

**Minor** concedes that a thing is small but real; **trivial** denies it any weight at all. It's a verdict rather than a measurement — call someone's objection trivial and you've dismissed it, not sized it. That sting is why "*this isn't trivial*" is a standard way of insisting something matters.

One neutral exception worth knowing: in maths, logic and programming, a **trivial case** or **trivial solution** just means the degenerate, obvious one (*x = 0*) — no insult, pure jargon.

### Trick to lock it in
> **TRIVIAL** = **TRIVIA** + al — pub-quiz facts: perfectly true, and nobody's life depends on them.

The hidden word carries the exact nuance — trivial things aren't false or tiny, they're simply not worth anyone's attention.

### In sentences
1. The court held the breach **trivial**, awarding damages of a single pound.
2. Stop sweating the font — that's **trivial**, we ship tomorrow.

### In the wild
**Trivial Pursuit** — the board game built entirely out of facts that are fun to know and useless to have. Same root as *trivia*, and the name is the definition.

### Where it comes from
Latin **trivium** = *tri-* ("three") + *via* ("road") — a fork where three roads met. That's the public street corner, so *trivialis* meant "the sort of thing you hear at a crossroads": common, vulgar, everyday chatter.

The **via** half is a workhorse: **deviate** (off the road), **devious**, **viaduct**, **convey**, and **obvious** — literally *ob viam*, "lying in the road" right in front of you.

# **utilitarian** *(adj. / n.)* — **yoo-til-i-TAIR-ee-uhn**

### Means
**Adj.:** made for use, not for looks — strictly functional, plain, no ornament. **Also:** relating to *utilitarianism*, the ethics of "greatest good for the greatest number." **Noun:** a person who holds that view.

Applied to objects and buildings, it's faint praise with a shrug inside it: the thing works, and that is genuinely all you can say for it. Not *ugly* — ugliness takes effort — just indifferent to beauty. Compare **spartan** (bare by discipline, almost proud of it) and **austere** (severe, morally weighted); *utilitarian* carries no principle at all, only usefulness. In the philosophical sense the word goes fully neutral and technical, so let the sentence tell you which one you're looking at.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Utilitarian** wears **utility** on its sleeve — and nobody has ever decorated a *utility room*.

The utility room holds the boiler and the mop: it exists to work, and no one asked it to be nice.

### In sentences
1. The post-war housing estates were relentlessly **utilitarian** — concrete, right angles, and not one gesture toward grace.
2. The kitchen's pretty **utilitarian**, but honestly? Everything's within reach and nothing's ever broken.

### In the wild
**Utilitarianism** — Bentham and Mill's "the greatest happiness of the greatest number" — is the same word doing philosophy: judge an action purely by how *useful* the outcome is, never by how noble it looks.

### Where it comes from
Latin **utilis** = *"useful,"* from **uti** = *"to use."* Same root gives **utility**, **utensil**, **utilise**, and **utilitarian** — all of them about a thing earning its keep by doing a job.

# **vapid** *(adj.)* — **VAP-id**

*Rhymes with rapid. A VAY-pid variant exists, but VAP-id is standard.*

### Means
Offering nothing — no flavour, no substance, no spark. Flat, empty, lifeless.

What makes it sting is that *vapid* things are usually not unpleasant. A vapid smile, a vapid pop song, a vapid conversation about the weather — all perfectly agreeable on the surface, and completely hollow underneath. **Boring** says you were bored; **vapid** says there was nothing there to be interested in. It's a critic's word, faintly snobbish, and it always looks down on what it describes.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Vapid** rhymes with **insipid** — and means the same thing.

Both describe something with all the flavour drained out, whether it's a soup or a screenplay.

### In sentences
1. The novel's dialogue is **vapid** — three hundred pages of pleasant people saying nothing to one another.
2. He gave me this **vapid** smile and asked if I'd had a nice weekend, for the fourth time.

### In the wild
It's the stock verdict in music and film reviews — the word a critic reaches for when a song is catchy, glossy, and utterly empty.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vapidus** = *"flat, stale"* — said of **wine that had lost its kick**. Same root as **vapor**: the good stuff has **evaporated**, and what's left in the glass is flavourless liquid.

That image is the definition. Vapid = the wine's gone flat.
