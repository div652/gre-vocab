# **acolyte** *(n.)* — **AK-uh-lyte**

### Means
Literally, an **assistant who helps a priest at the altar** — the one carrying the candle. Figuratively, a **devoted follower or attendant** of a leader, thinker, or movement.

Outside church, it usually carries a curl of the lip. A *disciple* learns; a *supporter* agrees; an **acolyte** *serves* — junior, hovering, devoted past the point of independent judgement. Journalists reach for it when they want to say "entourage" without saying "entourage." Calling someone a founder's acolyte is never quite a compliment to the acolyte.

⚠️ **acolyte ≠ neophyte.** An **acolyte** is defined by *whom he serves*; a **neophyte** is defined by *how little he knows*. A twenty-year acolyte is still an acolyte; a twenty-year neophyte is a contradiction.

### Trick to lock it in
> The aco**lyte** carries the **light** — and walks *behind* the man holding it.

The candle-bearer image locks in both halves of the word: real devotion, permanently second in line.

### In sentences
1. Surrounded by **acolytes** who transcribed his every aside, the professor lost the habit of being contradicted.
2. He's not an advisor, he's an **acolyte** — he'd agree with the boss twice on the same sentence.

### In the wild
Star Wars: **The Acolyte** (2024) — the title means exactly this: not a Sith lord, but the devoted servant trailing one.

### Where it comes from
Greek **akolouthos** = *"follower, one who walks the same path"* (*keleuthos* = path). The meaning hasn't moved in two thousand years — someone walking behind you.

# **anoint** *(v.)* — **uh-NOYNT**

### Means
**Literally:** to smear or rub with oil as part of a sacred rite — a king at his coronation, a body before burial, a sick person receiving last rites.

**Figuratively:** to single someone out as the chosen one, especially a successor.

The figurative sense keeps the incense in it. To *appoint* a deputy is paperwork; to **anoint** a successor is to bless them, to hand over the aura along with the job — the choice feels ordained rather than decided. Journalists lean on it precisely for that whiff of dynasty ("the anointed heir"), and **self-anointed** is pure sneer: you consecrated yourself and nobody else was consulted.

⚠️ **anoint ≠ appoint.**
- **appoint** = *assign* to a post — administrative, revocable
- **anoint** = *consecrate* as chosen — ceremonial, quasi-sacred

A board appoints a CEO. The retiring founder anoints one.

### Trick to lock it in
> **an-OINT hides *ointment*** — you can't anoint anyone without the oil, and that oil is what turns a mere choice into a blessing.

The word carries its own grease: *ointment* is right there in the spelling, and the oil is exactly what separates anointing from ordinary appointing.

### In sentences
1. The archbishop **anointed** the young queen with chrism before the crown ever touched her head.
2. Everyone knew he'd been **anointed** the moment the boss started bringing him to the investor calls.

### In the wild
**Christ** isn't a surname — Greek *khristos* means *"the anointed one"*, a direct translation of Hebrew *mashiach*, **Messiah**. Two of the most familiar words in English are both just "anointed."

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** + **unguere** = *"to smear on."* That root oils a whole family: **unguent** (a salve), **ointment**, and — best of all for the GRE — **unctuous**, literally "greasy," now meaning oily in the flattering, insincere way.

# **base** *(adj.)* — **BAYS**

### Means
**Morally low** — contemptible, dishonourable, unworthy of a decent person. *Base motives, base instincts, base flattery.*

Forget the noun (bottom, foundation) — the GRE wants the adjective, and it is a **judgement of character**. *Vile* and *disgusting* react to the deed; **base** measures the person against a standard of honour and finds them beneath it. It attaches most naturally to **motives and impulses** rather than acts: greed, cowardice, spite, betrayal for money. The word carries a whiff of the pulpit and the nineteenth-century novel — nobody says it in a bar, which is exactly why it reads as withering on the page.

⚠️ **base ≠ baseless.**
- **base** accusation = a *contemptible* one, made from a filthy motive
- **baseless** accusation = a *groundless* one, with no evidence behind it

Different words entirely — *baseless* belongs to the other "base," the foundation.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Base is bass** — the *low* note. A base man is a low man.

They sound identical because they *are* the same word, and "low" is the whole idea: low pitch, low character.

### In sentences
1. He refused the bribe, calling it an appeal to the **basest** part of a public servant.
2. Selling your friend's secrets for a promotion? That's just **base**.

### In the wild
Commodity markets and chemistry still split metals into **precious** and **base** — gold and platinum on one side, copper, lead and zinc on the other. The cheap ones were literally the *low*, ignoble metals; alchemists spent centuries trying to raise them.

### Where it comes from
Late Latin **bassus** = *"low, short"* → French *bas*. Same root gives **bass** (the low voice), **abase** and **debase** (to bring low), and the **basset** hound — a low-slung dog.

🚫 **Not the other *base*.** Foundation, baseball, basis, baseless all come from Greek **basis**, "a step, a footing." Identical spelling, unrelated word.

# **coercion** *(n.)* — **koh-ER-zhuhn**

*Also heard as koh-ER-shuhn; both are standard. The verb is coerce (koh-ERS).*

### Means
Making someone act against their will by **threat, pressure, or intimidation** — force applied to the *decision*, not necessarily to the body.

The key is that the victim still "chooses" — they sign, they confess, they hand over the money — but the choice was hollowed out beforehand. That makes coercion colder and more procedural than *bullying* and far more formal than *arm-twisting*; it is a word with legal teeth (a contract signed under coercion is void). In political theory it can be neutral, even technical — "the state's monopoly on coercion" — but in ordinary use it is an accusation.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Coerce** rhymes with **worse** — "do it, or things get *worse*."

Coercion is the threat hanging over the choice, not the blow itself.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal ruled that the testimony had been obtained by **coercion** and struck it from the record.
2. They didn't fire him — they just made it clear he'd never get another shift, which is **coercion** with better manners.

### In the wild
Every courtroom scene where a confession gets thrown out: nobody was beaten, the suspect was simply worn down and threatened until signing was the only way out — that's **coercion**, and it's why the evidence dies.

### Where it comes from
Latin **co-** (together) + **arcēre** (*to fence in, shut off*) — to hem someone in until only one option is left. That's the meaning exactly: coercion narrows the fence, it doesn't throw the punch.

Same root **arcēre** gives **exercise** (*ex-arcēre*, to drive out, keep at work) and **arcane** (shut away, hidden).

# **coin** *(v. / n.)* — **KOYN**

### Means
**Verb (the sense the GRE tests):** to **invent a word, phrase or name** and put it into circulation. **Noun:** a stamped piece of metal money.

The verb is narrow in a way *invent* and *devise* are not: you coin **language**, not machines or plans. It also carries a quiet claim of authorship — saying Freud *coined* "id" credits him with the term and implies the term caught on. Nobody coins a word that nobody else ever uses; a failed one is just a made-up word.

The literal metal sense survives in **coinage**, which does double duty: money, and a newly minted word.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Coin a word the way a mint coins money: stamp it fresh, then let it circulate.**

Both senses hang on one idea — something new is struck into existence and then passes from hand to hand.

### In sentences
1. Orwell **coined** "doublethink" in 1949, and the term has outlived the novel that carried it.
2. My sister **coined** "hangry" for our family years before the internet got hold of it.

### In the wild
People say **"to coin a phrase"** right before uttering a cliché — a joke about pretending to have just invented something everyone has said a thousand times.

### Where it comes from
Latin **cuneus** = *"wedge"* — the wedge-shaped die that stamped the metal. The die's name became the money's name, and the money's name became the verb.

Same root: **cuneiform** (wedge-shaped writing), **quoin** / **coign** (a cornerstone, a wedge).

# **cunning** *(adj. / n.)* — **KUN-ing**

### Means
**Adj.:** clever at getting what you want by indirect, sly, often deceptive means.
**Noun:** that skill itself — *he won by sheer cunning.*

The cleverness is never in doubt; the **honesty** is. *Clever* is neutral, *shrewd* is hard-headed but respectable, *cunning* is foxlike — it implies scheming, self-interest, and a willingness to go around the rules rather than through them. Calling a rival's plan cunning half-admires it and half-accuses it. The one clean use is of animals and thieves in stories, where slyness is the whole charm.

⚠️ **cunning ≠ canny.** Both come from the same "know" root, but **canny** means *shrewd and prudent* — a canny investor is careful with her money, and nobody is being tricked. Add the deceit and you get **cunning**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cunning = CAN + winning** — he knows how he *can* win, and he isn't fussy about how.

The word literally grew out of *can* ("to know how"), so the knowing is real — it's the method that's crooked.

### In sentences
1. The minister's resignation was a **cunning** manoeuvre: he left before the report could name him, and returned to the cabinet within a year.
2. She's not the smartest player at the table, but she's **cunning** — she'll let you win two hands and then clean you out.

### In the wild
Baldrick's catchphrase in *Blackadder* — **"I have a cunning plan."** The joke is that the plans are always elaborate, sneaky, and terrible, which is the word's whole flavour in four syllables.

### Where it comes from
Old English **cunnan** = *"to know how, to be able"* — the ancestor of modern **can**. So *cunning* first meant simply **skilled** (the King James Bible praises a "cunning workman"); the sly sense crept in later, as knowledge people used on you rather than for you.

Same root: **can**, **canny**, **uncanny** (literally *un-knowable* — hence eerie), and Scottish **ken** (*to know*).

# **discomfit** *(v.)* — **dis-KUM-fit**

*Middle syllable is the *cum* of *income*, and it takes the stress — not "DIS-com-fit."*

### Means
To throw someone off balance — to embarrass, unsettle, or frustrate them so that their poise collapses.

It is stronger and more active than *embarrass*: someone **does** it **to** you, and what gives way is your composure, your argument, your plan. A witness is discomfited by a lawyer's question; a general's strategy is discomfited by bad weather. Older texts use it to mean *rout in battle*, and that military edge still lingers — being discomfited is a small defeat, not just a blush. Formal, faintly bookish, and almost always passive: *he was visibly discomfited*.

⚠️ **discomfit ≠ discomfort.** *Discomfort* is a state you're in (physical or mild, often self-caused: a stiff chair, a hard question). *Discomfit* is something inflicted — an agent unsettles or defeats you. The two are unrelated in origin and only overlap by accident of sound.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside is **comfit** — a sugared sweet — not **comfort**. Different word, different job.

Spotting that the middle isn't *comfort* stops you from downgrading the word to "made mildly uneasy" — discomfit means someone actively undid you.

### In sentences
1. The minister was visibly **discomfited** when the reporter produced the memo he had denied ever seeing.
2. Nothing **discomfits** my dad like being asked to say a few words at a party.

### Where it comes from
Old French **desconfire** = *"to undo, destroy"* — *des-* (un-) + *confire* (to make, put together), from Latin **conficere**.

That *confire* root is a useful one: **confection**, **confetti** (originally sweets thrown at Italian carnivals), and **comfit** — the sugared almond hiding in the middle of this word.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *comfort*.** That's Latin *fortis*, "strong" — *fortify, fortitude, effort*. Same sound, unrelated family.

# **dissent** *(v. / n.)* — **di-SENT**

*Identical in sound to descent (a fall).*

### Means
**Verb:** to disagree openly with an official position, a majority, or an established doctrine.
**Noun:** that disagreement — or the people voicing it.

Dissent needs something to push against: a ruling, a consensus, a church, a party line. You can *disagree* with a friend over dinner; you **dissent** from a verdict. It's reasoned and on the record — the dissenter states grounds, usually in the very forum that overruled them. Milder in action than *rebellion* (no one is storming anything) but weightier in tone than *objection*, because dissent implies conscience rather than mere preference.

⚠️ **dissent ≠ assent.** One letter, opposite meanings: **assent** = *agree, consent*; **dissent** = *refuse to*. "The board assented" and "the board dissented" describe opposite meetings.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dissent** starts with **diss** — but it's a *diss* filed in writing, with reasons attached.

The "diss" gets you the disagreement; "with reasons attached" gets you the formality that separates dissent from just griping.

### In sentences
1. Two justices **dissented**, warning that the majority had quietly gutted a century of precedent.
2. Nobody **dissented** — everyone just nodded along and let the daft plan through.

### In the wild
Ruth Bader Ginsburg made the word a brand: her **dissenting opinions** and the black beaded "dissent collar" she wore to deliver them turned "I dissent" into T-shirt material.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dis-** (*apart*) + **sentīre** (*to feel*) — literally *to feel apart from the rest*.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **consent** (feel *with*), **assent** (feel *toward*), **resent** (feel it *back*), plus **sentiment, sentient, consensus**.

# **distill** *(v.)* — **dih-STIL**

*British spelling: distil, one l.*

### Means
**Literally:** to purify a liquid by boiling it and condensing the vapour — how whisky, gin and essential oils are made.
**Figuratively:** to extract the essential core of something large and reduce it to concentrated form.

The key is that the result is *stronger*, not just smaller. To **summarise** is to make short; to **condense** is to compress the bulk; to **distill** is to throw away everything inessential so what remains is more potent than the original. It implies patience and judgement — you distill a decade of research into one paragraph, and the paragraph hits harder than the decade.

⚠️ **distill ≠ instill.**
- **distill** = draw the essence **out** of something
- **instill** = drip a quality **into** someone (*instill discipline in the cadets*)

Same root, opposite direction.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **still** makes moonshine — what drips out is far stronger than what went in.

The word literally contains the apparatus, and distilling always yields something more concentrated than the source, never merely shorter.

### In sentences
1. Her final chapter **distills** forty years of fieldwork into a single, devastating claim about kinship.
2. Just **distill** it for me — I don't need the whole deck, give me the one thing that matters.

### In the wild
**"Triple Distilled"** on a bottle of Jameson — the whole boast is that running it through three times makes it purer and smoother. That's the figurative sense in miniature: more passes, less junk, stronger spirit.

### Where it comes from
Latin **de-** (*down*) + **stillare** (*to drip*), from **stilla**, *"a drop."* To distill is to let something come down drop by drop — which is why the word carries that sense of slow, patient extraction.

Same drop gives you **instill** (*to drip in*, gradually) and **distillation**.

# **dubious** *(adj.)* — **DOO-bee-uhs**

*British speakers often say DYOO-bee-uhs.*

### Means
Doubtful — and it points **both ways**:

- **Feeling doubt:** *I'm dubious about his plan.* (hesitant, unconvinced)
- **Causing doubt:** *a dubious character, dubious accounting.* (fishy, not to be trusted)

The second sense is where the GRE lives, and it works by **understatement**. *Fraudulent* is a charge you'd have to prove; *dubious* is a raised eyebrow — it says the thing smells wrong without committing you to saying so. That deniability is why journalists and auditors love it. It also runs ironic: a **dubious honour** or **dubious distinction** is an achievement nobody wants (*the dubious distinction of the highest crash rate in Europe*).

### Trick to lock it in
> **Du**bious = **duo** — of two minds, and never quite committing to either.

The word hovers between yes and no, which is exactly its tone: you don't accuse, you just decline to believe.

### In sentences
1. The committee accepted the figures, though several members remained **dubious** about how the surplus had appeared overnight.
2. He got the flat cheap, off a bloke with a **dubious** haircut and an even more **dubious** contract.

### In the wild
News writing runs on the fixed phrase **"the dubious distinction of"** — the standard polite way to report that someone leads a league table nobody wants to top.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dubium** = *"doubt"*, from **duo** = *"two"* — wavering between two options.

The same *du-* pays out elsewhere: **doubt**, **indubitable** (*not to be doubted* — the classic "certainly" word), **dubiety**, and plain **double**.

# **ebullient** *(adj.)* — **ih-BUL-yuhnt**

*Also heard as ih-BUUL-yuhnt; the -lli- is a "ly" glide, never "lee".*

### Means
Overflowing with cheerfulness and energy — visibly, audibly delighted.

This is joy that leaves the body. A happy person can be quiet; an **ebullient** one cannot — talking fast, laughing loud, greeting strangers. The word almost always approves, but it keeps a faint edge of *too much*: a hint that the mood is running hotter than the occasion requires and may not last. That edge is why journalists reach for it about crowds, campaigns and stock markets as often as about people.

### Trick to lock it in
> **E-BOIL-ient** — joy at a rolling boil, lid rattling, spilling down the sides of the pot.

Boiling is the whole idea: the feeling isn't just warm, it's escaping the container where everyone can see it.

### In sentences
1. The Prime Minister returned from the summit **ebullient**, promising reporters a deal by Friday.
2. He came back from the interview **ebullient** — bought a round for the whole office before they'd even called him.

### In the wild
Financial pages love "**ebullient** markets" — traders bubbling with optimism, prices frothing up. The root rather gives away how those stories tend to end.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ebullīre** = *"to boil out"* — **ex-** (out) + **bullīre** (to boil).

Same *bullīre* gives you **ebullition** (a sudden outburst of feeling), **bouillon** (broth — literally boiled stuff), and plain **boil** itself.

# **facetious** *(adj.)* — **fuh-SEE-shuhs**

*Rhymes with capricious — never "fa-SET-ee-us".*

### Means
Treating a serious matter with deliberately inappropriate humour — joking when the moment called for a straight answer.

It is not a compliment. **Witty** praises the joke; **facetious** criticises the timing. The word usually arrives as a rebuke (*"don't be facetious"*) or as a pre-emptive defence (*"I was only being facetious"*), which tells you what it implies about the speaker: a smart-alec dodging the actual question. The joke may even be funny — the charge is that it was flippant, not that it fell flat.

⚠️ **facetious ≠ fatuous.** *Facetious* is joking on purpose; *fatuous* (**FACH-oo-uhs**) is stupid without knowing it — a smug, empty remark from someone who thinks it's profound. The facetious man is being clever; the fatuous man only thinks he is.

### Trick to lock it in
> **f-a-c-E-t-I-O-U-s** — all five vowels, in order. The word is showing off, and so is the person.

It's the classic spelling curiosity, and it doubles as the meaning: facetiousness is cleverness performed at the wrong moment.

### In sentences
1. Asked to explain the missing funds, he offered a **facetious** remark about the office coffee budget, and the committee's patience ran out.
2. "Sorry, I was being **facetious** — I do actually think you should take the job."

### In the wild
The everyday habitat of this word is damage control: the "/s" tag online, or someone backtracking with *"I was being facetious"* after a joke lands in a serious thread.

### Where it comes from
Latin **facētus** = *"witty, elegant"* — originally pure praise. English kept the wit but soured the timing: what was once a compliment for a polished conversationalist is now a mild telling-off for cracking jokes in the wrong room.

🚫 Nothing to do with **face** or **facet** (those are Latin *faciēs*).

# **fallible** *(adj.)* — **FAL-uh-buhl**

### Means
Capable of making mistakes; liable to be wrong.

Note the *capable of* — **fallible** describes a standing capacity for error, not an error actually made. Calling a witness fallible doesn't say she lied; it says her memory is human equipment and human equipment slips. That's why the word usually arrives as a graceful concession — "I'm fallible," "juries are fallible," "the test is fallible" — a humane admission rather than an attack. Its real work is often done by its opposite: things are declared **infallible**, and *fallible* is what everything else quietly is.

⚠️ **fallible ≠ fallacious.**
- **fallible** = the *thinker* can err (a person, a memory, a method)
- **fallacious** = the *argument* does err (it contains a logical flaw)

A fallible scientist may still reason soundly; a fallacious proof is already broken.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fall**ible = **able to fall** — not fallen, just never guaranteed to stay standing.

The hidden *fall* plus *-ible* ("able to be") gives you the exact force of the word: potential for error, not proof of it.

### In sentences
1. Every constitution assumes that rulers are **fallible**, and builds the machinery to correct them.
2. Look, I'm **fallible** — I thought the meeting was Thursday.

### In the wild
**Papal infallibility** — the Catholic doctrine that the Pope cannot err when defining doctrine *ex cathedra*. It's the most famous claim of exemption from being fallible, which is precisely why the word sticks to it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fallere** = *"to deceive, to trip up."* One root, a large family: **fallacy** (a deceiving argument), **false**, **fail**, **default**, **fault**, and **infallible**.

Useful shade: the Latin is about *being deceived*, not merely being careless — a fallible mind is one that can be fooled.

# **florid** *(adj.)* — **FLOR-id**

*Also FLAH-rid in much of American English.*

### Means
**1.** Excessively ornate — of prose, speech, music, or decoration: overloaded with flourishes.
**2.** Of a face or complexion: **red, flushed, ruddy**.

Calling a style *florid* is never a compliment. *Ornate* can be admiring, *elaborate* is neutral, but *florid* says the decoration has crowded out the sense — too many flowers, not enough stem. Applied to a face, it's the flush of heat, drink, or high blood pressure, not the glow of health; doctors also use it for a symptom that has bloomed into full, unmistakable view (*florid psychosis*).

⚠️ **florid ≠ fervid.**
- **florid** = *overdecorated* (or red-faced)
- **fervid** = *burning with passion*

A florid speech is fussy. A fervid one is on fire. A politician can manage both at once.

### Trick to lock it in
> **FLOR** = **flor**al. Florid prose is *flowery* prose; a florid face has **bloomed**.

Both senses are the same picture — something has flowered too much, on the page or on the cheeks.

### In sentences
1. His **florid** dedication ran three paragraphs before it managed to name the person it was thanking.
2. Uncle Ray came in from the garden **florid** and sweating, complaining about the heat.

### In the wild
**Florida** — Ponce de León landed at Easter and named it *Pascua Florida*, "flowery Easter." Same root, and the state is genuinely lush. Florid = Florida = full of flowers.

### Where it comes from
Latin **flos, floris** = *"flower."* The family is large and free: **floral, flora, floret, flourish** (to bloom, to thrive), **efflorescence**, and **flourish** in the musical sense — a decorative curl of notes, which is exactly what florid writing is made of.

# **gawky** *(adj.)* — **GAW-kee**

### Means
Awkward and ungainly in the body — especially **tall, thin, and badly coordinated**, with limbs that seem to have arrived before the control over them did.

This is a word about the *body*, not the personality: a gawky person may be perfectly charming, they just knock over the glass. And it's rarely cruel — *gawky* is the mild, half-fond word we reserve for adolescence, carrying the assumption that they'll grow out of it. Call an adult gawky and you're being gentler than if you'd said *clumsy* or *oafish*.

**gawky vs. gauche.** *Gawky* is clumsy in the **limbs** — you trip on the step. *Gauche* (GOHSH) is clumsy in the **manners** — you ask the widow what the house sold for.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Gawky** is one letter off **lanky**, and it's exactly lanky plus **gawk**: too much limb, and everyone's staring.

Hear the *-anky/-awky* pair and you get the build; hear *gawk* inside it and you get the stares that the build attracts.

### In sentences
1. At fifteen he was all wrists and knees, a **gawky** boy folded uncomfortably into a school blazer two sizes too small.
2. Don't laugh — I was so **gawky** at that age I could trip over a flat floor.

# **inveigle** *(v.)* — **in-VAY-guhl**

*Also standard: in-VEE-guhl. Both are correct; never "in-VYE-gul".*

### Means
To win someone over by **flattery, charm, or sly deception** — to sweet-talk them into doing something they wouldn't otherwise do.

The victim always says yes. Nobody is forced, bribed outright, or threatened; they are *talked round*, and only later work out they were handled. That makes it slyer than **persuade**, warmer than **dupe**, and less pathetic than **wheedle** — an inveigler is smooth, not whiny.

Watch the grammar, because the GRE does: you **inveigle someone *into*** doing something, or **inveigle your way *into*** a place you had no business being.

⚠️ **inveigle ≠ inveigh.**
- **inveigle** *(into)* = coax someone with charm
- **inveigh** *(against)* = attack something in furious words

He inveighed **against** the scheme; she inveigled him **into** joining it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Inveigle** rhymes with **bagel** — you don't shove anyone through the door, you hold out the bagel and let them follow you in.

The lure does the work, which is exactly why the target walks in willingly and blames themselves afterwards.

### In sentences
1. Having no invitation and no title, he simply **inveigled** his way past the doorman with a plausible smile and a borrowed name.
2. She somehow **inveigled** me into helping her move flats on a Sunday, and I still don't know how.

### Where it comes from
From Old French **aveugler**, *"to blind"* — itself from Latin **ab oculis**, *"without eyes."* To inveigle someone is literally to **blind** them, and the flattery is the blindfold.

That *oculus* (eye) shows up everywhere once you see it: **ocular, oculist, binoculars, monocle, inoculate**.

# **jettison** *(v. (also n.))* — **JET-uh-suhn**

*Final syllable can be -suhn or -zuhn; both are standard.*

### Means
To **throw cargo overboard to lighten a ship or aircraft in distress** — and by extension, to **discard something deliberately because carrying it has become too costly**.

You don't jettison junk; you jettison things you'd have kept if you could. The word carries pressure behind it — a plan, a policy, a principle, a running mate — dumped because the vehicle won't stay up otherwise. *Discard* is neutral housekeeping, *abandon* suggests you walked away and left it lying there; **jettison** is a decision made under load, and it implies the thing had value and you're still moving afterwards.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Jet**tison = ***jet it*** out — you only jet it out when the ship must fly lighter.

The "jet" is literal (same root as the *jet* of a jet engine — stuff thrown out the back), and the "must fly lighter" is the nuance: it's a sacrifice, not a clear-out.

### In sentences
1. Facing a hostile committee, the minister quietly **jettisoned** the very clause he had spent two years defending.
2. Halfway through the move we just **jettisoned** the sofa — it was never getting up those stairs.

### In the wild
Watch any rocket launch stream: mission control calls out **"booster jettison"** and **"fairing jettison"** — expensive hardware thrown away on purpose, because the rest of the vehicle can't reach orbit dragging it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **iactāre** = *"to throw"* (frequentative of *iacere*). One root, a big family: **eject, project, reject, inject, trajectory, conjecture**.

The nautical branch is the fun one: **jetsam** (goods jettisoned, which sink or wash ashore — as opposed to *flotsam*, wreckage that floats off on its own), a **jetty** (thrown out into the water), and **jet** itself (a stream thrown forth).

# **mendacity** *(n.)* — **men-DASS-uh-tee**

*The adjective shifts the vowel: mendacious = men-DAY-shus.*

### Means
Untruthfulness — **lying, especially as a habit or a condition** rather than a single act.

A lie is a thing you told; **mendacity** is a thing you *are*, or a climate a whole household or institution breathes. That's why it lands so much harder than *dishonesty*: it is formal, faintly literary, and openly damning. Nobody calls their own fib mendacity. You use the word when you want to indict — to say the falsehood is systematic and the liar is corrupt through and through.

⚠️ **mendacity ≠ mendicancy.**
- **mendacious** = *lying*
- **mendicant** = *begging* (a mendicant friar lives on alms)

A beggar isn't a liar. Different Latin root entirely.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-acity names a standing quality: ten*acity*, ver*acity*, mend*acity*.** It's the anti-veracity.

The suffix tells you this is a permanent trait, not one occasion — tenacity isn't one grip and mendacity isn't one lie.

### In sentences
1. The report exposed a culture of **mendacity** at the highest levels: falsified figures, doctored minutes, and three years of denials.
2. After the fourth version of his story, we stopped calling it a misunderstanding — the guy's **mendacity** was the whole problem.

### In the wild
Tennessee Williams's *Cat on a Hot Tin Roof* runs on this word — Brick keeps naming the family's rot as **mendacity**, "a system that we live in." It's the play's central accusation.

### Where it comes from
Latin **mendum** = *"a fault, a blemish."* A *mendax* is someone with a defect in his speech — a liar.

Same root, opposite direction: to **a·mend** or **e·mend** is to *remove* the fault, and plain **mend** is the worn-down English version. So mending fixes the flaw; mendacity is the flaw.

# **munificent** *(adj.)* — **myoo-NIF-uh-suhnt**

### Means
Extremely generous in giving — **lavish, large-scale, open-handed**.

Anyone can be *generous*; **munificent** carries the size of the gift and the standing of the giver. It's the word for a patron endowing a library wing, not a friend picking up the bill. The tone is formal and admiring — it's praise offered publicly, usually to someone with real means, and it always points at the scale of what was handed over.

**munificent ≠ magnificent.** *Magnificent* = splendid to look at; **munificent** = splendid in what it gives away. A munificent donor might fund a magnificent building.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Muni**ficent = **money**-ficent — money made into a gift, and a big one.

The sound hook also carries the nuance: cash on a scale worth announcing, not small change.

### In sentences
1. The new research institute owes its existence to a **munificent** bequest from a former student.
2. He left a **munificent** tip — the waiter actually chased him down the street to check it wasn't a mistake.

### In the wild
Read the plaques in any university building: *"Made possible by the munificent gift of…"* — fundraising offices reach for this word precisely because it flatters the size of the cheque.

### Where it comes from
Latin **munus** = *"gift, public duty"* + **facere** = *"to make"* — literally *gift-making*.

That root pays out widely: **remuneration** (paying *back*), **immune** (*exempt* from public duty), **community** and **commune** (duties shared together), **municipal** (a town that takes on its own duties).

# **naive** *(adj.)* — **nah-EEV**

*Two syllables — never "NAY-iv". Often spelled naïve; the noun is naivety or naïveté.*

### Means
Lacking the experience or judgement to see how things really work — trusting too easily, expecting the world to be simpler and kinder than it is.

It is not an accusation of stupidity. Calling someone naive faults their **judgement while excusing their motives**: they were unspoiled, not dishonest — which is why it can land as gentle indulgence ("sweetly naive") or as a put-down ("don't be naive"), depending entirely on who says it. *Gullible* is narrower — it means you actually got taken in. *Ingenuous* is the warm version — frank and unguarded, no fault implied.

There's also a flatly neutral technical use: **naive art** (untrained painters like Rousseau), and **naive subjects** in an experiment — people with no prior exposure to the thing being tested.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Naive** rhymes with **believe** — the naive believe you.

The flaw isn't intelligence, it's that nothing has yet taught them to doubt.

### In sentences
1. The proposal rests on the **naive** assumption that goodwill alone will hold the ceasefire together.
2. Don't be **naive** — nobody upgrades your seat for free without wanting something.

### In the wild
The Kooks' 2006 hit **"Naïve"** — one of the most-played British indie singles of that decade, and a song addressed to exactly the kind of person the word describes.

### Where it comes from
French **naïf / naïve**, from Latin **nativus** = *"inborn, natural"* — from **nasci**, *"to be born."* A naive person is still in the state they were born in: untouched, untested, no experience layered on top.

That root is a GRE workhorse: **native, natal, nascent, innate, nation, nature, renaissance** (re-birth).

# **noble** *(adj. / n.)* — **NOH-buhl**

### Means
**Adj.:** (1) of high birth or aristocratic rank; (2) of high moral character — honourable, generous, willing to lose something for a principle.
**Noun:** a member of the aristocracy.

*Good* and *kind* describe decency at ground level; **noble** describes decency at altitude. It implies elevation — someone rising above pettiness, self-interest, or revenge, usually at a personal cost. That grandeur is also its weakness in modern speech: because it sounds lofty and faintly antique, "how noble of you" is far more often sarcasm than praise. In science the same idea goes literal — **noble gases** and **noble metals** are the ones too aloof to react or corrode.

⚠️ **noble ≠ Nobel.** **Noble** (NOH-buhl) is the adjective; **Nobel** (noh-BEL) is the prize, named after Alfred Nobel, the Swedish inventor of dynamite. The surname has nothing to do with the word — spelling them alike is a standard slip.

### Trick to lock it in
> **The noble gases are too aloof to react; the noble person is too high to stoop.**

Both senses turn on standing above the fray rather than merely being nice.

### In sentences
1. She refused the settlement on principle — a **noble** stand that cost her the contract and half her staff.
2. You cooked, you cleaned, and you let him take the credit? Very **noble** of you.

### In the wild
Mark Antony over Brutus's body in *Julius Caesar*: **"This was the noblest Roman of them all"** — praise for the one conspirator who acted from principle rather than envy. That's the moral sense in a single line.

### Where it comes from
Latin **nōbilis** = *"knowable, well-known, famous,"* from **noscere**, *"to know."* The nobility were simply the families everybody had heard of — rank was fame first, virtue later.

That root pays out widely: **notable**, **notorious** (known, but badly), **notice**, **recognize**, **cognition** — and **ignoble**, literally *"unknown,"* hence low-born, hence shabby.

# **parochial** *(adj.)* — **puh-ROH-kee-uhl**

### Means
**1.** Narrow in outlook — concerned only with your own small circle, unable to see beyond it. **2.** Literally: belonging to a parish (*parochial school*).

The insult isn't about intelligence, it's about **horizon**. Calling a view parochial says the person is competent inside their little patch and has never thought past its edge. It's cooler and more condescending than *narrow-minded*, which implies stubbornness or bigotry; parochial implies you simply never had the range. It attaches easily to institutions and debates — *parochial concerns*, *a parochial dispute* — meaning small-stakes squabbles that matter to no one outside the village.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Parochial** has **parish** in it — a mind that never leaves its own parish.

The village church marks the edge of the world for someone parochial: everything inside it is known, everything outside it is invisible.

### In sentences
1. Her critics dismissed the report as **parochial**, framing a global crisis entirely in terms of one county's tax base.
2. Once you've lived abroad for a year, the arguments back home start to sound pretty **parochial**.

### In the wild
In the US, a **parochial school** is a church-run school — the everyday survival of sense 2, and a free reminder that the whole word is built on *parish*.

### Where it comes from
Greek **oikos** = *"house, dwelling"* → *paroikia*, "a neighbouring dwelling," which became Church Latin for a **parish**.

That *oikos* root pays off widely: **economy** (managing the house), **ecology** (study of the house), **ecumenical** (the whole inhabited house — the opposite of parochial), **diocese**.

# **pedestrian** *(adj. / n.)* — **puh-DES-tree-uhn**

### Means
**Noun:** a person travelling on foot.
**Adjective (the GRE sense):** dull, unimaginative, uninspired — ordinary in a way that bores.

The adjective is a critic's dismissal, and its sting is that it doesn't call the work *bad*. It calls it competent and earthbound: nothing wrong, nothing lifting off. *Banal* accuses you of recycling other people's ideas; **pedestrian** accuses you of plodding along with your own. It's most at home reviewing prose, music, cooking, argument — anything that could have soared and instead walked.

⚠️ **pedestrian ≠ pedantic.**
- **pedestrian** = *dull, plodding, unremarkable*
- **pedantic** = *fussily obsessed with tiny rules and details*

A pedestrian lecture sends you to sleep; a pedantic one corrects your pronunciation of "espresso."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ped**estrian prose **walks** — it never flies.

The word literally means "on foot," and the insult is exactly that: stuck at ground level, moving but never taking off.

### In sentences
1. The reviewer conceded that the novel was flawlessly constructed, then killed it in one word: **pedestrian**.
2. Dinner was fine — just a bit **pedestrian**, honestly. I could've made that at home.

### In the wild
Every **"PEDESTRIANS ONLY"** sign you walk past is the literal sense doing the work for you — people on foot, at foot speed. The insult is the same picture applied to someone's writing.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pes, pedis** = *"foot"* → *pedester*, "on foot," which the Romans already used to mean plain, prosaic writing.

That root pays off across the exam: **pedal, pedestal, pedicure, biped, centipede**, plus two you'll meet on the GRE — **impede** (*feet shackled* = to hinder) and **expedite** (*feet freed* = to speed up).

# **prevaricate** *(v.)* — **pri-VAIR-i-kayt**

### Means
To speak evasively — to dodge, hedge, and shift ground so as to mislead **without ever committing to a flat lie**.

The whole force of the word sits in that gap. A liar says the false thing; a prevaricator says technically-true things in an order designed to leave you wrong. It's a formal, faintly lawyerly accusation — the word an editorial or a judge reaches for when "lied" would be too blunt, too actionable, or simply unprovable. Note also that it describes a *manner* of answering, not a single utterance: one dodge is a dodge, but a witness who keeps sliding is prevaricating.

⚠️ **prevaricate ≠ procrastinate.**
- **prevaricate** = dodge the **truth**
- **procrastinate** = dodge the **work**

They sound alike and both mean "avoid," which is exactly why the GRE likes the pair. A witness prevaricates; a student procrastinates.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pre-VARY-cate** — the story *varies* every time you ask it.

No single falsehood you can point to, just an answer that never twice comes out the same shape.

### In sentences
1. Pressed on what he knew and when, the minister **prevaricated** for forty minutes and conceded nothing.
2. Stop **prevaricating** — did you eat the last slice or not?

### In the wild
Standard newsroom vocabulary. When a politician won't answer, the write-up says he **prevaricated** — a way of alleging dishonesty in print without saying "lied" and inviting a libel suit.

### Where it comes from
Latin **praevaricari** = *"to walk crookedly, to straddle"* — from **varus**, *"bent, knock-kneed."* The literal image is a ploughman whose furrow wanders instead of running straight.

In Roman law it hardened into something sharper: a *praevaricator* was a prosecutor secretly in league with the defendant — straddling both sides of the case. Crooked line, crooked answer.

Same root gives you **varicose** veins — bent, twisted ones.

# **prime** *(adj. / n. / v.)* — **PRYME**

### Means
**Adj.:** first in rank or quality — *prime suspect, prime location, prime minister*.
**Noun:** the peak stretch of something's life — *an athlete past his prime*.
**Verb (the one the GRE tests):** to prepare something in advance so that it will work when the moment comes — prime a pump with water, prime a wall before painting, prime a jury with an opening statement.

As an adjective it's the language of the butcher and the estate agent: top-of-the-line, plainly stated, no poetry in it. The verb is where the interest lives — priming is always *preliminary*, a step whose whole value is that it comes before. Applied to people it slides toward coaching and manipulation: a witness who has been **primed** has been told what to say, and someone **primed for disappointment** has been quietly set up to fall.

⚠️ **prime ≠ prim.** *Prim* (PRIM) means stiffly proper, fussily correct — *a prim schoolmistress in a buttoned collar*. One letter apart, nothing in common.

### Trick to lock it in
> Everything *prime* is **first**: first in rank (prime minister), first in quality (prime cut), first in time (prime the pump).

If a sentence uses *prime* as a verb, it's the third one — the action that has to happen first for the real thing to work.

### In sentences
1. Counsel had plainly **primed** the witness: every answer arrived a half-second too smoothly.
2. Three coffees and a pep talk and he was **primed** for the interview.

### In the wild
**Optimus Prime** — the Transformers leader is literally "best-first," and he is the foremost of them all.

### Where it comes from
Latin **primus** = *"first."* The root pays out across the whole exam: **primary, primitive, primeval, premier, prince, principal, prima donna** (*first lady* of the opera), **primate** (*first-ranked* bishop, then first-ranked animal).

# **radical** *(adj. / n.)* — **RAD-i-kuhl**

### Means
**Adj.:** going to the root of something — **fundamental, thoroughgoing**; hence, in politics, favouring sweeping change to the foundations of a system.
**Noun:** a person who wants that kind of change.

The GRE almost always wants the first sense, and the distinction it tests is *depth vs size*. A **drastic** cut is a large cut; a **radical** rethink starts from the foundations and rebuilds. That's why *radical* sits so naturally beside **change, departure, overhaul, rethink, revision** — words about beginning again rather than doing more.

Politically it's the label of someone who won't settle for reform at the edges, and whether it reads as praise or accusation depends entirely on who's speaking. (The surfer-slang "radical!" = *awesome* is real but never tested.)

### Trick to lock it in
> **Drastic** is how *big*. **Radical** is how *deep* — all the way down to the root.

You can slash a budget drastically without changing anything fundamental; a radical budget asks what the money is for in the first place.

### In sentences
1. The committee rejected piecemeal amendments and called instead for a **radical** restructuring of the tax code.
2. She didn't just repaint the kitchen — she knocked the wall through. Pretty **radical** for a rental.

### In the wild
Every **√** you drew in school is officially the **radical sign** — and what it hands you is a *root*. Same word, same idea, no metaphor required.

### Where it comes from
Latin **rādīx, rādīcis** = *"root."*

The family is worth having: **radish** (a root you eat), **eradicate** (*e-* "out" + root — to pull up by the roots, not merely reduce), **deracinate** (uproot a person from their culture), **radicle** (a seedling's first root).

So *radical* never meant "extreme" first. It meant **at the root** — and extremity is just what happens when you insist on going that far down.

# **recrudescent** *(adj.)* — **ree-kroo-DES-uhnt**

*Noun form: recrudescence (ree-kroo-DES-uhns); verb: recrudesce.*

### Means
Breaking out **again** after a period of dormancy — a disease, a conflict, a grievance, an epidemic of rumours.

The word is reserved for **unwelcome** things. A trend can be *revived*, a career *resurgent*, a memory *rekindled* — but only a plague, a feud, or an old injury is **recrudescent**. It also implies the thing never truly left: it was quiet under the surface, not gone. Register is medical-formal, and a writer who uses it is usually signalling that the outbreak was inevitable.

### Trick to lock it in
> **re-CRUDE-escent** — the wound goes **crude** (*raw*) again.

Hear *crude* sitting in the middle and you get both halves at once: raw, and raw *again* — which is why nothing pleasant is ever recrudescent.

### In sentences
1. The ceasefire held for a decade before **recrudescent** border skirmishes made a mockery of it.
2. Every December my **recrudescent** hatred of shopping malls comes roaring back.

### In the wild
Epidemiologists use it precisely: a **recrudescent** malaria case is the *same* infection flaring back up from parasites that were never cleared — as opposed to a reinfection from a fresh mosquito bite. Same word, same logic: it was always still in there.

### Where it comes from
Latin **recrudēscere** = *"to become raw again,"* from **crudus** = *"raw, bloody, uncooked."*

That root is worth banking: **crude** (unrefined), **crudités** (raw vegetables), **ecru** (the colour of unbleached, raw cloth), and even **cruel** (*crudelis* — someone with a taste for blood).

# **temporal** *(adj.)* — **TEM-per-uhl**

### Means
**1.** Relating to **time** — temporal sequence, temporal distortion.
**2.** Relating to **this world** rather than the spiritual one — earthly, secular.

Sense 2 is the one the GRE actually wants, and it follows straight from sense 1: what exists in time is perishable, so *temporal* came to mean everything that isn't eternal. A bishop has spiritual authority; a king has **temporal** power. Used of ambition or pleasure, it quietly implies smallness — mere worldly stuff, set against something lasting.

(A third, purely anatomical sense: the **temporal** lobe, behind your temple.)

⚠️ **temporal ≠ temporary.**
- **temporal** = *of time* (or of this world)
- **temporary** = *lasting only a short time*

A temporal record spans centuries; a temporary one gets deleted Friday.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Temporal** is the exact opposite of **eternal** — in time, therefore of this world.

Anything caught inside time is earthly and perishable, which is how a word about clocks became a word about kings, money, and everything the soul is supposed to outlast.

### In sentences
1. The papacy's **temporal** holdings shrank to a few acres, though its spiritual reach was undiminished.
2. He talks about enlightenment a lot for a guy whose goals are all pretty **temporal** — car, corner office, bigger flat.

### In the wild
Every sci-fi **temporal anomaly** / **temporal paradox** — *Doctor Who*, *Star Trek*, *Loki* — is the time sense doing its day job.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tempus, temporis** = *"time."* One root, a lot of GRE mileage: **tempo** (speed in time), **contemporary** (sharing a time), **extemporaneous** (out of the moment, unrehearsed), **temporize** (stall for time), **temporary**, and even **tempest** (via *tempestas*, "season, weather").

# **transitory** *(adj.)* — **TRAN-zi-tor-ee**

### Means
Lasting only a short while; not permanent by its very nature.

Where *fleeting* is about speed and *ephemeral* is about sheer brevity, **transitory** is about **impermanence as a property**: the thing is a phase, and phases end. It suits abstractions — power, fame, pain, pleasure, an economic condition — and carries a faint moral or philosophical hum, the voice of someone telling you not to get attached. Compare *transient*, which more often describes things or people literally passing through (a transient population, a transient guest); transitory is what you say about the state of the world, not the man at the door.

⚠️ **transitory ≠ transitive.**
- **transitory** = *short-lived*
- **transitive** = *carrying over to an object* — a transitive verb takes one; a transitive relation passes along (if a=b and b=c…).

Same root, opposite usefulness on the exam.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Transit**ory — whatever it describes is only **in transit**, never resident.

The word wears *transit* on its face: it isn't stopping here, it's passing through on its way out.

### In sentences
1. Marcus Aurelius returns again and again to the same consolation: honour, grief and empire alike are **transitory**.
2. Don't panic about the bad reviews — this stuff is **transitory**, nobody will remember by Friday.

### In the wild
In 2021 the Federal Reserve insisted that surging prices were **"transitory" inflation** — a phase that would pass on its own. It didn't, and the word got retired from Fed press conferences in disgrace.

### Where it comes from
Latin **transīre** = *"to go across"* — *trans* (across) + **īre** (to go).

That little verb **īre** is a GRE workhorse: **exit** (*ex-* out), **transit**, **circuit** (*circum-* around), **initial** (*in-* going in), **perish** (*per-īre*, to go through and be gone), **ambition** (*amb-īre*, going around canvassing for votes), **itinerary**.

# **viable** *(adj.)* — **VEYE-uh-buhl**

### Means
Capable of **surviving on its own** — a viable seed sprouts, a viable newborn lives outside the womb. By extension: **workable enough to go ahead with** — a viable plan, a viable business, a viable candidate.

Notice how low the bar is. *Viable* promises survival, not success: it says the thing won't collapse, nothing more. That makes it quietly damning as praise — call a proposal "a viable option" and you've said it's alive, not that it's good. *Feasible* asks whether you can do it at all; *viable* asks whether it can keep going once you have.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Vi**able = **vi**tal, **sur-vi**ve, **vi**vid — the *vi* is life. A viable plan has a pulse; that's the whole claim.

If you remember that *viable* only certifies a heartbeat, you'll never mistake it for a compliment.

### In sentences
1. Below twenty-four weeks the foetus was not considered **viable**, and the surgeons planned accordingly.
2. Look, moving in with your parents is a **viable** plan — it's just not a fun one.

### In the wild
Every startup's **MVP — Minimum Viable Product**: deliberately the least you can ship that still survives contact with a customer. The whole term hangs on *viable* meaning "barely alive, but alive."

### Where it comes from
French **vie** = *"life"*, from Latin **vīta**. Same root: **vital**, **vitamin**, **survive**, **revive**, **vivid**, **vivacious** — anything with that *vi-* is about living.
