# **accord** *(n. / v.)* — **uh-KORD**

### Means
**Noun:** a formal agreement between parties — especially nations; more loosely, a state of harmony.
**Verb:** to **grant** something (a right, a status, a courtesy) formally, or to **be consistent with** ("her story *accords with* the evidence").

*Agreement* is the everyday word; **accord** is the one that gets signed in a room with flags — treaty register, diplomatic weight. The verb is equally elevated: you don't *accord* someone a sandwich, you accord them recognition, honours, a hearing — it's bestowal from a position that has the standing to bestow. Learn the frozen phrase too: **of one's own accord** = unprompted, from nobody's urging but your own.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **chord** is notes agreeing; an ac**cord** is parties agreeing — and both are harmony you can hear from across the room.

The sound hook also carries the register: a chord is deliberate and structured, and so is an accord — never a casual understanding but a composed, formal one.

### In sentences
1. The 1998 **accord** held for a decade before either side seriously tested it.
2. Nobody made her apologise — she did it of her own **accord**.

### In the wild
The **Honda Accord** was named for exactly this: harmony between the car, its driver, and society. A word you've read on a boot lid a thousand times.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **cor, cordis** (*heart*) — literally *bringing hearts together*. That's why "of one's own accord" means *from your own heart*.

One root, a whole GRE family: **concord** (hearts together — peace), **discord** (hearts apart — strife), **cordial** (warm, heartfelt), **courage** (heart), and **record** (originally *to call back to the heart* — to memorise).

# **ad hoc** *(adj. / adv.)* — **ad HOK**

*Also heard as ad HOHK; both are standard.*

### Means
Made or done **for this one specific purpose**, with no thought to any wider plan — and therefore improvised, temporary, unsystematic.

Two flavours pull in opposite directions. **Neutral-administrative:** an *ad hoc committee* is simply one convened for a single question and dissolved afterwards — nobody is being insulted. **Mildly damning:** an *ad hoc* solution, policy, or hypothesis is a patch, cobbled together to handle the case in front of you, with no principle behind it that would survive the next case. In science and philosophy the label is close to an accusation: an *ad hoc* assumption is one bolted onto a theory purely to stop it being refuted.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ad hoc** is one letter from **a hack** — and that's exactly what an ad hoc fix is.

It works for *this* case and nothing else, which is why the word so often carries a faint whiff of "we made it up on the spot."

### In sentences
1. The board appointed an **ad hoc** subcommittee to review the single disputed clause, with a mandate to disband once it reported.
2. There's no process — we just fix things **ad hoc** whenever someone complains loudly enough.

### In the wild
Every analytics tool sells **"ad hoc queries"** or **"ad hoc reporting"** — meaning one-off questions you ask on the spot, as opposed to the scheduled dashboards.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad hoc** = *"to this."* Straight to *this* case, no further.

Worth banking the whole *ad-* phrasebook, since the GRE likes them: **ad hominem** (*to the man* — attack the person, not the argument), **ad nauseam** (*to sickness*), **ad infinitum** (*to infinity*), **ad lib** ← *ad libitum* (*at pleasure*).

# **adhere** *(v.)* — **ad-HEER**

### Means
**Literally:** to stick fast to a surface (glue, tape, barnacles). **Figuratively — the GRE sense:** to follow a rule, standard, plan or belief faithfully and continuously.

The figurative use is a compliance word: you adhere to a protocol, a schedule, a treaty, a diet. *Obey* is about authority in the moment; *adhere* is about staying attached over time, usually by your own choice — which is why a believer is an **adherent**, not an obeyer. It's slightly formal and faintly bureaucratic; nobody says "I adhered to the recipe" over dinner, but every compliance memo says it.

⚠️ **adhere ≠ cohere.**
- **adhere** = stick *to something else* (tape to a wall, a party to its manifesto)
- **cohere** = stick *together, internally* (an argument whose parts hold as one)

An adhesive bonds two things; a coherent essay holds itself.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ad-HERE** — you stay *here*, and keep staying here.

The buried word *here* gives you both halves at once: attachment to a fixed point, sustained rather than one-off.

### In sentences
1. The signatory states pledged to **adhere** to the terms of the accord for a further decade.
2. If you'd just **adhered** to the instructions, the shelf wouldn't be leaning like that.

### In the wild
Every workplace email that ends "please **adhere** to the guidelines" — the word lives in policy documents, dress codes, and safety notices.

### Where it comes from
Latin **haerēre** = *"to stick."* Add *ad-* ("to") and you get sticking **to** something.

The root pays out widely: **cohere/coherent** (stick together), **inherent** (sticking *in* — built into the thing), **adhesive**, and — the surprise — **hesitate**, from *haesitāre*, "to be stuck," which is exactly what hesitation is.

# **all-encompassing** *(adj.)* — **awl-en-KUM-puh-sing**

*Hyphenated in both British and US usage; stress lands on KUM, not on "all".*

### Means
Covering everything within its scope — leaving nothing and nobody out.

It attaches to abstractions rather than objects: a theory, a vision, a definition, a grief, a love. *Comprehensive* is the auditor's word — thorough, box-ticked. **All-encompassing** is bigger and vaguer, and carries a hint of awe: the thing has no edges. Usually admiring, but turn it on a feeling and it becomes claustrophobic — an all-encompassing obsession has room for nothing else.

**all-encompassing ≠ all-consuming.** All-encompassing = *includes* everything. All-consuming = *devours* you, crowding out everything else. A framework is all-encompassing; a jealousy is all-consuming.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's a **compass** in it — draw one big circle, and everything's inside.

The word literally encircles its subject, which is why it suits boundless things — visions, theories, obsessions — rather than tidy checklists.

### In sentences
1. Hegel's ambition was an **all-encompassing** system in which every art, religion and state found its allotted place.
2. She's got this **all-encompassing** spreadsheet for the wedding — seating, playlist, who's not speaking to whom.

### Where it comes from
Old French **compasser** = *"to measure by pacing around"* — from Latin **com-** (together) + **passus** (a step). To *encompass* something is to walk a ring around it.

Same root steps into **compass**, **pass**, **passage**, **pace** and **surpass**.

# **aphorism** *(n.)* — **AF-uh-riz-uhm**

*Not "uh-FOR-izm" — the stress lands on the first syllable, as in the adjective aphoristic (af-uh-RIS-tik).*

### Means
A **short, polished statement of a general truth** — one sentence that compresses an observation about life into its smallest hard form. *"Less is more." "The unexamined life is not worth living."*

What marks it off from its neighbours is **authorship and craft**. A proverb is folk property, worn smooth by centuries of anonymous use; an aphorism has a writer behind it and shows the tooling — balance, surprise, often a twist of wit. A maxim tells you what to *do*; an aphorism tells you what *is*. And calling something an aphorism is mildly admiring: it means the line earned its brevity. The insult version is **platitude** or **cliché**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Aphorism** shares its root with **horizon**: one clean line that fences a whole truth off from everything else.

Both come from Greek *horizein*, "to draw a boundary" — the aphorism's whole trick is where it decides to stop.

### In sentences
1. Nietzsche wrote in **aphorisms** rather than arguments, trusting a single detonating sentence to do the work of a chapter.
2. My gran had an **aphorism** for everything, and annoyingly she was usually right.

### In the wild
Oscar Wilde is the patron saint of the form — *"I can resist everything except temptation."* That's the shape: short, balanced, and it turns on you at the end.

### Where it comes from
Greek **aphorismos**, from **apo-** (*off, away*) + **horizein** (*to bound, to define*) — literally a *marking-off*, a definition. Hippocrates' medical *Aphorisms* opened with "Life is short, art is long."

Same **horizein** gives you **horizon** — the line that bounds what you can see. Same **apo-** gives **apogee**, **apostasy**, **apostrophe**.

# **benchmark** *(n. / v.)* — **BENCH-mark**

### Means
**Noun:** a fixed standard or reference point against which other things are measured, compared, or judged.
**Verb:** to test something by measuring it against that standard.

The word is cold and quantitative — a benchmark is a *line*, not an ideal. A **paragon** is perfection you admire; a **benchmark** is simply the agreed measuring stick, and it can be mediocre (a fund can beat its benchmark and still lose money). It also implies **comparability**: the same test, run the same way, on everything. That's why it belongs to reviewers, analysts, and engineers rather than to praise.

⚠️ **benchmark ≠ hallmark.**
- **benchmark** = the *standard you measure against*
- **hallmark** = a *distinguishing feature* that identifies something ("obsessive detail is the hallmark of his work")

### Trick to lock it in
> A benchmark is a **mark**, not a **medal** — you measure against it, you don't win it.

Clearing a benchmark only means you crossed the reference line, which is why a chip, a fund, or a student can be "above benchmark" and still be unremarkable.

### In sentences
1. The 1962 recording remains the **benchmark** against which every later performance of the concerto is quietly judged.
2. Don't **benchmark** yourself against people who got a ten-year head start.

### In the wild
Every phone and laptop review runs **benchmark** scores — apps like *Geekbench* and *3DMark* exist for nothing else: run the identical test on every device so the numbers can be lined up side by side.

### Where it comes from
Literally a **bench** + a **mark**. Victorian surveyors chiselled a mark into a wall or stone at a precisely known height, then rested the bracket ("bench") of their levelling rod in it — every other elevation in the district was measured from that one immovable cut.

So the whole modern meaning is already in the object: a *fixed physical reference point that everything else is measured from*. Same **-mark** family, different jobs: **landmark** (you navigate by it), **hallmark** (it identifies), **earmark** (it reserves).

# **blighted** *(adj. / v. (past part.))* — **BLEYE-tid**

### Means
Ruined, withered, spoiled — struck by something that eats away at what was once healthy or promising.

The word starts in agriculture: a **blight** is a plant disease that shrivels a crop in the field. Everything else is that image applied elsewhere. So *blighted* carries three things a plain word like *ruined* doesn't: the damage **crept in from outside**, it **spread**, and the thing was **flourishing or full of promise beforehand**. A blighted career had prospects. A blighted neighbourhood had life in it. Blighted hopes were real hopes. Nothing is *blighted* if it was never going anywhere.

⚠️ **blight ≠ plight.** A **blight** is the affliction that ruins something; a **plight** is the bad situation a person is stuck in. The famine was a blight; the farmers' plight was starvation.

### Trick to lock it in
> **b-LIGHT-ed** — the *light* has gone out of it.

Something blighted was once lit up with promise and has been slowly dimmed by an outside affliction.

### In sentences
1. Decades of factory closures left the town **blighted** — half its shopfronts boarded, its young people gone.
2. One bad tackle and his whole season was **blighted**.

### In the wild
**Potato blight** caused the Irish Famine — a fungus that crept through the fields and turned a healthy staple crop black in the ground. That's the literal picture behind every figurative use.

# **content** *(adj. (also v. / n.))* — **kuhn-TENT**

*Stress carries the meaning: kuhn-TENT = satisfied; KON-tent = the stuff inside (the noun).*

### Means
**Adj.:** satisfied with what you have; not wanting more. **Verb:** *content oneself with* = to accept something lesser without complaint.

This is a **low-temperature** word. *Happy* and *delighted* point upward; **content** points at an absence — no craving, no restlessness, nothing missing. You can be content and completely unexcited. That's why *"he was content to stay in the junior role"* reads faintly of settling: contentment is peace, but on the GRE it often shades into **undemanding acquiescence**.

⚠️ **content ≠ contentious.** *Content* = at peace, wanting nothing. *Contentious* = quarrelsome, or hotly disputed. Near-identical spelling, opposite temperament — and unrelated roots (*hold* vs *strive*).

### Trick to lock it in
> **conTENT = your wanting is con-*tained*.**

The satisfied sense and the *container* sense are the same word: desires held in, lid on, nothing spilling out toward more.

### In sentences
1. Having renounced the estate, he professed himself **content** with a scholar's stipend and a room facing the garden.
2. Honestly? Give me a sofa and bad TV and I'm **content**.

### In the wild
Philippians 4:11 — *"I have learned, in whatsoever state I am, therewith to be **content**"* — the verse that fixed the word's meaning in English as *acceptance*, not joy.

### Where it comes from
Latin **continēre** = *"to hold together, hold in"* (**con-** *together* + **tenēre** *to hold*) → *contentus*, "restrained, satisfied."

The whole family is about holding: **contain, container, continent** (as an adjective, *self-restrained*), **incontinent** (unable to hold it in), **countenance** (your bearing — how you hold yourself). And **tenēre** alone gives **tenacious, tenant, retain, detain, tenable**.

# **debase** *(v.)* — **dih-BAYS**

### Means
To **lower the value, quality, or dignity** of something — usually by mixing in something inferior, cheapening it, or dragging it down morally.

The word carries a sense of **corruption from within**, not destruction from outside. You don't debase a thing by smashing it; you debase it by diluting it while it keeps its old name and face value. A currency is debased when the coin still says *silver* but is half copper; a language is debased when the words survive but mean less; a person is debased when they're made to act beneath themselves. There's always a **before** — something had worth, and you degraded it.

⚠️ **debase ≠ abase.**
- **debase** = cheapen the **quality or worth** of a thing (coinage, art, standards)
- **abase** = humble the **status** of a person, especially oneself (*he abased himself before the king*)

You debase the currency; you abase yourself.

### Trick to lock it in
> **De-BASE** = drop it to the **base** metal — same face value, less gold in it.

The word wears its meaning on the outside: *base* metals are the cheap ones (lead, copper), so to debase is to sneak them in while the surface still looks precious.

### In sentences
1. Successive emperors **debased** the denarius until a coin of silver was a coin of copper wearing a thin silver skin.
2. He wouldn't **debase** himself by begging for a job he'd been doing for free all year.

### In the wild
"**Debasing the currency**" is live financial vocabulary — every argument about money-printing, inflation, or Bitcoin's fixed supply runs on it, and it means exactly what it meant to the Romans: same coin, less metal.

### Where it comes from
**de-** (*down*) + **base**, but not the base you stand on — this is Latin **bassus**, *"low, short."* That's the *base* in **base metals** and in the old insult *"a base and cowardly act,"* and the **bass** that's a low note.

So the whole word is literally *"to lower the low-ness"* — to push something further down the scale of worth.

# **disconcerting** *(adj.)* — **dis-kuhn-SER-ting**

### Means
Unsettling in a way that throws you off balance — it disturbs your composure, confidence, or sense of what's normal.

Strength matters here: *terrifying* is fear, *appalling* is moral shock, but **disconcerting** is much cooler and smaller — you're not frightened, you're wrong-footed. It's the word for a stare held one second too long, a smile that doesn't match the news, an answer that doesn't fit the question. Educated, slightly understated register: a British reviewer or a doctor uses it precisely because it withholds panic.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's a **concert** inside it — *dis-concert* means the orchestra fell out of tune, not that the hall caught fire.

The damage is to harmony and poise, not to safety — which is exactly why the word stays mild.

### In sentences
1. The witness gave her testimony with a **disconcerting** calm, as though describing someone else's life.
2. It's a bit **disconcerting** when your GPS says "turn left" and there's just a lake there.

### Where it comes from
From **concert** (via French *concerter*, from Latin *conserere / concertare* — "to bring into agreement"), plus the reversing prefix **dis-**. To *concert* is to get parties acting in harmony; to **disconcert** is to break that harmony.

Same root gives you **concert**, **concerted** (*a concerted effort* = agreed and coordinated, not merely strong), and **concerto**.

# **extant** *(adj.)* — **EK-stuhnt**

*Also heard as ek-STANT; both are accepted.*

### Means
Still in existence; not lost, not destroyed, not extinct.

The word almost always looks **backwards in time**. You don't call your phone extant — you call it that when something has *survived a process that destroyed the rest*: three of the letters are extant, the oldest extant copy of the manuscript, the last extant colony of a species. It carries the quiet implication that most of the set is gone. Register is scholarly — archivists, historians, biologists, lawyers — and it's coolly neutral: no relief, no celebration, just the surviving inventory.

⚠️ **extant ≠ extinct.** One letter, opposite verdicts — **extant** = still here; **extinct** = wiped out. An extant species is alive today; the dodo is extinct.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ex-STANT — it's still *standing*. Ex-TINCT — it's been *extinguished*.**

The *-stant* is literally *stare*, "to stand," while *-tinct* is the same root as *extinguish*, so one word is upright and the other is snuffed out.

### In sentences
1. Of the twelve original panels, only four are **extant**; the rest burned with the abbey in 1643.
2. There's exactly one **extant** photo of my grandad as a kid, and my mum won't let anyone touch it.

### In the wild
Open almost any Wikipedia species page and the taxobox reads **"Extant"** or **"Extinct"** right under the fossil-range bar — the two words are the standing labels for alive-today versus gone.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** (*out*) + **stāre** (*to stand*) — "standing out, still standing there."

**stāre** is a workhorse root worth banking: **status, stable, stance, constant, stationary, obstacle** (something standing in the way), **circumstance** (what stands around you), **substance** (what stands underneath).

🚫 Not related to **extent** — that's *tendere*, "to stretch" (*extend, tension, tendency*). Same first five letters, unrelated word.

# **facility** *(n.)* — **fuh-SIL-uh-tee**

### Means
**1.** An **effortless ability** to do something well — *a facility with numbers, a facility for languages*.
**2.** (everyday) A building or service set up for a purpose — *a medical facility*.

The GRE tests sense 1. *Facility* isn't raw talent or deep genius; it's **smoothness in execution** — the thing looks easy when you do it. *Aptitude* is potential, *facility* is potential already fluent. And it's faintly double-edged: praising someone's *facility* for excuses, or for turning a phrase, can hint that the ease came cheaply.

⚠️ **facility ≠ facile.** Same root, opposite verdict. **Facility** = admirable ease (*a facility with words*). **Facile** = ease that shows the work wasn't done (*a facile argument* is shallow, not skilled).

### Trick to lock it in
> A **facility** is a building that makes a job easy — the trait is the same easiness, installed in a person.

The boring sense you already know and the GRE sense are one idea: *facility* is whatever makes the doing effortless.

### In sentences
1. Her **facility** with cross-examination was such that witnesses often conceded points before realising they had been led there.
2. He's got this weird **facility** for remembering everyone's coffee order after meeting them once.

### In the wild
Every sign you walk past — *sports facility, correctional facility, medical facility* — is naming a place built to make one activity easy. Same word, same idea, just poured into concrete.

### Where it comes from
Latin **facilis** = *"easy,"* from **facere** = *"to do, to make."* That root is everywhere: **facilitate** (make easy), **faculty** (an ability — or the people who have it), **factory**, **manufacture**, **benefactor**, **affect**. If a GRE word has *fac-* or *fic-* in it, it's usually about **doing or making**.

# **fitful** *(adj.)* — **FIT-fuhl**

### Means
Happening in short, irregular bursts with pauses between — **stop-start, unsteady, never settling into a rhythm**.

*Intermittent* is a neutral clock word: the light blinks, the signal drops. **Fitful** carries restlessness and frustration with it — the starting and stopping is unsatisfying, and usually the thing never properly gets going. Hence its favourite companions: **fitful sleep**, a fitful breeze, fitful progress, a fitful recovery. If a nation's economy grows fitfully, the writer is telling you the growth doesn't hold.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fitful = "in fits and starts"** — the *fit* is a spasm, not a good fit.

The everyday phrase *by fits and starts* already means exactly this, and *fitful* is just that phrase compressed into an adjective.

### In sentences
1. Her recovery was **fitful** — weeks of steady improvement undone by a single bad night.
2. I got maybe three hours of **fitful** sleep, half of it waiting for the alarm to go off.

### In the wild
Macbeth on the murdered Duncan: *"After life's **fitful** fever he sleeps well."* Life as a bout of restless, feverish twitching — and death as the first real rest.

### Where it comes from
From the noun **fit** in its old sense of *a sudden seizure or paroxysm* — an epileptic **fit**, a coughing fit, a fit of rage. Something fitful comes in those little attacks.

🚫 **Not the *fit* that means "suitable" or "in shape."** Unrelated word entirely — a fitful jacket is not a well-tailored one.

# **incite** *(v.)* — **in-SITE**

### Means
To **stir people up into action** — usually violent, unlawful, or at least reckless action: a riot, a mutiny, a mob.

The blame sits on the inciter, not the crowd. You *inspire* people to be better and you *provoke* someone into a reaction, but you **incite** a group into doing something — and the word quietly assumes that something is bad. It's also the verb the law reaches for (*incitement to violence*), which is why it lands as an accusation rather than a description. Note the shape: you incite a **riot**, or incite a **crowd to** riot — it wants an act, not a feeling.

**incite ≠ insight.** Same sound, opposite worlds: **incite** *(verb)* = whip a crowd into action; **insight** *(noun)* = a flash of deep understanding. One starts a fire, the other switches on a light.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Incite** is **ignite** — with people as the fuel.

Same ending, same idea: you set the crowd alight and stand back while it burns.

### In sentences
1. The pamphlet was suppressed on the grounds that it **incited** the garrison to mutiny.
2. He wasn't even at the protest — he just posted a video that **incited** half the city to show up.

### In the wild
The standard news-and-courtroom phrase **"charged with inciting a riot"** — the word does most of its living in headlines about someone who stirred up a crowd but never threw a punch.

### Where it comes from
Latin **citāre** = *"to set in motion, summon, call forth"*, with **in-** = *"on, into"*. Literally: to set someone moving.

That root is worth banking: **cite** (summon a source), **recite** (call out again), **excite** (stir out of calm), **solicit**, **resuscitate** (call back to life).

# **induce** *(v.)* — **in-DYOOS**

*Commonly in-DOOS in American speech.*

### Means
**1.** To lead someone into doing something — usually by offering them something. **2.** To bring about or cause a state or process (*induce sleep, induce vomiting, induce labour*). **3.** In logic: to infer a general rule from particular cases.

Force compels and orders command; *induce* works sideways. The person still walks in on their own feet — but the path was laid for them. That's why the noun is **inducement**: money, a discount, a promise, a threat sanded down into an offer.

In the causing sense it carries a whiff of the artificial. A drug **induces** sleep; a doctor **induces** labour. The outcome wouldn't have come on its own — or not yet.

**induce ≠ deduce.** Both are *ducere*, but they run opposite ways: **induce** reasons *up* from particular cases to a general rule (every swan I've seen is white → swans are white); **deduce** reasons *down* from the rule to the case. Induction can be wrong; valid deduction can't.

### Trick to lock it in
> You can't **induce** anyone without an **induce-ment** — the carrot is built into the word.

Nobody is forced by an inducement; they're offered one, and then they choose exactly what you wanted.

### In sentences
1. The treaty's tax concessions were designed to **induce** foreign manufacturers to build their plants inland rather than at the ports.
2. No amount of free pizza could **induce** me to sit through another three-hour all-hands.

### In the wild
**Induced labour** — when a birth won't start on its own, the hospital starts it. Everyday proof that *induce* means "make it happen that wouldn't have, or not yet."

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** ("in") + **dūcere** ("to lead") — literally *lead in*.

**-duc-** is one of the highest-yield roots on the GRE: **conduct** (lead together), **produce** (lead forth), **reduce** (lead back), **seduce** (lead astray), **abduct** (lead away), **educate** (lead out), **aqueduct** (leads water). Spot *-duc-* and you're looking at leading.

- **Opposites** · [Heading it off vs. bringing it on](groups.md#antonym__heading-it-off-vs-bringing-it-on) — with *forestall, obviate, preclude, avert, deter, engender, precipitate, foment, incite*
- **Root family** · [Root: dūcere (to lead) — conducive, induce, subdued](groups.md#root__ducere) — with *conducive, subdued*

# **infelicity** *(n.)* — **in-fuh-LIS-uh-tee**

### Means
An **awkward, ill-chosen or inappropriate expression** — a phrase that lands badly. More broadly, any small unfortunate feature of a thing, or (older, rarer) plain unhappiness.

Register does the work: this is the reviewer's and the diplomat's word. Calling a sentence *clumsy* is an insult; calling it *an infelicity* is a courtesy — you concede the writer meant well and simply picked the wrong words. It's mild, countable ("a few infelicities of style"), and almost always about **phrasing rather than fact**: an infelicity isn't an error, it's a tactless or graceless way of putting a thing.

⚠️ **infelicity ≠ infidelity.** One is a badly chosen *phrase*; the other is a badly chosen *partner*. Read the middle syllables — **-lic-** (Latin *felix*, happy) vs **-del-** (*fides*, faith).

### Trick to lock it in
> **An infelicity is a blunder in a bow tie.**

Same mistake, dressed politely — which is exactly why critics and diplomats reach for the word.

### In sentences
1. The translation is accurate enough, but a scattering of **infelicities** — "he ambulated to the door" — betrays a tin ear.
2. The apology was fine apart from one **infelicity**: he called her "the complainant" three times.

### In the wild
**Felix Felicis**, the liquid-luck potion in *Harry Potter*, is the root doubled up — *felix* = lucky, happy. Put **in-** on the front and you have the opposite: the unlucky, unhappy choice of words.

### Where it comes from
Latin **felix, felicis** = *"happy, lucky, fruitful"* + the negating **in-**.

The whole family is cheerful except this one: **felicity** (bliss), **felicitous** (aptly, happily phrased), **felicitations** (congratulations), and the name **Felix** — "the lucky one."

Note that *felicitous* already means **well-put**, not merely happy — so *infelicitous* is the natural adjective for a phrase that misses.

# **intelligible** *(adj.)* — **in-TEL-i-juh-buhl**

### Means
Able to be understood; comprehensible.

It's a **low bar, not a compliment**. *Lucid* praises writing as clear and elegant; *intelligible* only certifies that the meaning got through at all. That's why it lives mostly in the negative or the grudging — *barely intelligible*, *scarcely intelligible*, *unintelligible mumbling*. Applied to someone's speech, it usually implies a listener was straining.

In philosophy it hardens into a technical sense: the **intelligible** world is what's grasped by intellect alone, as opposed to the **sensible** world of what you can see and touch.

⚠️ **intelligible ≠ legible.**
- **legible** = the marks on the page can be *made out*
- **intelligible** = the meaning can be *got*

A note can be perfectly legible and still unintelligible — every letter crisp, the sentence nonsense.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Intel**ligible — you can get the **intel** out of it.

The information reaches you, which says nothing about whether it was delivered well.

### In sentences
1. His prose becomes **intelligible** only on a second reading, once the terminology has settled.
2. She was crying so hard that maybe one word in five was **intelligible**.

### In the wild
Transcripts and closed captions stamp **[unintelligible]** into the text wherever the audio defeats the transcriber — a court record, a leaked call, a YouTube auto-caption. The tag marks exactly this: sound arrived, meaning didn't.

### Where it comes from
Latin **inter** (*between*) + **legere** (*to pick out, read*) → *intellegere*, "to read between, to discern."

**legere** is a GRE workhorse: **legible**, **lecture**, **legend** (*things to be read*), **elect** and **select** (*pick out*), **collect**, and **intellect** itself.

# **makeshift** *(adj. (also n.))* — **MAYK-shift**

### Means
Serving as a **temporary substitute**, thrown together from whatever happened to be lying around: *a makeshift raft*, *a makeshift office at the kitchen table*.

*Temporary* says only "not for long." **Makeshift** adds two things: it was **improvised**, and it is **slightly inadequate** — it does the job, but visibly badly. There's a double edge to it. Calling a solution makeshift half-praises the resourcefulness and half-admits the thing is crude and will have to be replaced.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nothing **makeshift** stays put — the second half of the word is **shift**.

A makeshift thing is built to be shifted out the moment the real one arrives.

### In sentences
1. Cut off by the flood, the villagers erected a **makeshift** bridge of scaffolding planks and rope.
2. We had no music stand, so he propped the sheet on a **makeshift** one made of two textbooks and a shoebox.

### In the wild
News reports run on this word: a **makeshift memorial** of flowers and candles on a pavement, **makeshift hospitals** in convention centres during Covid. Both are the same idea — real need, no proper facility, so people build one out of what's to hand.

### Where it comes from
From the old phrase **"to make shift"** = *to contrive, to manage somehow*. **Shift** here means **an expedient**, not movement.

That older sense unlocks a cousin: **shiftless** = not lazy in the sleepy sense but **lacking resourcefulness**, unable to contrive anything for yourself.

# **onetime** *(adj.)* — **WUN-tyme**

### Means
**Former, ex-, previous** — someone or something that once held a role and no longer does: *a onetime rival, her onetime employer*.

Where *ex-* is blunt and personal and *erstwhile* is arch and literary, **onetime** is the tidy, journalistic option — it states the fact of a finished role without any feeling about it. It usually carries an implied *then vs. now* contrast: the onetime ally is mentioned precisely because he's now an enemy. Note the grammar, which is half the word: it only sits **in front of a noun**. You can say *his onetime partner*, never *"his partner is onetime."*

⚠️ **onetime ≠ one-time.** Solid, *onetime* = **former**. Hyphenated, *one-time* usually = **happening only once** (*a one-time payment*). A onetime donor used to give; a one-time donor gave exactly once.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Onetime** goes *before* the noun, because the person's time is already behind them.

If the word can't follow "is," you're dealing with the "former" sense, not the "only once" sense.

### In sentences
1. The committee was chaired by a **onetime** finance minister, long since retired from public life.
2. That's my **onetime** flatmate — we haven't spoken since the lease ended.

### In the wild
Justin Bieber's debut single **"One Time"** (2009) — and it means *once*, not *former*, which is exactly the split to remember: two words for the counting sense, one word for the has-been sense.

# **opportunistic** *(adj.)* — **op-er-too-NIS-tik**

*British often op-er-tyoo-NIS-tik.*

### Means
Seizing whatever chance presents itself — and, in most human contexts, doing so **without regard for principle, loyalty, or long-term plan**.

The word rarely describes a strategy; it describes the *absence* of one. Call a politician's reversal opportunistic and you're saying the position was chosen by circumstance, not conviction. Note the split, though: in sport, tactics, and biology the word loses its sting and becomes pure praise or plain description — an **opportunistic striker** is a compliment, an **opportunistic feeder** is just a fact about the animal. Damning of people, neutral of organisms.

**opportunistic ≠ opportune.**
- **opportune** = *well-timed*, a favourable moment (an opportune arrival) — praise for the **timing**
- **opportunistic** = *exploiting* the moment — usually a judgement on the **person**

### Trick to lock it in
> **opportun-ist**, like *pur-ist* or *career-ist* — an *-ist* is someone with one commitment, and his is to the opening itself.

The purist believes in purity and the careerist in his career; the opportunist believes in nothing but the gap in front of him.

### In sentences
1. Critics dismissed the ceasefire proposal as **opportunistic** — a bid for legitimacy timed to the week the sanctions took effect.
2. He's not loyal to the team, he's just **opportunistic** — the second a better offer landed he was gone.

### In the wild
In medicine, an **opportunistic infection** is one a healthy body shrugs off — thrush, pneumocystis — that turns lethal the moment the immune system is weakened. The pathogen isn't strong; it's just waiting for the opening.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob portum** = *"toward the harbour"* — said of a wind that happens to be blowing your ship home. So an *opportunity* was originally just favourable weather you didn't arrange.

That gives you the whole word's judgement for free: the opportunistic man has no course of his own, he simply sails wherever the wind is already going. Same **portus** root as **port, portal, import, export**.

# **plausible** *(adj.)* — **PLAW-zuh-buhl**

*The s is a z, never a hissed s.*

### Means
Seeming reasonable, believable, likely to be true — **on the face of it**.

That last phrase is the whole word. *Plausible* rates how something **sounds**, not whether it's true: an alibi, an excuse, a theory. Calling an argument plausible is faint praise — you're saying it survives first contact, nothing more. And in the mouth of a skeptic it tips negative: *a plausible enough story* means you don't believe a word of it. Compare **credible** (you actually lean toward believing it) and **specious** (it looks good and is definitely false); *plausible* sits between them, deliberately uncommitted.

**plausible ≠ feasible.** *Plausible* = believable (a claim, a story). *Feasible* = doable (a plan, a schedule). A plausible explanation of the delay; a feasible way to fix it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Plausible gets the applause, not the verdict.**

It's the story the room claps for on hearing — approval, not proof.

### In sentences
1. The defence offered a **plausible** account of the missing hour, though the jury noticed it rested entirely on the accused's own word.
2. Nice try — that's a **plausible** excuse, but I saw your car outside the pub at six.

### In the wild
**"Plausible deniability"** — the Cold War spy-and-politics phrase for arranging things so the boss can be believed when he says he knew nothing. Every thriller and every corporate scandal runs on it, and it captures the word exactly: believable, not true.

### Where it comes from
Latin **plaudere** = *"to clap, applaud"* → *plausibilis*, "deserving applause." It meant *approval-worthy* long before it meant *believable* — the audience claps, that's all.

Same root: **applaud, applause, plaudits** — and **explode**, from *explodere*, "to clap a bad actor **off** the stage."

# **posturing** *(n. / v. (gerund))* — **POS-cher-ing**

### Means
Behaviour or speech adopted **for effect** — striking an attitude to impress, intimidate, or signal a position you don't actually intend to act on.

The word is an accusation of hollowness. Calling a threat *posturing* says the speaker is performing toughness with no intention of following through; calling a principled stand *posturing* says the principle is a costume. It's cooler and more dismissive than *grandstanding* (loud, attention-hungry) and more strategic than *pretending* — a posturer usually wants something out of the pose, typically leverage.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Posture** is a position you *hold on purpose* — posturing is holding a moral position the same way: consciously, for the room, and only until you can relax.

Nobody's straight back at a dinner table is natural, and neither is the outrage of a politician who knows the cameras are on.

### In sentences
1. The ultimatum was dismissed by every analyst as **posturing**, calculated to extract concessions before the deadline rather than to precipitate a war.
2. Ignore him — all that gym-bro chest-thumping is just **posturing**, he'd run a mile from an actual fight.

### In the wild
**\"Political posturing\"** is one of the most-used phrases in news writing — any time a correspondent says a bill, a walkout, or a threat was \"mere posturing,\" they mean it was theatre staged for voters, not governing.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pōnere / positus** = *"to place, to put."* A **posture** is literally how you've placed your body; posturing is placing yourself in a stance for an audience.

Same root, whole GRE family: **position, posit, deposit, compose, impose, juxtapose, repository**.

# **potent** *(adj.)* — **POH-tuhnt**

### Means
Powerful — but specifically **able to produce a strong effect**. A potent drug, a potent argument, a potent symbol.

Where *strong* describes the thing itself and *forceful* describes how it's delivered, **potent** describes what it *does to you*: concentrated capacity, effect out of proportion to size. A small dose, a single sentence, a two-word slogan can all be potent. It's why the noun is **potency** — a measurable strength-per-unit, printed on pill bottles. Slightly formal, and quietly admiring of the power even when the thing wielding it is poison.

⚠️ **potent ≠ portent.** One letter apart, no relation.
- **potent** = powerful, effective
- **portent** *(n.)* = an omen, a sign of what's coming — adj. **portentous**

### Trick to lock it in
> **Impotent** means it can't do a thing — strip the *im-* and *potent* is the thing that does.

The negative is the more familiar word, and it pins the meaning exactly: potency is not size or noise but whether the thing actually works.

### In sentences
1. The prosecution's most **potent** evidence was a single timestamp neither witness could explain away.
2. Careful with that punch — it's way more **potent** than it tastes.

### In the wild
Every medicine box you own is rated for **potency**: 200 mg vs 500 mg, "extra strength," "high-potency" vitamins. Same word, doing its literal job — how much effect per dose.

### Where it comes from
Latin **posse / potens** = *"to be able, being able."* Enormously productive: **potential** (able to become), **omnipotent** (able to do all), **potentate** (a ruler), **possible**, and — via French — **power** itself.

So the core sense is always **ability**, not bulk. Something potent *can do* something.

# **redundant** *(adj.)* — **ri-DUN-duhnt**

### Means
**Surplus to requirement** — and usually surplus *because it repeats something already said or already there*. "A free gift" is redundant; "advance planning" is redundant.

*Superfluous* just means extra; **redundant** points at the duplication that made it extra. That's why editors love the word: it's the charge of saying the same thing twice under two different coats of paint. It carries a faint sting of incompetence — you didn't notice you'd already done it.

Two live side-senses worth knowing: in British usage, a worker **made redundant** is laid off (the job, not the person, is declared unnecessary); in engineering, **redundant** systems are the deliberate spare — a second engine, a backup server — where the duplication is the whole point and the word turns *positive*.

### Trick to lock it in
> Red-**UND**-ant — the same *unda* ("wave") as in **inundate** and **abundant**: it's the one wave too many, slopping over the rim.

Redundant isn't merely extra — it's overflow, the surplus that spills because the cup was already full.

### In sentences
1. The committee's third report was **redundant**, restating in forty pages what the first had settled in four.
2. Do we really need two people on this? Feels a bit **redundant**.

### In the wild
Corporate life: being **"made redundant"** is the British phrase for being laid off — the euphemism works precisely because it says *the role was unnecessary*, not *you were bad at it*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **redundare** = *"to overflow"* — *re-* (back) + **unda** (*wave*). That *unda* is a high-yield GRE root: **inundate** (flood in), **abundant** (waves of it), **undulate** (move in waves), **surround**.

# **sacrosanct** *(adj.)* — **SAK-roh-sankt**

*Also heard SAY-kroh-sankt; the first syllable is the only thing that varies.*

### Means
So sacred, or treated as so important, that it must not be touched, changed, cut, or criticised.

The literal religious sense (an oath, a relic, a shrine) is now the rarer one. In modern prose it's almost always **secular and faintly sardonic**: a budget line, a Sunday lunch, a founder's original vision. Calling something sacrosanct usually says less about the thing than about the people defending it — they've put it beyond argument, and the writer is raising an eyebrow at that. Compare *sacred*, which is sincere, and *inviolable*, which is legal and cold; *sacrosanct* carries a whiff of protected privilege.

### Trick to lock it in
> **You almost never meet this word standing up — you meet it being knocked down: "nothing is sacrosanct."**

The word's natural habitat is the negative, which tells you it means *fenced off from change* — and that someone is about to climb the fence.

### In sentences
1. The committee insisted that the charter's first article was **sacrosanct** and would not be reopened for debate.
2. Cut whatever you like from the script — but the last scene is **sacrosanct**, don't touch it.

### In the wild
Standard boardroom and Westminster boilerplate before bad news: *"In this review, no department is sacrosanct."* Whenever you hear it, cuts are coming.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sacrōsanctus** = *sacer* ("sacred") + *sanctus* ("consecrated") — holy said twice, belt and braces, because it originally described something protected by a binding religious oath.

Both halves are GRE workhorses: *sacer* → **sacred, sacrifice, sacrament, consecrate**; *sanctus* → **saint, sanctuary, sanctity, sanction, sanctimonious**.

# **shrewd** *(adj.)* — **SHROOD**

*One syllable, rhymes with *food* — not "SHROWD".*

### Means
Having **sharp practical judgment** — quick to size up a person, a deal, or a situation and see where the advantage lies.

It's a compliment with a cold edge. **Wise** implies moral depth; **intelligent** implies brainpower; **shrewd** implies you can't be fooled and you know what things are really worth. It's the intelligence of the marketplace, not the library — which is why *shrewd* survives being applied to someone you don't especially like. Push it one step further and you get **cunning** or **crafty**, where the self-interest stops being tasteful.

**shrewd ≠ shrewish.** Same root, opposite compliment: **shrewd** = astute; **shrewish** = nagging and ill-tempered.

### Trick to lock it in
> You can be a **shrewd** investor, negotiator, or judge of character — never a shrewd saint.

The word lives wherever money and motive are, and it praises your eye for advantage rather than your goodness.

### In sentences
1. Her decision to buy the land before the rail line was announced proved **shrewd**: its value tripled within two years.
2. He's not the smartest guy in the room, but he's **shrewd** — he always knows who's bluffing.

### In the wild
Football pundits reach for it constantly: a low-fee signing who turns out brilliant is always called **"a shrewd piece of business."** Same instinct in the business press, where Warren Buffett is a **shrewd** investor rather than a wise one.

### Where it comes from
From Middle English **shrewe** = *"a wicked, malicious person"* (and the small sharp-nosed animal, believed venomous and spiteful). **Shrewd** first meant *"dangerous, wicked,"* then drifted through *"cunning"* to today's *"astute."*

That history is exactly why the word still has a faint chill in it — the praise was originally an insult, and never fully lost the edge.

# **unadorned** *(adj.)* — **un-uh-DORND**

### Means
Plain — carrying **no decoration, ornament, or embellishment**. Used of rooms, dress, prose, and facts.

The word is descriptive, and often quietly approving: *drab* and *bare* say something is lacking, but **unadorned** says nothing unnecessary was added. A cathedral is ornate; a Quaker meeting house is unadorned — and the second is a choice, not a failure. Applied to speech or writing it means stripped of flourish, which on the GRE usually pairs with honesty: the *unadorned truth* is the truth with no flattering trim on it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **un-AD-ORN-ed** — nothing has been **ORN**amented onto it.

The *orn* sitting in the middle of the word is the same *orn* as in *ornament*, so unadorned = ornaments removed, not beauty removed.

### In sentences
1. The chapel was **unadorned** — whitewashed walls, wooden benches, and a single window above the altar.
2. She gave me the **unadorned** version: the deal was dead, and it had been dead for a week.

### In the wild
Journalists and lawyers live on the collocation **"the plain, unadorned facts"** — facts with no spin, no adjectives, no narrative bolted on.

### Where it comes from
**adorn** ← Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **ornāre** (*to equip, to embellish*). Same root gives **ornament**, **ornate**, and **suborn** (literally *to equip secretly* — i.e. to bribe a witness).

So *ornate* and *unadorned* are exact opposites built from the same Latin block.

# **valedictory** *(adj. / n.)* — **val-uh-DIK-tuh-ree**

### Means
**Adj.:** serving as or relating to a **farewell**. **Noun (chiefly US):** the farewell address itself.

This is goodbye with a podium under it. A *final* email is just the last one; a **valedictory** email knows it's the last one, looks back over the whole run, and hands something on. The word carries ceremony, deliberateness, and a low hum of elegy — it attaches to *address, speech, tour, interview, lecture*, almost never to anything casual. Note the implication: someone has chosen to mark the ending rather than simply reach it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Every valedictorian gives the goodbye speech** — and gives it from a stage, in a gown, having rehearsed.

The word you already know supplies both halves: *valedictory* means farewell, and it means farewell done formally.

### In sentences
1. In his **valedictory** address to the court, the retiring justice warned that habits of courtesy, once lost, are not easily rebuilt.
2. She fired off one **valedictory** message to the group chat and left it for good.

### In the wild
The **valedictorian** at graduation — literally the student chosen to deliver the valedictory. The *salutatorian* speaks second and says hello (*salutare*); the valedictorian closes and says goodbye.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vale** (*"farewell"* — literally *"be well,"* from **valēre**, to be strong) + **dīcere** (*"to say"*). Two of the most productive roots on the GRE: *valēre* gives **valid, valour, prevail, convalesce**; *dīcere* gives **verdict, edict, dictate, benediction, malediction**.

# **warranted** *(adj. / v. (past of *warrant*))* — **WOR-uhn-tid**

### Means
**Justified by the circumstances — deserved, called for, proportionate.** (Second sense, from commerce: **guaranteed**, as in goods *warranted* free of defects.)

The word carries a whiff of the courtroom and the audit report: it doesn't say an action was *good*, it says there were **sufficient grounds** for it. Something is warranted when the evidence licenses it, not when you merely feel like doing it — which is why it lives so naturally in the cool, impersonal voice of officials ("no further action is warranted"). Its negative twin does most of the heavy lifting on the GRE: an **unwarranted** assumption, an unwarranted intrusion, unwarranted optimism — the charge each time is *disproportion*, a response bigger than its grounds.

### Trick to lock it in
> **No warrant, no *warranted*** — you need the paper, not just the feeling.

A judge signs a warrant only when the evidence justifies the search, so *warranted* always means backed by grounds rather than by mere impulse.

### In sentences
1. The committee concluded that the outrage, however loud, was not **warranted** by the facts.
2. Two hours late and no text — honestly, a bit of shouting was **warranted**.

### In the wild
The sticker on your laptop or phone: *warranted against defects for two years*. Same word, older sense — the manufacturer is standing behind the claim.

### Where it comes from
Old North French **warant** = *"protector, guarantee."* Northern French kept the **w-**; Parisian French turned the same sound into **gu-** — so English, greedily, borrowed both forms of several words:

**warranty / guarantee**, **warden / guardian**, **ward / guard**, **wile / guile**.

A warrant is the paper that *guarantees* an act is lawful — hence *warranted* = backed, licensed, justified.

# **wistful** *(adj.)* — **WIST-fuhl**

### Means
Quietly sad with longing — usually for something past, distant, or out of reach.

The sadness is **soft and half-pleasant**. *Mournful* is grief, *melancholy* is a settled gloom, *bitter* is a grudge; **wistful** is the gentle ache of remembering something fondly and knowing it's not coming back. It carries **resignation** — the wistful person isn't reaching for the thing, only looking at it. Often used of faces, voices, and glances rather than arguments: a *wistful smile*, a *wistful sigh*, gazing *wistfully* out of a window.

⚠️ **wistful ≠ wishful.** *Wishful* thinking means believing something because you want it to be true — it's self-deception, and slightly foolish. *Wistful* is sad longing with no illusion attached. She was *wistful* about her twenties; he was *wishful* about his chances.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Wist**ful is a **whis**per of a wish — too quiet to act on, too sweet to let go.

The whisper captures the register: not a cry of grief, just a soft private ache you never say out loud.

### In sentences
1. He grew **wistful** at the mention of the old house, as though some part of his life were still standing in its rooms.
2. She got all **wistful** scrolling through photos from that summer, then closed the app and went back to work.

### In the wild
The Beatles' **"In My Life"** is wistfulness in three minutes — *\"There are places I'll remember all my life, though some have changed\"* — fond, backward-looking, and completely free of bitterness.
