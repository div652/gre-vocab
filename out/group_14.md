# Group 14

# **acquiesce** *(v.)* — **ak-wee-ES**

*Stress lands on the last syllable, not the first — never AK-wee-ess.*

### Means
To accept or go along with something **without protest** — to give in quietly.

The word carries the objection you swallowed. Someone who *agrees* is on board; someone who **acquiesces** had reservations and simply stopped fighting. It's consent measured by silence rather than enthusiasm, and it usually implies a power gap — you acquiesce to a boss, a government, a stubborn spouse. Faintly disapproving: it hints at weakness, or at least at surrender.

Note the grammar the GRE likes: you acquiesce **in** or **to** a decision — never "acquiesce a decision."

### Trick to lock it in
> **AcQUIEsce** — you go **quiet**, and the silence counts as a yes.

The word has *quie(t)* sitting in the middle of it, which is exactly the kind of agreement it describes — you stop objecting rather than actually consent.

### In sentences
1. The board **acquiesced in** the chairman's proposal, though several members later admitted they had never believed in it.
2. I didn't want pineapple on it, but everyone else did, so I **acquiesced**.

### In the wild
**Oasis — "Acquiesce"** (1995), the Liam-and-Noel duet B-side that became one of their biggest live songs. The chorus — *because we need each other, we believe in one another* — is two brothers who fight constantly agreeing to stop fighting, which is precisely what the word means.

### Where it comes from
Latin **quiēs / quiēscere** = *"rest, be still"*, plus *ad-* ("to"). To acquiesce is literally to *come to rest* about something — to stop struggling.

That root pays out across the GRE: **quiescent** (dormant, inactive), **quiet**, **tranquil**, and **requiem** (a mass for the *rest* of the dead).

# **adroit** *(adj.)* — **uh-DROYT**

### Means
Skilful and quick — especially **quick in a tricky spot**.

*Dexterous* and *deft* are about the hands; **adroit** has largely migrated to the mind. It's the word for the diplomat who defuses the question, the negotiator who reframes the deal, the witness who never quite gets cornered. Praise, but cool praise: it admires the manoeuvre more than the person, and pushed a little further it shades into *slick*.

**adroit ≠ adept.** *Adept* = deeply skilled through practice (an adept surgeon). *Adroit* = nimble in the moment (an adroit reply). You become adept over years; you are adroit in seconds.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Adroit** rhymes with **avoid** — the adroit answer *avoids* the trap without ever looking like a dodge.

The rhyme carries the nuance: adroitness is escaping a difficulty with such ease that nobody sees the effort.

### In sentences
1. An **adroit** shift of the agenda buried the motion before anyone noticed it had been raised.
2. She's scarily **adroit** at getting out of plans she never wanted to make.

### In the wild
Weezer's 2002 album **Maladroit** — literally "clumsy," the negative of this word. If *maladroit* is the guy tripping over the cable, *adroit* is everyone else.

### Where it comes from
French **à droit** = *"to the right."* Being right-handed was skill; being left-handed was not — and English quietly imported the whole prejudice.

- **adroit** = to the right → skilful
- **maladroit** = badly to the right → clumsy
- **gauche** = French "left" → socially awkward
- **dexterous** (Latin *dexter*, right) vs **sinister** (Latin *sinister*, left)

One bias, six GRE words.

# **amend** *(v.)* — **uh-MEND**

### Means
To **alter something formally in order to correct or improve it** — a law, a motion, a contract, a statement, a tax return. Also of conduct: to *amend your ways*.

The key is that the original **survives**. Amending is patching, not scrapping: a bill that is amended is still that bill; a bill that is repealed is dead. Compare *revise* (neutral, any scale of change) and *rewrite* (start again) — **amend** is narrow, procedural, and usually official. It also carries a faint whiff of prior fault: you amend something because something in it was wrong.

Separately, the fossilised noun phrase **make amends** = to compensate someone for a wrong done.

⚠️ **amend ≠ emend.**
- **amend** = change a document or your behaviour, usually to improve it
- **emend** = correct actual errors in a *text* — a scholar's word for fixing a corrupt manuscript

You amend a constitution. You emend a typo in Chaucer.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A-MEND** — the word has **mend** sitting inside it: you mend the flaw, you don't bin the thing.

Amending keeps the original intact and repairs a fault in it, which is exactly what mending means.

### In sentences
1. The committee voted to **amend** the clause rather than strike it, softening the penalty from dismissal to suspension.
2. Fine, I'll **amend** my statement — the food wasn't terrible, it was just cold.

### In the wild
The **First Amendment**, the **Fifth**, the **Nineteenth** — the US Constitution has never been rewritten, only patched. Every amendment is a fix bolted onto a document that still stands.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ēmendāre** = *"to remove a fault"* — from **ex-** (out) + **menda** (*a fault, a blemish*).

That root gives you **emend** and **emendation**, and — worn down through French — plain **mend** itself. All three are the same idea: take the flaw out.

🚫 **Not related to *amenity*.** That's Latin *amoenus*, "pleasant."

# **animus** *(n.)* — **AN-uh-muhs**

### Means
Ill will — a **settled hostility toward someone that drives how you act toward them**.

Anger is hot and passing; **animus is cold and standing**. It isn't the flare-up, it's the grudge underneath that explains the behaviour, which is why lawyers and journalists reach for it: you allege that a policy, a firing, or a prosecution was *motivated by* animus. It implies a motive, not just a mood — and it points at the hostile party, never the target.

(A second, unrelated sense belongs to Jung: the *animus* is the masculine element of the female psyche, paired with the *anima*. Context makes it obvious which is meant.)

### Trick to lock it in
> **Animus is what *animates* the grudge** — the spirit that moves you against someone.

Same *anim-* as *animation*: animus isn't a passing temper but the driving force behind hostile action.

### In sentences
1. The court found that the ordinance served no purpose but to express **animus** toward a disfavoured group.
2. I've got no **animus** against the guy — I just don't want him running the meeting.

### In the wild
A courtroom and newsroom staple: **"racial animus," "religious animus," "motivated by personal animus."** When a headline says a law was struck down for animus, it means the judges decided the only thing behind it was dislike.

### Where it comes from
Latin **animus** = *mind, spirit, passion* (sibling of **anima**, *breath/soul*). The root pays for itself across the GRE: **unanimous** (of one mind), **magnanimous** (great-souled, generous), **pusillanimous** (tiny-spirited, cowardly), **equanimity** (even mind), **animosity**, **animate**.

Note the drift: the neutral Latin "spirit" narrowed in English to *hostile* spirit specifically.

# **apologist** *(n.)* — **uh-POL-uh-jist**

### Means
Someone who argues **in defence** of a doctrine, regime, institution, or practice — especially one most people find indefensible.

The word almost never describes the person themselves; it's what *other* people call them. Labelling someone "an apologist for the junta" isn't reporting that they defend it — it's saying they're doing dishonest work on its behalf, dressing up the ugly parts as reasonable. Neutral defenders get called *advocates* or *proponents*; you reach for **apologist** when you want the defence to look like a cover-up. The one clean, non-pejorative use is theological: **Christian apologists** are professional defenders of the faith, and wear the title proudly.

⚠️ **apologist ≠ apologetic.**
- **apologetic** = sorry, sheepish, backing down
- **apologist** = sorry for nothing — standing there defending the thing

Same root, opposite posture.

### Trick to lock it in
> **An apologist never apologises.**

He isn't saying "my side was wrong" — he's saying "my side was right, and here's why," which is exactly why the label stings.

### In sentences
1. Later historians dismissed him as an **apologist** for empire, forever finding civilising motives behind the gunboats.
2. Don't get me wrong, I'm no **apologist** for the guy — I just think the timeline doesn't add up.

### In the wild
It's a standard weapon in political journalism and comment threads — "a Kremlin **apologist**", "an **apologist** for Big Tobacco". Notice it's always aimed *at* someone, never claimed.

### Where it comes from
Greek **apologia** = *"a speech in your own defence"* (*apo-* "away" + *logos* "speech") — courtroom language, not contrition. Plato's **Apology** is Socrates on trial defending himself and conceding nothing.

The "sorry" sense is the late arrival; **apologist** simply kept the original job. Same *logos* family: *monologue, eulogy, prologue, dialogue*.

# **astringent** *(adj. / n.)* — **uh-STRIN-juhnt**

### Means
**Literally:** causing tissue to contract — drying, puckering, tightening. A tart, mouth-puckering taste (strong tea, unripe fruit, tannic red wine) is astringent; so is the toner that tightens your pores.

**Figuratively:** sharp, severe, unsparing — usually of criticism, humour, or prose.

The figurative sense keeps the physical one alive: astringent commentary is *dry and tightening*, not hot. **Caustic** burns, **vitriolic** attacks, **acerbic** is sour wit — **astringent** is bracing. There's a hint of the medicinal about it: it stings, but it's doing you good. Applied to a writer's style, it's often praise.

⚠️ **astringent ≠ stringent.** Same root, different jobs: **stringent** describes *rules* — tight, strict, hard to satisfy (*stringent safety standards*). **astringent** describes a *taste, a substance, or a tone*. Stringent rules; astringent wit.

### Trick to lock it in
> **a-STRING-ent** — a **string** pulled tight: it puckers your mouth, and it tightens rather than burns.

The hidden *string* is the actual root, and "draws tight" is exactly the difference between astringent criticism and the scalding kind.

### In sentences
1. Her reviews were **astringent** but never cruel — she cut a novel down in three sentences and left the author grateful.
2. That tea's been brewing for twenty minutes; it's gone all **astringent** and it's drying out my mouth.

### In the wild
Walk down any skincare aisle: bottles of witch hazel labelled **astringent**, sold on the promise of tightening pores. That's the literal sense sitting on a shelf.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **stringere** (*to draw tight, bind*) — literally "binding up."

*Stringere* is a workhorse root: **stringent**, **strict**, **constrict**, **restrict**, **constrain**, and the **boa constrictor**. All of them squeeze.

# **collaborate** *(v.)* — **kuh-LAB-uh-rayt**

### Means
To **work jointly with others** on a shared piece of work — a paper, an album, a project.

The second sense is the one the GRE cares about: to **cooperate traitorously with an enemy** occupying your country. Same word, no separate spelling. *The mayor collaborated* in peacetime is praise; *the mayor collaborated* under occupation is an accusation of treason — and **collaborator** carries that stain even more strongly than the verb. Context alone decides which sense you're reading, so watch for war, occupation, or resistance nearby.

⚠️ **collaborate ≠ corroborate.**
- **collaborate** = *work* with someone
- **corroborate** = *confirm* someone's account with evidence

A witness who corroborates your alibi supports it; a witness who collaborates with you helped commit the crime.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Co-labour.** Every collaborator labours alongside someone — the only question is *whose side*.

The word itself is morally blank, which is exactly why it can mean both a co-author and a traitor.

### In sentences
1. Historians still argue over how many officials actively **collaborated** with the occupying regime, and how many merely complied.
2. We **collaborated** on the deck for about three hours and honestly she did most of it.

### In the wild
Every "feat." credit on Spotify is a **collab** — the shortening is so ordinary that the war sense catches people completely off guard on the exam.

### Where it comes from
Latin **com-** (*together*) + **laborare** (*to work*), from **labor** (*toil*).

That root spreads wide: **labour**, **laboratory** (a workroom), **elaborate** (worked *out* in detail), **belabour** (to keep working at something past the point of use).

### Also sits in
- **Meaning cluster** · [Plotting together](groups.md#meaning__plot-together) — with *collude, conspire, abet, intrigue*
- **Lookalike** · [collaborate / corroborate / elaborate](groups.md#lookalike__collaborate-corroborate) — with *corroborate, elaborate*

# **competent** *(adj.)* — **KOM-puh-tuhnt**

### Means
Having the skill or qualification to do something **properly** — able to meet the standard required.

Watch the ceiling built into it. *Competent* certifies that nothing went wrong; it never says anything went well. Call a surgeon **skilled** and you're praising her; call her **competent** and you've only confirmed she shouldn't be struck off. Reviewers weaponise this — "a competent thriller" means dull but functional.

It also has a hard legal sense with no faint praise in it at all: **legally fit or authorised**. A defendant *competent to stand trial*, a witness *competent to testify*, a *court of competent jurisdiction* — there it means qualified, full stop.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Competent** = fit to **compete** — in the race, but not winning it.

The word literally hides *compete*, which is exactly the level it grants you: qualified to be on the field, never described as the best on it.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal ruled that she was **competent** to manage her own financial affairs.
2. The food was **competent** — nothing was wrong with it, and nothing was worth ordering twice.

### In the wild
Anyone who's had a performance review knows this one: "competent" in the box means *meets expectations* — the polite ceiling right below "strong" and just above trouble.

### Where it comes from
Latin **competere** = *"to come together, be suitable"* — **com-** (together) + **petere** (*to seek, aim at, rush toward*).

**petere** is a GRE workhorse: **petition** (seek formally), **appetite** (seek toward), **impetus** (rush into), **perpetual** (seeking on and on), **centripetal** (seeking the centre) — and **compete**, the same word as *competent*, which drifted from "coincide, be fit" to "rival."

# **correlate** *(v. / n.)* — **KOR-uh-layt**

*As a noun, often flattened to KOR-uh-luht.*

### Means
**Verb:** to show, or to have, a mutual relationship — two things that rise and fall together. *Rainfall correlates with crop yield.*
**Noun:** either of two things so linked — *fluency is a correlate of early reading*.

The whole force of the word is that it stops short of cause. To say two things are related is vague; to say they *correlate* is a measured, almost statistical claim: they track each other, and that is all you are promising. It's the vocabulary of researchers and analysts hedging honestly — strong on pattern, silent on why.

⚠️ **correlate ≠ corroborate.**
- **correlate** = two things *move together*
- **corroborate** = evidence *backs up* a claim

Your alibi is corroborated by a witness, not correlated with one.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Co-relate: they relate *together* — side by side, neither one steering.**

The *cor-* is just "co-", so the word means partnership between two facts, never one pushing the other.

### In sentences
1. Longitudinal data **correlated** parental income with university completion, though the authors declined to call one the cause of the other.
2. Weirdly, my worst gym days **correlate** with the days I skip breakfast.

### In the wild
**"Correlation does not imply causation"** — the most-quoted line in all of statistics, and the reason journalists get scolded every time a study says coffee drinkers live longer.

### Where it comes from
Latin **com-** *("together")* + **relatus** *("carried back")*. Before an *r*, *com-* assimilates to **cor-** — which is the same prefix hiding in **correspond**, **corroborate**, **correct**, and **corrupt**. Spot a *cor-* and you're usually looking at "together" or "thoroughly."

# **deride** *(v.)* — **dih-RYDE**

### Means
To mock or ridicule — to laugh at something as beneath serious consideration.

The contempt is what does the work. *Tease* is affectionate, *mock* can be playful, but to **deride** is to dismiss from above: the target isn't just funny, it's worthless. It's the verb of choice for critics and columnists — proposals, films, and beliefs get derided, usually in public and usually by someone who thinks the matter is already settled. Noun form **derision** carries the same sneer.

⚠️ **deride ≠ decry.**
- **deride** = *laugh at* it as ridiculous
- **decry** = *denounce* it as wrong or harmful

You deride a silly idea; you decry an injustice. Derision is amused; decrying is deadly serious.

### Trick to lock it in
> **deride** rhymes with **snide** — the laugh comes with a sneer.

Both words pair humour with contempt, which is exactly what separates deriding from merely teasing.

### In sentences
1. Contemporary reviewers **derided** the novel as sentimental trash; a century later it is required reading.
2. Everyone **derided** his plan to drive there in one go — then he did it in eleven hours.

### In the wild
**"Widely derided"** is a standing phrase in tech and film journalism — the fixed label for a product launch or sequel the internet decided to laugh at.

### Where it comes from
Latin **rīdēre** = *"to laugh"*, plus **de-** (*down, at*) — to laugh *down at* someone.

That root is worth banking: **ridiculous** (laughable), **ridicule**, **derision**, and the GRE favourite **risible** (*RIZ-uh-buhl*, laughable, absurd).

# **dictate** *(v. / n.)* — **DIK-tayt**

*Americans stress the first syllable for both verb and noun; many British speakers say dik-TAYT for the verb, DIK-tayt for the noun.*

### Means
**Verb:** (1) to speak words aloud so someone else can write or type them down; (2) to lay down terms, rules or conditions with authority — to impose rather than propose.
**Noun:** an order or governing principle handed down (*the dictates of conscience, the dictates of fashion*).

The thread joining both senses is **one-way flow**: the person dictating supplies the words, the other party supplies only the pen. Sense (2) is the GRE sense and it carries no consultation — you *negotiate* terms but you *dictate* them, and calling someone's manner dictatorial is never a compliment. There is a third, cooler use where no person is in charge at all: *budget dictates the timeline*, *symptoms dictate treatment* — here it just means "determines, leaves no choice," and the tone is flatly neutral.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dictate** is what a **dictator** does — he talks, you take it down, nobody argues.

The two words are literally the same root, and the dictator image supplies the missing half of the definition: not just *speaking*, but speaking that cannot be answered back.

### In sentences
1. The victors did not negotiate at Versailles; they **dictated** the terms and handed Germany a pen.
2. Look, I'm not trying to **dictate** how you spend your weekend — I just need the car back by six.

### In the wild
Microsoft Word and Google Docs both put a literal **Dictate** button in the toolbar — tap the mic, talk, and the machine plays scribe. That's sense (1) sitting in your ribbon every day.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dicere, dictum** = *"to say"* → the frequentative **dictāre**, *"to say over and over, to prescribe."*

This root is one of the best returns on investment in the whole GRE list: **verdict** (truly-said), **contradict** (speak against), **edict** (spoken out by authority), **predict**, **indict**, **benediction**, **diction**, **dictionary**, **dictum**, **dictator**.

# **discreet** *(adj.)* — **dih-SKREET**

*Identical in sound to *discrete* — the difference is only on the page.*

### Means
Careful about what you say and do, so as not to attract attention or give away something private; tactful.

It's a **compliment about judgment**, not a description of shyness. A *secretive* person seems to be hiding something; a **discreet** one is trusted *with* something. The word almost always implies a third party being protected — the client, the friend, the affair, the diagnosis. Note also the pairing with *inquiry* and *glance*: a **discreet** enquiry is one made so lightly that nobody realises a question was asked.

⚠️ **discreet ≠ discrete.**
- **discreet** = *tactful, unobtrusive*
- **discrete** = *separate, individually distinct* (discrete data, discrete units)

### Trick to lock it in
> **In *discrete*, the T pushes the two E's apart — separated. In *discreet*, the E's stay together, keeping it between them.**

The spelling itself acts out the meaning: split E's for split things, huddled E's for a kept confidence.

### In sentences
1. The family's solicitor made **discreet** enquiries, and no word of the settlement ever reached the press.
2. Can you be **discreet** about this? I haven't told my manager I'm interviewing.

### In the wild
Online checkouts promising **\"discreet packaging\"** — a plain brown box with no branding, protecting you from the neighbours rather than from thieves. That's the word in one image: not hidden, just not announced.

### Where it comes from
Latin **discernere** = *"to separate, to distinguish"* (*dis-* apart + *cernere* to sift). Its participle *discretus* gave English **one** word that later split into two spellings — the sifting sense stayed with *discrete*, the sense of a person with good judgment went to *discreet*.

The root pays off elsewhere: **discern**, **discernment**, **concern**, **certain**, **secret** (*se-* + *cernere*, "set apart").

# **divorced** *(adj. (also v., past tense))* — **dih-VORST**

### Means
Beyond the marriage sense, the one the GRE actually tests: **separated from, cut off from, having no working connection with.**

The word carries an accusation. *Unrelated* is a neutral fact; **divorced** implies the two things were once joined, and *ought* still to be — so the split is a failure. A theory **divorced from** the evidence isn't merely different from the evidence; it has drifted free of the thing that was supposed to anchor it. That's why it lands as criticism: the speaker is saying *you've lost your tether*.

Almost always passive and almost always followed by **from**.

### Trick to lock it in
> On the GRE, **divorced** is nearly always followed by **from** — and never by a spouse.

The moment you see "divorced from," swap in "cut loose from what should be holding it down."

### In sentences
1. His grand economic model, elegant on paper, was wholly **divorced** from the conditions of any actual market.
2. Mate, that budget is **divorced** from reality — rent alone eats half of it.

### In the wild
**"Divorced from reality"** is the standard put-down in political and business commentary — a forecast, a manifesto, a boss's timeline. Hear that phrase and you're hearing this exact sense.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dīvertere** = *"to turn aside"* — **di-** (apart) + **vertere** (to turn). A divorce is literally two people turning away from each other.

**vertere** is one of the highest-yield GRE roots: **divert, convert, invert, subvert, avert, revert, versatile, vertigo, adverse, controversy** — every one of them a turning.

### Also sits in
- **Meaning cluster** · [Driven Apart: Enmity, Drift, and Rupture](groups.md#meaning__driven-apart-from-others) — with *antagonize, alienate, estranged*
- **Root family** · [Root: vertere / versus (to turn) — the prefix decides the direction](groups.md#root__vertere) — with *adverse, aversion, incontrovertible, versatile*

# **elitist** *(adj. / n.)* — **ih-LEE-tist**

*The base word is ih-LEET, never "EE-lite" — the stress sits on the second syllable throughout.*

### Means
Holding — or acting on — the belief that a chosen few (by birth, money, brains, or taste) are superior and should get the power, the say, and the good seats. As a noun, the person who thinks that way.

This is a word people throw *at* others. Nobody describes themselves as an elitist; it's a charge of contempt for ordinary people, aimed at attitude rather than achievement. A surgeon who is genuinely the best in the country isn't elitist — she becomes elitist the moment she implies that people like her should decide things for people like you. Register is political and journalistic, and it stings: it accuses you of snobbery *and* of wanting that snobbery institutionalised.

⚠️ **elite ≠ elitist.**
- **elite** = the top tier — usually a compliment (*elite athlete, elite unit*)
- **elitist** = the ideology of the top tier — always an attack

Being elite is a fact about you. Being elitist is a fact about how you treat everyone else.

### Trick to lock it in
> An elit**ist** in**sist**s that the elite should be in charge.

The *-ist* is what does the damage — it upgrades a description of a group into a creed someone is accused of holding, exactly as in *racist* or *sexist*.

### In sentences
1. The reforms were denounced as **elitist**: free tuition, but only for those who had already survived a school system the poor could not afford.
2. Charging forty quid for a burger and calling it "curated" is peak **elitist** nonsense.

### In the wild
Anton Ego in **Ratatouille** — the gaunt food critic whose whole posture says *taste belongs to people like me*. The film's answer to him is its slogan: **"Anyone can cook."** That's the anti-elitist argument in three words.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ēligere** = *"to pick out, choose"* (*ē-* out + *legere* to gather, select). The **elite** are literally *the chosen ones*.

Same root, same idea of choosing: **elect, election, eligible, select, elegant** (originally "choosy about taste" — which is where the snobbery seeps in).

- **Opposites** · [Levelling vs. Ranking: Equal, Fair, and Above](groups.md#antonym__everyone-equal-vs-a-chosen-few-above) — with *egalitarian, equitable, echelon, chauvinistic, patronize*

# **exacting** *(adj.)* — **ig-ZAK-ting**

### Means
Making severe demands on your care, effort, or skill — either of a **person** (hard to satisfy) or of a **task** (requiring precision and stamina).

Unlike *picky*, *fussy* or *nitpicking*, **exacting** grants the demander legitimacy: the standard is high because the work genuinely requires it, not because someone is being difficult. An exacting editor is respected and exhausting at once. And the word costs you something — exacting work drains you, which *demanding* only half implies.

⚠️ **exacting ≠ exact.** *Exact* = precise, correct. *Exacting* = **demanding**. An exacting standard isn't a precisely-specified one; it's one that's hard to meet.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hear the **verb** *exact* — "to exact a toll." An exacting job exacts a toll from you.

The buried word isn't the adjective *exact* (precise) but the verb *exact* (to demand and extract), which is exactly what such a boss or task does to you.

### In sentences
1. Restoring the fresco was **exacting** work: a square inch a day, under magnification, with solvents mixed fresh each morning.
2. She's **exacting**, sure — but nothing she asks for is unreasonable, and the final cut is always better for it.

### In the wild
"Built to **exacting** standards" is the fixed phrase of premium advertising — Swiss watches, single malts, hi-fi speakers. If a product page brags about tolerances, this word is nearby.

### Where it comes from
Latin **exigere** = *"to drive out, demand, require"* (*ex-* out + *agere* to drive). The demanding sense is the original; "precise" came later, from the idea of a payment weighed out to the last coin.

Same root: **exact** (v., to demand and take), **exigent** (pressing, urgent), **exigency** (an urgent demand), and via *agere* — **agent, agenda, agile, act**.

- **Meaning cluster** · [Strict and demanding](groups.md#meaning__strict-and-demanding) — with *stringent, rigor, onerous, draconian*

# **flummoxed** *(adj. (past participle of *flummox*, v.))* — **FLUM-uhkst**

### Means
Completely **baffled** — so confused you have no idea what to do next.

The word is light on its feet. *Bewildered* has a hint of distress, *perplexed* is cool and intellectual, but **flummoxed** is faintly comic: you're stumped, and everyone can see it on your face. It's the confusion of a man staring at flat-pack instructions, not of a man facing tragedy. Informal and slightly jokey, so it takes the sting out — people say *"I'm utterly flummoxed"* about themselves far more readily than they'd admit to being *lost*.

⚠️ **flummoxed ≠ flustered.** *Flummoxed* = you can't **understand**; *flustered* = you can't **cope** — rattled, flapping, but perfectly clear on what's going on.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Flumm-OX** — you stand there blinking like an ox.

The **ox** hiding at the end catches the flavour of the word: not quiet inner puzzlement but big, dumb, visible bafflement.

### In sentences
1. The committee was **flummoxed** by a clause that appeared to forbid the very thing the statute was written to permit.
2. Ask him anything about football and he'll talk for an hour — ask him to work the dishwasher and he's completely **flummoxed**.

# **fruitful** *(adj.)* — **FROOT-fuhl**

### Means
Producing good results — **productive of something worth having**.

The word judges the **outcome, not the effort**. A gruelling day of work that produced nothing is not fruitful; a ten-minute conversation that solved everything is. Its opposite, *fruitless*, is the standard GRE pairing, and the contrast is exactly that: effort spent versus something to show for it.

Mildly warm and slightly formal — it lives in business and diplomatic speak (*a fruitful exchange of views*, *a fruitful collaboration*), where it can quietly mean "nobody stormed out." The literal sense (soil, trees, bearing fruit) still exists but is now mostly poetic.

### Trick to lock it in
> You can only call it **fruitful** if you can name the **fruit** you walked away with.

The test isn't how hard the work was — it's whether you're carrying something when you leave.

### In sentences
1. Three years of correspondence between the two laboratories proved unexpectedly **fruitful**, yielding a vaccine neither could have developed alone.
2. Long meeting, but actually **fruitful** for once — we came out with a decision.

### In the wild
**"Be fruitful, and multiply"** — Genesis 1:28, one of the most quoted lines in English, and the original literal sense: bear fruit, produce offspring.

# **inborn** *(adj.)* — **IN-born**

### Means
Present in a person **from birth** — a quality, instinct or tendency that came with them rather than one they picked up.

Compare the neighbours: **innate** is the abstract, philosophical word (*innate rights, innate ideas*); **ingrained** is the exact opposite in origin — worked in by years of habit; **inborn** is the plain, human one, used of instincts and gifts in a real person. It also stays neutral: you can have an inborn talent, an inborn stubbornness, or an inborn defect.

⚠️ **inborn ≠ inbred.** *Inbred* now reads first as *bred from close relatives* — insulting, and about ancestry. *Inborn* is about the individual: what they arrived with.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Inborn came in with the baby; ingrained got rubbed in later.**

Both start *in-*, but one is delivered at birth and the other is ground in by repetition — the split is *given* versus *acquired*.

### In sentences
1. Chomsky's claim was that the scaffolding of grammar is **inborn**, not assembled from imitation alone.
2. She's had an **inborn** sense of rhythm since she was three — nobody sat her down and taught her that.

### In the wild
Every newborn in a hospital gets a heel-prick blood test screening for **inborn errors of metabolism** — the standard medical phrase for conditions a baby is literally born carrying.

# **polymath** *(n.)* — **POL-ee-math**

### Means
A person of **wide-ranging expertise** — someone who has genuine command of many unrelated fields (physics *and* poetry *and* medicine), not just an interest in them.

The word is close to reverent. You don't call yourself one; other people award it to you, usually in a profile or an obituary. The depth is the whole point: a **dilettante** samples many subjects and masters none, a **polymath** masters them. Reach for it only when the breadth is real, or the praise curdles into flattery.

### Trick to lock it in
> **poly** (many) + **math** — but not many *maths*: many **learnings**.

The *-math* is the Greek word for *learning*, so a polymath has learned much across many subjects — mathematics just happens to be another child of the same root.

### In sentences
1. A restless **polymath**, she published on protein folding, medieval finance, and the acoustics of cathedrals — and was taken seriously in all three.
2. He's read one book on architecture and now he's calling himself a **polymath**.

### In the wild
**Leonardo da Vinci** is the word's default illustration — painter, anatomist, military engineer — and \"the ultimate polymath\" is close to a fixed phrase whenever he comes up.

### Where it comes from
Greek **polumathēs** = *"having learned much"* — **poly-** (many) + **manthanein** (to learn).

That second half is the useful one: **mathēma** meant *"a thing learned"*, which is why **mathematics** is literally "the learned subject." And **poly-** pays out everywhere on the GRE: **polyglot** (many tongues), **polygamy** (many marriages), **polymer** (many parts), **polygon** (many angles).

# **reticent** *(adj.)* — **RET-uh-suhnt**

### Means
Unwilling to speak freely — holding back what you could say, especially about your own feelings, plans, or affairs. (Noun: **reticence**.)

Shyness is temperament; reticence is a **decision**. A shy person can't get the words out; a reticent one has them and keeps them. It's also narrower than *taciturn*, which means habitually short on words in general — reticence usually attaches to a **subject**: reticent *about* his finances, *about* her first marriage. Which way it reads depends on the scene: on a diplomat it's discretion, on a witness it's evasion.

**reticent ≠ reluctant.** *Reticent* is about **speech** (won't say); *reluctant* is about **action** (won't do). "Reticent to sign the contract" is the mistake — that's *reluctant*. If nobody's talking, it's reticent.

### Trick to lock it in
> reti**CENT** — he won't give you his two cents.

He has the opinion; he just isn't handing it over — which is holding back, not being tongue-tied.

### In sentences
1. Ordinarily expansive about his research, he grew notably **reticent** whenever the funding source came up.
2. Mum's weirdly **reticent** about how she met Dad — thirty years and we've never got the story.

### Where it comes from
Latin **reticēre** = *"to keep silent"* — *re-* (back) + **tacēre** (to be silent). That root pays off across the exam: **tacit** (understood without being said), **taciturn** (habitually silent), **tacitly**.

- **Intensity scale** · [Sparing with words](groups.md#intensity__sparing-with-words) — with *laconic, taciturn*

- **Opposites** · [Talks too much vs. says little](groups.md#antonym__talks-too-much-vs-says-little) — with *garrulous, loquacious, voluble, expatiate, ramble, laconic, taciturn*

# **stringent** *(adj.)* — **STRIN-juhnt**

### Means
Strict, exacting, allowing no slack — of rules, standards, conditions, or requirements that must be met precisely.

Compare the neighbours: **harsh** and **draconian** describe punishment and imply cruelty; **stringent** describes the *tightness of the bar*, not the pain of failing it. That makes it the rare strictness-word that can be a compliment — *stringent safety testing*, *stringent peer review* — because the tightness is the point. It attaches to systems, not personalities: standards, criteria, controls, budgets are stringent; a strict grandmother is not.

⚠️ **stringent ≠ strident.**
- **stringent** = *tight standards* — a rule that admits no slack
- **strident** = *loud and grating* — a voice or tone that grates

A stringent policy is quietly unbending; a strident critic is impossible to ignore.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Stringent** starts with **string** — pulled taut, zero slack.

The word measures how tightly the rule is strung, not how badly it hurts to break it.

### In sentences
1. Admission to the programme is governed by **stringent** criteria: two published papers, a research proposal, and a reading knowledge of German.
2. They've got **stringent** rules about laptops in the exam hall — even the case has to stay outside.

### In the wild
Standard news vocabulary you've already absorbed: *stringent emissions standards*, *stringent lockdown restrictions*, GDPR routinely called the world's most **stringent** privacy law. Note that in every one of those, stringent is doing regulatory work, not moral scolding.

### Where it comes from
Latin **stringere** = *"to draw tight, bind."* The whole family is about tightening: **strict** (bound), **restrict** and **constrain** (bound back), **stricture** (a narrowing), **astringent** (a lotion that tightens the skin).

Learn the root once and four GRE words come with it.

# **subservient** *(adj.)* — **sub-SER-vee-uhnt**

### Means
**1.** Excessively willing to obey — submitting to someone else's will past the point of self-respect. **2.** (of a thing) subordinate, serving a larger purpose: *form subservient to function*.

Obedience is what the word describes; contempt is what it adds. A soldier who follows orders is **obedient**; a soldier who has stopped having opinions is **subservient**. **Deferential** respects authority and keeps its own judgment — subservient hands the judgment over. It's almost always an outsider's verdict: nobody calls themselves subservient, and the word quietly indicts the person above as much as the person below.

⚠️ **subservient ≠ subversive.**
- **subservient** = props the power up (*sub- + serve*)
- **subversive** = tears the power down (*sub- + vertere*, "to turn")

Four shared letters, opposite politics.

### Trick to lock it in
> **SERVE** is sitting in the middle, and **sub-** puts you *under* while you do it.

Not just serving — serving from beneath, which is why the word stings rather than compliments.

### In sentences
1. The court chronicler's history is worthless: every page is **subservient** to the king who paid for it.
2. He wasn't being polite, he was being **subservient** — he agreed with her twice before she'd finished the sentence.

### In the wild
Burger King's 2004 viral ad **Subservient Chicken** — a man in a chicken suit who did whatever you typed into the box. The whole joke was the word: it obeys anything, and there's something faintly degrading about watching it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** (*under*) + **servīre** (*to serve*), from **servus** = *slave*. That root runs through **servant, service, servile, servitude, deserve** — and **serf**.

Pair it with the *vertere* (*to turn*) family — **subvert, invert, divert, avert** — and the two sub- words stop blurring for good.

# **surreptitious** *(adj.)* — **sur-uhp-TISH-uhs**

*The spelling shows "rept", but nobody says sur-REP-tish-us — the stress lands on TISH.*

### Means
Done **in secret and by stealth**, specifically because open doing would be forbidden, punished, or embarrassing.

Scale is what separates it from its neighbours. **Covert** and **clandestine** are big secrecy — operations, cells, affairs, things planned to stay hidden. **Surreptitious** is small and quick: the glance at your phone under the table, the extra file copied to a USB stick, the bite taken from someone else's plate. It also carries a whiff of guilt that *discreet* doesn't — being discreet is tactful, being surreptitious means you'd have been stopped if anyone had looked up.

⚠️ **surreptitious ≠ serendipitous.** Similar length, similar *-itous* ending, opposite spirit: *surreptitious* is deliberately sneaky, *serendipitous* is a lucky accident.

### Trick to lock it in
> Sur-**REPT**-itious hides a **reptile** — it moves without a sound, and you only notice afterwards.

The word is about the small silent slither, not the grand conspiracy — the thing that gets away with it while you're looking elsewhere.

### In sentences
1. The clerk had for years made **surreptitious** alterations to the ledger, each too small to notice and too regular to be accident.
2. He took a **surreptitious** look at the answers while the invigilator was turned around.

### In the wild
**"Surreptitious recording"** is the fixed legal phrase for taping a conversation without telling the other person — the exact thing wiretap and one-party-consent laws are written about.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** ("under") + **rapere** ("to seize, snatch") → *surripere*, "to snatch from underneath."

**rapere** is a workhorse root: **rapid** (snatched away fast), **rapture** (seized by emotion), **rapacious** (grabbing greedily), **ravish**, and **usurp** (*usu* + *rapere* — to seize by use).

# **tantalizing** *(adj.)* — **TAN-tuh-lye-zing**

*British spelling: tantalising.*

### Means
Temptingly attractive — but held **just out of reach**, so the desire is never satisfied.

The withholding *is* the word. Anything delicious can be *tempting*; only something you can almost-but-not-quite have is **tantalizing**. It carries a built-in gap: the smell through a closed kitchen door, a clue that stops one step short of the answer, a result that's "tantalizingly close." Take away the frustration and you've just got *alluring*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tantalizing = a taunt with a prize attached.**

Hear the *taunt* in **tant-** — the thing dangles the reward and then pulls it back.

### In sentences
1. The dig yielded a **tantalizing** fragment of the inscription, breaking off precisely where the king's name would have appeared.
2. We were **tantalizingly** close to the playoffs — one point, one game, and it slipped away.

### In the wild
Food advertising runs on it: **"tantalize your taste buds"** is a decades-old ad cliché — the smell reaching you before the meal does.

### Where it comes from
From **Tantalus**, the Greek king punished in Hades by being stood in water up to his chin beneath a fruit tree: the water drained away whenever he stooped to drink, the branches lifted whenever he reached to eat. Eternal near-miss.

That myth also named a metal — **tantalum**, so called because it sits in acid and refuses to absorb any of it, like Tantalus in his pool.

# **tantamount** *(adj.)* — **TAN-tuh-mownt**

### Means
Equivalent **in effect or seriousness** to something else — even if it isn't that thing literally. Always followed by **to**: *tantamount to treason, tantamount to a confession.*

It isn't a maths word. Nobody says two halves are tantamount to a whole; you say it when an act *technically isn't* the grave thing but **counts as** it. That gap is the whole point — the speaker is stripping away the technicality and naming what the act really amounts to. So it tends to arrive attached to something damning, in the voice of a prosecutor, an editorial, or a diplomat: refusing to answer was tantamount to admitting it.

⚠️ **tantamount ≠ paramount.** Same *-amount* ending, unrelated jobs: **tantamount to X** = *equal in effect to X*; **paramount** = *supreme, above all others* (*safety is paramount*). Paramount needs no "to."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tant-amount** — it *amounts to* the same thing, and what it amounts to is usually bad.

The word **amount** is sitting inside it, and "amounts to" is exactly how you'd say it in plain speech — *walking out now amounts to quitting.*

### In sentences
1. The court held that the company's silence, given its duty to disclose, was **tantamount** to fraud.
2. Asking him to babysit your cat is **tantamount** to giving the cat away — you're never seeing it again.

### In the wild
A newsroom and courtroom staple — *"tantamount to a declaration of war," "tantamount to an admission of guilt."* Notice it never introduces good news; hearing the collocation teaches you the tone.

### Where it comes from
Anglo-French **tant amunter** = *"to amount to as much"* — *tant* (so much) + *amounter*, from Latin **ad montem**, *"to the mountain,"* i.e. to go up to a total.

That *mount* root pays out widely: **amount, surmount, insurmountable, paramount, mountain, mount**.

# **torpor** *(n.)* — **TOR-per**

### Means
A state of **sluggish inactivity** — physical, mental, or both: no energy, no motion, no appetite for either. Also the biological sense: the dormant, slowed-metabolism state an animal drops into to survive the cold.

Laziness is a choice and *sloth* is a sin; **torpor is a condition**. It carries no moral scolding — the person (or economy, or bureaucracy) isn't refusing to act, they've gone inert. It's a heavy, clinical, slightly literary word, and it stretches easily from a body to an institution: *the torpor of a provincial town*, *decades of administrative torpor*.

⚠️ **torpor ≠ stupor.** They rhyme, so the GRE will happily swap them.
- **torpor** = *sluggish, energyless inactivity* (a hibernating animal)
- **stupor** = *dazed near-unconsciousness* (a drunk man on the floor)

Torpor is slow. Stupor is senseless.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Torpor = torque-poor.** The engine is intact; there's just no force left to turn it.

Torpor isn't refusal to move but the absence of power to move, which is why it fits a hibernating animal and a stalled economy equally well.

### In sentences
1. Reform proposals arrived every spring and died in the same committee, victims of a **torpor** no minister cared to disturb.
2. Two helpings of biryani and the whole table sank into afternoon **torpor**.

### In the wild
Nature documentaries use it in its exact technical sense: a hummingbird burns so much fuel that it drops into **torpor** every night — heart rate crashing, body cooling — just to survive until morning.

### Where it comes from
Latin **torpēre** = *"to be numb, stiff, motionless."* Gives **torpid** (sluggish) and — the one that sticks — **torpedo**, originally the electric ray, a fish that *numbs* what it touches. The weapon was named after the fish.

# **trenchant** *(adj.)* — **TREN-chuhnt**

### Means
Of speech, writing, or analysis: **sharp, vigorous and penetrating** — it cuts straight to the essential point.

The word praises the *mind* behind the remark, not the damage it does. **Scathing** is about hostility, **acerbic** about sourness of tone; **trenchant** is about accuracy — the criticism lands because it's *right*, and you admire the person who made it even if you're the one bleeding. Hence its natural home: reviews, essays, courtroom argument, political commentary. Almost never used for praise or for small talk — a trenchant compliment sounds wrong; a trenchant critique sounds inevitable.

⚠️ **trenchant ≠ truculent.** Both sound aggressive and start the same way, but *truculent* means **belligerent and scowling** — a bad temper, not a good argument. A trenchant critic is worth reading; a truculent one is just spoiling for a fight.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Trenchant** has **trench** in it — it doesn't bruise the argument, it cuts a channel clean through it.

A trench is a deep, deliberate cut, which is exactly the difference between a merely angry remark and a trenchant one.

### In sentences
1. Her dissent was the most **trenchant** paragraph in the entire judgment: four sentences that dismantled the majority's reasoning.
2. He doesn't say much in meetings, but when he does it's usually pretty **trenchant** — one question and the whole plan falls apart.

### In the wild
Read the back of almost any serious non-fiction paperback and you'll find it: *"a trenchant analysis of…"* is standard blurb-speak for a book that cuts deep, and reviewers reach for it constantly.

### Where it comes from
French **trancher** = *"to cut"* (ultimately Latin **truncare**). Same root gives you **trench** (a cut in the ground), **retrench** (cut back spending), **truncate** (cut short), and **trenchant** — literally *cutting*.

# **umbrage** *(n.)* — **UM-brij**

*Not "um-BRAHZH" — the ending is plain, like *porridge*.*

### Means
Offence, resentment, or wounded pride — the feeling of having been slighted.

It survives almost entirely in one phrase: **to take umbrage at** something. That phrasing carries a faint eyebrow-raise. Where *offended* is neutral about whether the offence was warranted, *umbrage* quietly suggests the person is a touch thin-skinned and rather enjoys the grievance — it's the word for a colonel bristling at a junior officer's tone, not for someone hurt by real cruelty. Formal, slightly arch, and often used at the expense of the person taking it.

### Trick to lock it in
> You never *feel* umbrage — you **take** it, the way you take a stand.

The verb gives the game away: this offence is picked up and held on purpose, which is why the word always hints at self-importance.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador took **umbrage** at being seated below the trade delegation and left before the toast.
2. Don't take **umbrage**, but that shirt is doing you no favours.

### In the wild
**Dolores Umbridge** in *Harry Potter* — Rowling built the name on *umbrage*, and the character is one long fit of bristling, punitive offence-taking.

### Where it comes from
Latin **umbra** = *"shadow."* Umbrage first meant literal shade, then "a shadow cast over someone" — a slight — and finally the resentment itself.

The root is everywhere: **umbrella** (a little shade), **penumbra** (half-shadow), **adumbrate** (to sketch in shadow, to foreshadow), **sombre** (*sub umbra*, under shadow).

# **versatile** *(adj.)* — **VER-suh-tuhl**

*British: VER-suh-tyle, rhyming with "tile."*

### Means
Able to turn your hand to many different things — and be **good** at all of them. Also used of objects: a tool or material with many uses.

The praise is about **range plus competence**, which is what separates it from its neighbours. *Adaptable* means you cope when conditions change; *flexible* means you bend to others' demands; **versatile** means you already have several skills sitting ready. It's a compliment nobody objects to — an actor who does comedy and Chekhov, a fabric that works in summer and winter.

⚠️ **versatile ≠ volatile.** Same rhythm, opposite temperament: **versatile** = usefully many-sided; **volatile** = liable to explode or swing wildly.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hiding in it is **versa** — as in *vice versa*: it turns the other way, and any other way you need.

The versatile one keeps turning to face a new task and lands each one well.

### In sentences
1. A **versatile** performer, she moved from stand-up to Shakespeare without any visible change of gear.
2. Get the black one — it's the most **versatile** jacket you own; it goes with everything.

### In the wild
Sports commentary lives on this word: the cricketing **all-rounder** who bats, bowls and keeps, or the footballer who can fill in at three positions, is always called "the most versatile player in the squad."

### Where it comes from
Latin **vertere** = *"to turn"* → *versatilis*, "capable of turning." The same root turns up everywhere on the GRE: **convert, invert, divert, avert, revert, adverse, vertigo**, and **anniversary** (the year turning round again).

# **wayward** *(adj.)* — **WAY-werd**

### Means
Hard to control or predict; drifting off the proper course — morally, or just physically.

A *rebellious* teenager is fighting you; a **wayward** one has simply wandered somewhere you can't follow. The word carries a faint pastoral disapproval, the tone of a parent or a preacher, and often a little pity or fondness mixed in — you don't call someone wayward unless you'd rather they came back. It also stretches to things with a mind of their own: a wayward strand of hair, a wayward pass, a wayward missile.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-ward** always names a direction: *homeward* heads home, *wayward* heads **away**.

That's literal history — *wayward* is *awayward* with the *a* worn off, so the word means "turned away," not "fighting back."

### In sentences
1. The report described a **wayward** institution: funds unaccounted for, minutes unrecorded, trustees who had not met in two years.
2. He was a **wayward** kid — never nasty, just never once where he said he'd be.

### In the wild
Kansas, **"Carry On Wayward Son"** — the wandering son told to carry on, rest coming when he's done. The whole song is the word.

### Where it comes from
Middle English **awayward** = *"turned away"* — the initial *a-* simply fell off. So the "way" you see is really **away**, and **-ward** is the same direction-suffix as in *toward, backward, homeward, wind­ward*.
