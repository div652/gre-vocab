# **abeyance** *(n.)* — **uh-BAY-uhns**

*Not "uh-BEE-ance" — the middle syllable is *bay*.*

### Means
A state of **temporary suspension** — something real and still-existing whose operation has been parked, pending a decision, an event, or an heir.

*Cancelled* kills a thing; *dormant* is just natural quiet. **Abeyance is deliberate and conditional**: someone with authority pressed pause, and something specific is expected to restart it. The word is stiffly formal — law, parliaments, boardrooms, HR letters — and lives almost entirely inside the frozen phrases **"in abeyance"** and **"held in abeyance."** You rarely meet it in the wild as a bare noun.

⚠️ **abeyance ≠ obeisance** (oh-BAY-sunss) — a deep bow or gesture of submission. Nearly identical sound, unrelated sense: one *pauses* a matter, the other *bends the knee*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Held in a-BAY-ance = held at bay** — not driven off, just kept waiting at a distance.

The sound hook carries the nuance: nothing has been resolved or destroyed, it's simply been stopped where it stands until someone lets it come forward.

### In sentences
1. The peerage fell into **abeyance** in 1712, there being two equal claimants and no way to choose between them.
2. They're keeping the whole rebrand **in abeyance** until we see what the lawyers say.

### In the wild
Standard courtroom and HR wording: a ruling **held in abeyance pending appeal**, a disciplinary case **held in abeyance pending investigation** — if you ever see the word at work, it will be in that exact phrase.

### Where it comes from
Old French **abeance**, "a gaping, an expectation," from **baer** = *to gape, to stand open*. A medieval estate in abeyance was thought of as **sitting open-mouthed, waiting** for its rightful owner to appear.

Same *baer* gives English **bay** in *bay window* — an opening in a wall.

# **abstract** *(adj. / n. / v.)* — **AB-strakt**

*As a verb, the stress jumps to the end: ab-STRAKT (like REcord / reCORD).*

### Means
**Adj.:** existing as an idea rather than a physical thing; general and theoretical rather than tied to particular cases.
**Noun:** a **summary** — the condensed version at the head of a paper.
**Verb (ab-STRAKT):** to **draw out**, extract, or reduce something to its essentials.

All three senses are one motion: pulling the idea *off* the object. Note that **abstract is not vague** — mathematics is maximally abstract and perfectly exact; vagueness is imprecision, abstraction is distance from the concrete. Neutral in itself, but in an argument "that's too abstract" is a complaint: the point has floated free of any real case.

**abstract ≠ abstruse.** *Abstract* = removed from the concrete (an abstract principle). *Abstruse* = obscure, hard to follow (an abstruse proof). An abstract idea can be crystal clear; an abstruse one, by definition, isn't.

### Trick to lock it in
> Stress it like a verb and it **moves**: to ab-STRACT is to pull something out — leave it **AB**-stract and it just sits there as an idea.

Front-stress gives you the pulled-*away* result (the summary, the theory), end-stress gives you the act of pulling.

### In sentences
1. Rawls's principle stays **abstract** until someone applies it to an actual tax code.
2. Can you stop being so **abstract** for a second and just tell me what I'm doing on Monday?

### In the wild
Every paper you open on arXiv starts with the **Abstract** — the whole argument dragged out and set down in one paragraph.

### Where it comes from
Latin **abs-** (*away*) + **trahere, tractus** (*to drag, pull*) — literally "pulled away."

That root is a GRE goldmine: **extract, retract, distract, subtract, protract, contract, attract, traction, tractor**. All of them are something being pulled — out, back, apart, or along.

# **affront** *(n. / v.)* — **uh-FRUNT**

### Means
**Noun:** an open insult — a deliberate act of disrespect aimed at someone's dignity.
**Verb:** to insult someone openly, to their face.

Scale and formality separate this from *insult*. An insult can be petty and private; an affront is **public and treated as an offence against standing** — your honour, your office, your rights. It's also the word English reaches for when the injured party isn't a person at all: *an affront to democracy, to decency, to human dignity*. And note the grammar the GRE loves: an affront is always **to** something.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Af-front** = delivered **up front**, to your face, in front of everyone.

The word literally has *front* in it, which is exactly what makes an affront worse than a private insult — it's done openly, where the humiliation lands.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador walked out, calling the remark an **affront** to his country and to the treaty that bound them.
2. Don't put ketchup on that — the chef would take it as a personal **affront**.

### In the wild
Open any newspaper: *"an affront to democracy," "an affront to human dignity."* It's the standard register for outrage in print — which is why the *to* is welded on.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad frontem** = *"to the forehead"* — via Old French *afronter*, **to strike someone in the face**. The insult is aimed at the front of your head.

Same root gives you **front, confront, frontier, frontispiece** — and, best of all, **effrontery**: shameless nerve, from *ex-* + *frons*, "without a face" — no blush left to give you away.

# **agitate** *(v.)* — **AJ-i-tayt**

### Means
To **stir something up so it can't settle** — three related uses:

1. **Physical:** shake or churn. *Agitate the solution before decanting.*
2. **Emotional:** disturb, unsettle. An **agitated** person is jittery, unable to sit still — visibly rattled, not calmly angry.
3. **Political:** campaign loudly and persistently. Always with *for* or *against* — *agitate for reform*.

The third sense is the GRE's favourite and the one people miss. It carries no insult on its own, but the noun **agitator** almost always does: those in power call you an agitator, you call yourself an activist. Note the difference in force from *provoke* — provoking is a single jab that gets a reaction; agitating is sustained churn that denies anyone the quiet of the status quo.

⚠️ **agitate ≠ aggravate.** *Agitate* = stir up, set in motion. *Aggravate* = make an already-bad thing worse (*aggravate an injury*). Running aggravated her knee; the diagnosis left her agitated.

### Trick to lock it in
> The **agitator** in a washing machine is the spindle that never lets the water sit still.

Same word, same job in every sense — the thing that keeps water, nerves, or a nation from settling.

### In sentences
1. For four decades she **agitated** for prison reform, outliving three governments that ignored her.
2. He was **agitated** all morning — checking his phone, pacing, snapping at people for nothing.

### In the wild
Frederick Douglass's advice to a young man asking what to do with his life: **"Agitate, agitate, agitate."** A century later, segregationists dismissed civil-rights organisers as *outside agitators* — the exact word, weaponised.

### Where it comes from
Latin **agere** = *"to drive, to do"* → **agitāre**, its frequentative: *to drive again and again, keep in motion.*

That root is everywhere on the GRE: **agent** (one who does), **agenda** (things to be done), **agile**, **act**, **exigent**, **navigate**, **prodigal**. The frequentative *-it-* is the reusable bit — it means *repeatedly*.

# **august** *(adj.)* — **aw-GUST**

*The adjective stresses the second syllable — aw-GUST. The month is AW-guhst. Same spelling, different beat.*

### Means
Majestic and **dignified in a way that commands reverence** — impressive because of age, rank, or moral weight.

Not just grand. *Grand* can be a hotel lobby; *august* needs standing. It attaches to people and institutions that have earned deference — an august jurist, an august assembly, the august halls of a university — and it carries a whiff of the old and established. Because it's so formal, it turns easily ironic: call a rowdy committee "this august body" and everyone hears the sneer.

### Trick to lock it in
> **aw**-GUST begins with **awe** — and that's exactly what it asks of you.

The month is flat AW-guhst, but push the stress off the front and the leftover "aw" is the awe an august figure inspires.

### In sentences
1. The verdict was delivered by an **august** bench of nine judges, none under seventy.
2. Three of us in a rented office — hardly the **august** institution the website promises.

### In the wild
**Augustus** Caesar wasn't born with that name — the Senate awarded it to Octavian as a title meaning "the venerable one," and he then renamed a month after himself. The emperor, the month, and the adjective are the same word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **augustus** = *"consecrated, venerable,"* from **augere** = *"to increase."* The august person is the one who has been *magnified* — raised above ordinary rank.

That root pays out widely: **augment** (increase), **auction** (bidding goes up), **auxiliary** (added forces), **author** (one who makes grow).

# **burnish** *(v. / n.)* — **BER-nish**

### Means
Literally: to **polish metal by rubbing** until it gleams. Figuratively — and this is how the GRE uses it — to **improve the lustre of something abstract**: a reputation, an image, credentials, a legacy.

The key limit is that you can only burnish what already exists. *Build* a reputation, *burnish* an existing one — it's buffing a surface, not manufacturing substance. That's why the figurative use carries a faint whiff of PR: when a newspaper says a politician is burnishing his image, the implication is cosmetic work on the shine rather than change underneath.

⚠️ **burnish vs. tarnish** — near-rhymes, exact opposites.
- **burnish** = rub *bright*
- **tarnish** = go *dull*, stain

A scandal tarnishes the record the memoir was written to burnish.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tar** blackens; **burn** brightens.

The rhyme locks the pair as opposites, and the "rubbing" idea reminds you that burnishing works on a surface that's already there — it polishes a reputation, it doesn't build one.

### In sentences
1. Rather than answer the allegations, the firm spent a fortune on advertising designed to **burnish** an image it had spent a decade dulling.
2. He's only doing the charity 5K to **burnish** his UCAS form, and everyone knows it.

### In the wild
Political journalism runs on this verb — "burnishing his foreign-policy credentials," "a trip meant to burnish her legacy" are newsroom boilerplate before any election.

### Where it comes from
Old French **burnir / brunir** = *"to make brown, to make bright"*, from Germanic **brūn** (*brown*) — the colour of polished bronze catching the light. Same root gives you **brown** and **brunette**.

# **coy** *(adj.)* — **KOY**

### Means
Two linked senses:

**1.** Pretending to be shy or modest — usually to charm or tease. **2.** Deliberately vague; unwilling to give details.

The key is that it's **shyness performed, not felt**. A shy person can't help it; a coy person is *choosing* it, and wants you to notice the withholding. That's why "coy" carries a faint eye-roll: a coy smile is inviting you to press further, and a CEO who is **coy about the numbers** isn't bashful — she's managing you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Coy** is hiding inside **decoy** — and a decoy works by *pretending* to be what it isn't.

Coyness is bait: the withholding is the lure.

### In sentences
1. The minister remained **coy** about the date of the election, deflecting every question with a smile.
2. Stop being **coy** — just tell me who you're texting.

### In the wild
Andrew Marvell's **"To His Coy Mistress"** — the most famous seduction poem in English, and the whole thing is a man arguing that her *coyness* (holding back, teasing him along) is a waste of the little time they have.

### Where it comes from
Old French **coi** = *"quiet, still,"* from Latin **quietus**. English *coy* first meant simply "quiet, modest" — then slid into *affected* modesty, which is where it sits today.

Same root gives you **quiet, quiescent, acquiesce, requiem** — all the "at rest" words.

# **deprecate** *(v.)* — **DEP-ruh-kayt**

*Stress on the first syllable — not "de-PRECK-ate".*

### Means
To **express disapproval of** something — to say, in measured terms, that it is undesirable or ill-advised. In tech, the specialised sense: to **mark as obsolete**, still working but officially discouraged and headed for removal.

The disapproval is cool, not hot. You *denounce* a scandal, you *condemn* a crime — you **deprecate** a tendency, a habit, a fashion. It's the disapproval of a committee or a leading article: dry, authoritative, no raised voice. That mildness is why *self-deprecating* means playing yourself down with a joke rather than actually attacking yourself.

⚠️ **deprecate ≠ depreciate.**
- **deprecate** = disapprove of, discourage
- **depreciate** = *fall in value* (a car depreciates the moment you drive it off the lot)

Note the extra **i** — and that *self-deprecating* is the standard form.

### Trick to lock it in
> Depre**ci**ate drops the **price**; depre**c**ate drops the **praise**.

One is about worth on a balance sheet, the other about approval withheld — politely.

### In sentences
1. The editorial **deprecated** the government's habit of legislating by press release, though it stopped short of calling for resignations.
2. Careful — that function's been **deprecated** since version 3; it still runs, but it'll vanish next release.

### In the wild
Every programmer meets this word as a yellow warning: **`DeprecationWarning`** — your code works today, but the maintainers officially disapprove and are removing it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **de-** ("away") + **precari** ("to pray") — to *pray something away*, to avert it by entreaty. It started as a plea, not an attack, which is exactly why the modern word still disapproves so quietly.

Same root: **imprecation** (a curse — praying *at* someone), **precarious** (held only by someone else's goodwill, so liable to collapse), and plain **pray**.

# **disdain** *(n. / v.)* — **dis-DAYN**

### Means
**Noun:** contempt for something you regard as unworthy of you.
**Verb:** to treat with that contempt — or to **refuse** to do something because it's beneath you (*he disdained to answer*).

Temperature is what sets it apart. **Scorn** is loud and **contempt** is harsh, but disdain is *cold* — a raised eyebrow, a refusal to engage, the look you give something you've decided not to take seriously. It always implies the disdainer is standing higher up, which is why it says as much about their vanity as about the thing dismissed.

### Trick to lock it in
> **dis-** + **deign**: you will not *deign* to stoop to it.

The word *deign* is literally sitting inside *disdain*, and it carries the looking-down-from-above posture that separates disdain from ordinary dislike.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador received the apology with visible **disdain**, and **disdained** to answer it in writing.
2. He looked at the microwaved lasagna with total **disdain** and ordered a pizza.

### In the wild
Mr Darcy at the Meryton ball in *Pride and Prejudice* — refusing to dance, dismissing Elizabeth as \"tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me.\" That's disdain in one line: not anger, just a man who won't lower himself.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dignus** = *"worthy."* Add *dis-* (not) and you get *"to deem unworthy"* — which is exactly what the word still does.

The root pays off widely: **dignity**, **dignitary**, **deign** (to consider something worthy of your effort), **indignant** (angry at being treated as unworthy), **indignity**, and **condign** (a punishment *worthy* of the crime).

# **disperse** *(v.)* — **dis-PERS**

### Means
To **scatter in every direction** — a crowd, seeds, smoke, light — so that what was concentrated becomes thin and spread out.

The key is that the *gathering itself dissolves*. **Distribute** hands things out on purpose, to chosen recipients; **disperse** just breaks the mass apart and lets the pieces go wherever. That's why the word does double duty: physically neutral in science (pollen disperses on the wind, a prism disperses light), but faintly coercive in the news, where crowds are ordered to disperse by someone with a baton.

⚠️ **disperse ≠ disburse.**
- **disperse** = *scatter* — the crowd, the fog
- **disburse** = *pay out* — the funds

**Disburse** has a **purse** in it. Money comes out of a purse; everything else disperses.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Disperse** leaves things **sparse** — same root, one letter apart.

You haven't dispersed a crowd by moving it somewhere else; you've dispersed it when it's thinned out to nothing.

### In sentences
1. Windborne spores **disperse** across hundreds of miles, which is why the blight reached the coast before anyone had named it.
2. One whiff of tear gas and the whole square **dispersed** in about ninety seconds.

### In the wild
The standard police loudhailer line at any protest — *"This is an unlawful assembly. You are ordered to **disperse**."* You've heard it in news footage and in half the riot scenes ever filmed.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dis-** (*apart*) + **spargere** (*to scatter, sprinkle*).

That root is worth banking: **sparse** (scattered thinly), **intersperse** (scatter among), and the GRE favourite **aspersion** — literally *sprinkling onto*, hence to **cast aspersions**, flicking dirt at someone's name.

# **distend** *(v.)* — **dih-STEND**

### Means
To **swell or bulge outward from pressure inside** — a stomach after a huge meal, a balloon, a corpse, a vein under a tourniquet.

The direction matters: things *expand* in any manner, but they **distend** because something within is pushing the walls out. It's the vocabulary of bodies and containers, and it almost always carries discomfort — a distended belly is bloated, strained, wrong. You'd never call a happy stretch distension.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ex**tend reaches outward; **dis**tend bulges outward — same *tend* (stretch), but the pressure is inside.

Both words are the root *tendere*, "to stretch," so the prefix does all the work: extending is voluntary reach, distending is being stretched from within.

### In sentences
1. Weeks of famine had **distended** the children's bellies — the cruel paradox of hunger that looks like fullness.
2. Two plates of biryani in, I was flat on the sofa with a **distended** stomach, regretting every bite.

### In the wild
Standard clinical shorthand: every physical exam note in a hospital records the abdomen as **"soft, non-distended."** Doctors write the word dozens of times a day.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dis-** (*apart*) + **tendere** (*to stretch*) — stretched apart from within.

*Tendere* is a high-yield GRE root: **extend, tension, tendon, tent** (stretched fabric), **contend, portend, tendentious**.

# **endemic** *(adj.)* — **en-DEM-ik**

### Means
**1.** (of a disease or condition) constantly present in a particular place or group. **2.** (of a plant or animal) native to and found *only* in one region — *lemurs are endemic to Madagascar*.

The key idea is **permanent residence, not arrival**. An epidemic surges and passes; something endemic has settled in and become part of the furniture. That's why the figurative use bites so hard: say *corruption is endemic to the ministry* and you're not reporting an outbreak, you're saying it's structural, baked in, and nobody expects it to leave. Biology's use is the neutral one; applied to human institutions, it's close to an indictment.

⚠️ **endemic ≠ epidemic.**
- **endemic** = *en-* (in) — always **in** the population, steady background level
- **epidemic** = *epi-* (upon) — sweeping **onto** the population, a sudden spike

Malaria is endemic to the tropics; cholera broke out as an epidemic.

### Trick to lock it in
> **End**emic things never **end** — they're the resident, not the visitor.

The false syllable *end* is a lie about the etymology but a perfect grip on the meaning: an endemic problem is the one that's always been there and shows no sign of stopping.

### In sentences
1. Chronic underfunding is **endemic** to the region's public hospitals, less a crisis than a permanent condition.
2. Honestly, missed deadlines are just **endemic** here — nobody even flinches anymore.

### In the wild
The whole 2022 debate over whether COVID had "gone **endemic**" — meaning not *gone*, but downgraded from emergency to permanent background resident.

### Where it comes from
Greek **dēmos** = *"the people."* *En-dēmos* = "in the people."

One root, a whole cluster: **democracy** (rule by the people), **demographic** (measuring the people), **demagogue** (one who leads — and inflames — the people), **epidemic** (upon the people), **pandemic** (all the people).

# **enmity** *(n.)* — **EN-mi-tee**

### Means
Deep, settled hostility between people or groups — the **condition of being enemies**.

Animosity flares up and dies down; enmity just *sits there*. It names a standing relationship rather than a feeling of the moment, and it doesn't have to be actively expressed — two families can be at enmity for eighty years without a word exchanged. It's usually mutual, often inherited, and the register is formal and slightly grand: clans, nations, houses, and factions have enmity; your annoying coworker gets animosity.

⚠️ **enmity ≠ amity.** *Amity* = friendly relations (the Treaty of **Amity** and Commerce). Same root, opposite sign — the *en-* is a buried "not."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Enmity** = **enemy** + **-ity** — the *state* of being enemies, not a flash of temper.

*-ity* builds lasting conditions (purity, sanity, gravity), so enmity is hostility that has hardened into a permanent arrangement.

### In sentences
1. Centuries of enmity between the two houses had outlived every person who could remember how it began.
2. There's no enmity there — we just haven't spoken since the wedding.

### In the wild
Genesis 3:15, King James Bible — God to the serpent: *"And I will put **enmity** between thee and the woman."* Not a quarrel; a permanent state of war written into the world.

### Where it comes from
Latin **amīcus** = *"friend"* (from *amāre*, to love). Add the negating **in-** and you get *inimīcus*, "not-friend" → Old French *enemi* → **enemy**, and its abstract noun **enmity**.

One root, both poles: **amicable, amiable, amity** on the friendly side; **enemy, enmity, inimical** on the hostile side. *Inimical* ("harmful, hostile to") is the GRE's favourite member of that family.

# **gauche** *(adj.)* — **GOHSH**

*One syllable, rhyming with posh — never "GAW-chee" or "gowch".*

### Means
Socially clumsy — lacking the tact, polish, or ease that a situation calls for. Said of people, remarks, and gestures: a **gauche** question, a **gauche** gift.

Note who's speaking. *Awkward* is sympathetic and usually inward — you feel awkward. *Gauche* is a verdict delivered from above: the speaker is claiming to know the rules and pronouncing that you don't. It implies the blunder came from not being brought up right, not from nerves. So the word says as much about the person using it as the person described — there's a whiff of the snob in anyone who reaches for it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Gauche** near-rhymes with **posh** — it's what posh people call everyone else.

The rhyme fixes the sound (GOHSH) and the sneer built into the word at the same time.

### In sentences
1. Asking a novelist how much money she makes is considered **gauche**, however sincerely one means it.
2. He gave the bride a gift receipt with the present — a bit **gauche**, but at least she could swap it.

### Where it comes from
French **gauche** = simply *"left"* — as in the left hand, the clumsy one. Left-handedness has been slandered in half of Europe's vocabulary, and it gives you a matched set worth memorising together:

- **gauche** ← French *left* = clumsy
- **sinister** ← Latin *sinister*, *left* = ominous, evil
- **adroit** ← French *à droit*, *to the right* = skilful, deft
- **dexterous** ← Latin *dexter*, *right* = nimble

Learn one, get four. (In French politics *la gauche* is still just the left wing — no insult intended there.)

# **hysterical** *(adj.)* — **hi-STER-i-kuhl**

### Means
**1.** Overcome by emotion past the point of self-control — sobbing, screaming, panicking, unable to be reasoned with. **2.** *(informal)* Extremely funny.

Sense 1 is rarely neutral when aimed at a person: to call someone hysterical is to say their feeling has voided their judgement, which is why it lands as a put-down rather than a description. A crowd can be hysterical (**mass hysteria**); a warning you want taken seriously should never be called it. Sense 2 is pure spoken register — *"that's hysterical"* is a compliment, and no one uses it in writing that matters.

⚠️ **hysterical ≠ histrionic.**
- **hysterical** = genuinely out of control
- **histrionic** = *performing* the loss of control for an audience

The hysterical person can't stop; the histrionic one could stop the moment nobody was watching.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Hysterical = the brakes have failed** — grief or laughter, either way you can't stop.

The two wildly different senses share one idea, loss of control, which is why the same word covers a weeping widow and a very good joke.

### In sentences
1. By the third hour of the siege the crowd had turned **hysterical**, and the officers' instructions went entirely unheard.
2. Don't tell him that story again — he went **hysterical** the first time and nearly fell off his chair.

### In the wild
*Ghostbusters* — Bill Murray warning the mayor of the apocalypse: **"Human sacrifice, dogs and cats living together — mass hysteria!"** Panic on a civic scale, played for laughs.

### Where it comes from
Greek **hystera** = *"womb."* Ancient and then Victorian doctors blamed uncontrollable emotion in women on a wandering or diseased womb and called the condition **hysteria** — the same root that gives you **hysterectomy** (surgical removal of the womb). The sexist diagnosis is dead; the sneer survives in the word.

🚫 **Not *history*** (Greek *historia*, "inquiry") and **not *histrionic*** (Latin *histrio*, "actor"). Three lookalike stems, three unrelated origins.

# **impudent** *(adj.)* — **IM-pyoo-duhnt**

### Means
Boldly disrespectful — cheeky, saucy, lacking the deference the situation demands.

The offence isn't cruelty, it's **presumption**: an impudent person talks to a superior as though rank didn't exist, and rather enjoys it. *Rude* is about manners; *insolent* is colder and more contemptuous; *impertinent* is prissier and fussier — *impudent* comes with a grin. It's also a word spoken **downwards**: teachers, magistrates and outraged fathers call people impudent, and it slightly dates whoever uses it.

**impudent ≠ imprudent** — one letter, opposite failing.
- **impudent** = cheeky, shameless
- **imprudent** = unwise, rash

An impudent remark insults your boss; an imprudent one costs you money.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hiding at the front is an **imp** — a small grinning devil, not a dangerous one.

The imp isn't threatening you, it's simply refusing to be afraid of you, and that cheerful lack of fear is exactly what makes impudence sting.

### In sentences
1. The court reporter noted the defendant's **impudent** smirk as the sentence was read out.
2. Don't give me that **impudent** look — I asked you a straight question.

### In the wild
It's the stock rebuke of period drama — the outraged "you impudent boy!" — which tells you its register: this is what authority splutters when a subordinate answers back.

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** ("not") + **pudēns** ("ashamed"), from **pudēre**, *to feel shame*. Literally: **shameless**.

🚫 **Not *imprudent*.** That's *prudēns*, "foreseeing" (from *prōvidēre*) — the root behind **prudent, providence, provide**. Different Latin word, one letter apart in English.

# **inchoate** *(adj.)* — **in-KOH-it**

*The ch is a hard k, and the ending is "-it," not "-ate": never "IN-choh-ate." (in-KOH-ayt is an accepted variant.)*

### Means
Just begun and therefore **still without shape** — rudimentary, unformed, not yet coherent enough to be described properly.

The key is *formless*, not merely *unfinished*. A half-built house is incomplete; an **inchoate** plan is barely a plan at all — a mood, an impulse, a first stirring. That's why it pairs so naturally with feelings and movements: *inchoate rage*, *inchoate longing*, *an inchoate protest movement* — things real enough to feel but too shapeless to name. Distinctly formal and literary; in speech it can sound like a mild put-down of someone's thinking.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Inch**oate — it has moved about an **inch**.

The word opens with "inch" on the page: the thing has crept an inch from the starting line, so it isn't half-built, it's barely begun and still shapeless.

### In sentences
1. The committee's proposals were still **inchoate** — a scatter of grievances that had not yet hardened into demands.
2. I've got an **inchoate** idea for the app, but don't ask me how it works yet.

### In the wild
Lawyers use it constantly: **inchoate offences** — attempt, conspiracy, solicitation — are crimes that got started but never reached the finished act.

### Where it comes from
Latin **incohāre**, *"to begin"* — originally to hitch the ox to the plough (*cohum*, the yoke strap), i.e. work only just getting underway.

🚫 **The *in-* is not the negative *in-*.** There is no word *choate*, and nothing here connects to **chaos** — the shapelessness comes from *newly begun*, not from a "not" prefix.

# **penchant** *(n.)* — **PEN-chunt**

*Some speakers keep the French: PAHN-shahn. Either passes.*

### Means
A strong and habitual **liking or inclination** for something — a taste you keep coming back to.

A penchant is a *known* fondness, the thing people would list about you: a penchant for loud jackets, for gossip, for arriving late. It's lighter and more indulgent than its neighbours — a **propensity** sounds clinical (a propensity for violence), a **proclivity** hints at vice, but a penchant is usually charming or wryly noted, and often attaches to something a bit frivolous. Nobody has a penchant for breathing; you have a penchant for good champagne.

### Trick to lock it in
> **p-ENCHANT** — a penchant is a small **enchantment**.

The word you're charmed by sits inside it, and that's the right strength: a penchant delights you, it doesn't compel you.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador was tolerated despite a **penchant** for saying, at dinner, precisely what everyone else had agreed not to.
2. He's a decent flatmate, but he's got this **penchant** for reorganising my kitchen when I'm out.

### In the wild
Sports commentary runs on it — *\"he has a penchant for the dramatic\"* is what they say about the player who always scores in the 90th minute.

### Where it comes from
French **pencher** = *"to lean"* (← Latin **pendere**, to hang/weigh). A penchant is literally which way you tilt.

That unlocks the whole cluster, because they're all leaning words: **inclination** (*clinare*, to lean), **proclivity** (*clivus*, a slope), **propensity** (*pendere* again — hanging forward). Four GRE words, one gesture.

# **quandary** *(n.)* — **KWON-duh-ree**

*An older second pronunciation, kwon-DAIR-ee, still turns up; KWON-duh-ree is standard.*

### Means
A state of **uncertainty over what to do**, especially when you must choose and every option costs you something.

The word describes the *person*, not the problem — you are **in** a quandary, paralysed, turning it over. A **conundrum** is a puzzle that has an answer waiting to be found; a **quandary** has answers, and you dislike all of them. Milder and more everyday than *predicament* (which implies you're already in trouble), it carries a faint air of hand-wringing — the speaker is stuck, and slightly embarrassed to be stuck.

### Trick to lock it in
> Qu-**AND**-ary: on one hand… **and** on the other.

The **and** sitting in the middle of the word is the two competing options you're wedged between.

### In sentences
1. Faced with a dying patient and a family divided over treatment, the surgeon found herself in a genuine **quandary**.
2. I'm in a bit of a **quandary** — both parties are the same night and I've said yes to both.

# **quarantine** *(n. / v.)* — **KWOR-uhn-teen**

### Means
**Noun:** a period of enforced isolation for people, animals, or things that **may** carry disease. **Verb:** to place under such isolation.

The key is *suspicion, not confirmation*. You **isolate** the sick; you **quarantine** the exposed — the ones who might be carrying something and are held apart until the doubt clears. It's precautionary and time-bounded, and it's imposed by an authority (port, hospital, government), not chosen. The metaphorical uses keep that flavour: a quarantined file, a quarantined dataset, a politician quarantined by his own party — set aside as a possible contaminant, pending judgement.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Quarantine is a waiting room, not a ward.**

You're held there because you *might* be infectious, whereas isolation is for those already known to be.

### In sentences
1. Livestock imported from the mainland were **quarantined** for six weeks before being admitted to the herd.
2. They **quarantined** the new laptop off the network until IT could confirm it was clean.

### In the wild
Your antivirus doesn't delete a suspicious file — it drops it in **Quarantine**, sealed off but not condemned, exactly the sense of the word: guilty until cleared.

### Where it comes from
Italian **quarantina giorni** = *"about forty days."* Plague-era Venice made arriving ships sit at anchor for forty days before anyone could land. The number is baked into the word: Italian *quaranta* ← Latin **quadrāgintā**, "forty," from *quattuor*, "four" — the same four that gives you *quart, quarter, quadrant, quartet*.

# **quash** *(v.)* — **KWOSH**

*Rhymes with wash, not with cash.*

### Means
To **put down completely** — a rebellion, a rumour, a hope — or, in law, to **annul** something official: a subpoena, an indictment, a conviction.

Two things mark it. First, **authority**: the quashing is done from above, by a court, a government, a general, a boss. Second, **finality in one stroke** — you *suppress* dissent over months, you *quell* a crowd by calming it down, but you quash a thing and it is simply gone. A rumour that keeps resurfacing was never quashed.

⚠️ **quash ≠ quell.**
- **quash** = *cancel it outright* — one decisive act, often on paper (a court quashes a verdict)
- **quell** = *calm it down* — soothe or subdue something agitated (quell a riot, quell your fears)

You can quell panic. You cannot quash it — there's no order to sign.

### Trick to lock it in
> **quash** joins **smash, crash, bash, squash** — the **-ash** verbs are all one blow, and then it's over.

Nothing in that family happens gradually, which is exactly the difference between quashing something and merely suppressing it.

### In sentences
1. The Court of Appeal **quashed** the conviction after the forensic evidence was shown to be worthless.
2. He **quashed** the rumour with one tweet and nobody brought it up again.

### In the wild
**"Motion to quash"** — the standard courtroom move to have a subpoena or indictment thrown out, and a stock line in every legal drama from *Law & Order* to *Suits*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **quatere / quassus** = *"to shake, to shatter"*, via the intensive **quassāre**. Shake something hard enough and it breaks — hence the crushing sense.

That root pays out widely: **concussion** (shaken together), **percussion** (struck through), **discuss** (originally to shake apart, i.e. examine), **squash**, even **rescue** (*re-ex-quassāre*, to shake free).

The legal sense arrived separately, from Latin **cassāre** *"to annul"* (from *cassus*, "empty, void") — the two words fused in English, which is why one verb both crushes revolts and voids indictments.

# **quibble** *(v. / n.)* — **KWIB-uhl**

### Means
**Verb:** to argue about a small, unimportant detail — usually one that doesn't affect the main point.
**Noun:** the petty objection itself.

The word's whole force is in the *size* of the complaint. To **argue** is neutral; to **object** is serious; to **quibble** is to fuss over wording, a decimal place, a comma — while the real question goes untouched. It also runs in a polite direction that its cousins don't: "my only **quibble** is the ending" is a soft way of saying *I basically liked it*. So the label can be an accusation of pedantry, or a signal that you're conceding almost everything.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Quibble** rhymes with **nibble** — a quibbler nibbles at the edges instead of biting into the argument.

Nibbling is small by definition, which is exactly what makes a quibble petty rather than a real objection.

### In sentences
1. The defence spent an hour **quibbling** over the precise minute of the phone call, as though the recording itself were not in evidence.
2. Great meal — my only **quibble** is that the chips were cold.

### In the wild
Shops and insurers advertise a **"no-quibble refund"** or *no-quibble guarantee* — bring it back and we won't pick a petty argument to avoid paying you.

# **ravage** *(v. / n. (usu. pl. *ravages*))* — **RAV-ij**

*Rhymes with *savage* — not the French-sounding "ruh-VAHZH".*

### Means
**Verb:** to destroy or lay waste to something violently and thoroughly — a region, a body, a population.
**Noun (usually plural):** the damage left behind — *the ravages of time, war, disease*.

The word implies **destruction that sweeps and keeps coming**, not a single blow. A bomb *destroys* a building; a plague, a fire, an army, or forty hard years **ravages** a whole country or a whole face. The agent is typically an impersonal force, and the scale is total: whatever it passes over is left ruined.

⚠️ **ravage ≠ ravish.**
- **ravage** = *devastate, lay waste*
- **ravish** = *seize and carry off* — or, bizarrely, *to fill with delight* (hence **ravishing**, stunningly beautiful)

Same Latin root, opposite emotional temperature: a ravaged coastline, a ravishing dress.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Rav-AGE** — the ravages of **age**: slow, sweeping, and it never stops coming.

The word literally ends in *age*, the one force that ruins everything gradually and completely — exactly the kind of destruction *ravage* names.

### In sentences
1. Cholera **ravaged** the port districts for three summers, emptying whole streets before the wells were finally sealed.
2. He's only forty-five, but you can see the **ravages** of twenty years of night shifts on him.

### In the wild
The **Ravagers** in *Guardians of the Galaxy* — Yondu's crew of space pirates who raid and plunder for a living. The name is the job description.

### Where it comes from
Old French **ravir**, *"to seize, carry off"*, from Latin **rapere**, *"to seize by force."*

That root is worth a lot on the GRE: **rapacious** (greedily grasping), **rapt** and **rapture** (seized — by attention, by joy), **raptor** (the seizer), **rapid** (originally, snatching you along), **ravish**. Every one is about something being *taken by force*.

# **recant** *(v.)* — **ri-KANT**

### Means
To **publicly withdraw** a belief, statement, or accusation you once professed — and to declare that withdrawal openly.

This is not quietly changing your mind. Recanting is a **performance of retraction**, usually before an audience that demanded it: a court, a church, a party, a press conference. That's why it so often carries a whiff of pressure — you recant *under* something. A scientist who revises a theory has updated it; a scientist made to stand up and disown it has recanted. The word also implies the belief was held loudly enough that taking it back requires equal volume.

⚠️ **recant ≠ decant.**
- **recant** = take back what you said
- **decant** = pour liquid off gently (wine, into a decanter)

One letter, two utterly unrelated acts.

### Trick to lock it in
> **re-CANT** — you *can't* keep saying it, so you say so out loud.

The stress lands on **CANT** because recanting isn't a private rethink — it's the public admission that you can no longer stand behind the claim.

### In sentences
1. Under threat of excommunication, the astronomer **recanted** every word of the treatise he had spent a decade defending.
2. He **recanted** the whole story about the missing laptop the second his sister walked in.

### In the wild
**Galileo**, 1633: hauled before the Inquisition and forced to kneel and **recant** the claim that the Earth moves. The textbook case — right, and made to say he was wrong.

### Where it comes from
Latin **re-** (*back*) + **cantāre** (*to sing*) — literally to *sing it back*, to unsing your own tune.

That *cant-* root sings through a whole family: **chant**, **incantation**, **enchant** (to sing a spell over someone), **cantor**, **descant**, **accent**.

# **redoubtable** *(adj.)* — **ri-DOW-tuh-buhl**

*The b is silent, exactly as in doubt.*

### Means
Formidable — so strong, skilled or forceful that anyone facing you has reason to be afraid.

The fear here comes with a bow. **Formidable** can be cold and neutral; **intimidating** can be a complaint; **redoubtable** is always a compliment, paid by someone who respects the threat. It's the word for a great opponent, not a great friend — a chess grandmaster, a cross-examining barrister, a fearsome aunt. Faintly old-fashioned and often lightly amused, which is why it survives best in sports writing and obituaries.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Re-doubt** — you'd think twice before taking them on.

The hesitation is the whole meaning: not "doubtful" but *worth doubting your own chances against*.

### In sentences
1. The defence faced a redoubtable prosecutor who had not lost a case in eleven years.
2. Gran's ninety-one and still runs the whole family — redoubtable old woman.

### In the wild
Navies name warships this way: the French *Redoutable* was the ship that fired the shot that killed Nelson at Trafalgar. You name a vessel for what you want the enemy to feel.

### Where it comes from
French **redouter** = *"to dread,"* from Latin **re-** + **dubitare** = *"to doubt, to waver."* Dread is doubt turned outward — the same root gives **doubt, dubious, indubitable**.

🚫 **Not a *redoubt*.** That's a small fort (Italian *ridotto*, "a retreat") — it only picked up the silent *b* by copying *doubt*. Same look, unrelated word.

# **retiring** *(adj.)* — **rih-TEYE-ring**

### Means
Shy, reserved, unassuming — inclined to keep out of the spotlight and out of company.

On the GRE this is almost never about leaving a job. It's a settled *disposition*, not a mood: a retiring person isn't anxious or awkward, they simply decline attention. That makes it gentler and mildly approving where *timid* is weak and *antisocial* is hostile — a retiring colleague does excellent work and lets someone else present it.

**retiring ≠ reticent.**
- **retiring** = holds back your **presence** — you leave the room
- **reticent** = holds back your **words** — you stay, and say nothing

A reticent guest sits silent at the party. A retiring one never came.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Retiring** = always **retiring** early — from the party, the stage, the conversation.

The whole word is just *retire* doing its ordinary job, applied to attention rather than to work.

### In sentences
1. A **retiring** scholar, he published prolifically and lectured almost never, declining every honour that required a speech.
2. He's not exactly **retiring** — he'd been at the mic for twenty minutes before anyone could stop him.

### In the wild
The stock phrase **"shy and retiring"** is now used almost entirely sarcastically — *"our new boss isn't the shy and retiring type."* If you've heard the phrase, it was probably an insult about someone loud.

### Where it comes from
French **retirer** = *re-* ("back") + *tirer* ("to pull, draw"). To retire is literally to **draw yourself back** — from an army, from a job, or from the room.

That one image covers both senses, so you never have to store them separately.

# **shrill** *(adj.)* — **SHRIL**

### Means
**Literally:** high-pitched and piercing to the point of pain — a whistle, a smoke alarm, a scream.

**Figuratively (the GRE sense):** of a voice, a critic, or a campaign — loud, insistent and grating, harping on one note until people stop listening.

No one is ever shrill in a good way. And the figurative use is a rhetorical weapon: to call an argument *shrill* is to refuse to engage with it, attacking the pitch of the complaint rather than its substance. The word says more about the listener's irritation than the speaker's logic — which is why it's so often aimed at women and at activists.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Shr-ILL** — the noise makes you *ill*.

The word ends in *ill* because the complaint is never that the sound is loud, but that it hurts to hear.

### In sentences
1. The debate degenerated as the panel's objections grew **shrill**, each speaker pitching louder rather than better.
2. Her alarm went off with a **shrill** beep that could wake the dead.

### In the wild
Lindy West titled her memoir **Shrill** (later a Hulu series with Aidy Bryant) precisely because it's the insult thrown at women who won't quieten down — she took the slur and put it on the cover.

# **sophistry** *(n.)* — **SOF-i-stree**

### Means
Reasoning that **looks** valid and **isn't** — clever, plausible, elegantly constructed argument used to mislead rather than to find the truth.

A **fallacy** can be an honest mistake; sophistry is a charge of **bad faith**. It accuses you of knowing better and arguing anyway, and of enjoying your own cleverness while you do it. That's why it stings: it says the polish *is* the trick. Reserve it for arguments that are too well-made to be innocent — the tobacco lawyer, the ideologue, the politician who never once misspeaks.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sophistry** wears **sophisticated** clothes — a lie in a good suit.

Both come from Greek *sophos*, "wise," and the whole point of sophistry is that it sounds far too smart to be dishonest.

### In sentences
1. The defence rested on pure **sophistry**: a chain of impeccable inferences from a premise no one had agreed to.
2. Don't give me that **sophistry** — you said you'd be home by eight and it's midnight.

### Where it comes from
Greek **sophos** = *"wise."* The **Sophists** were paid teachers in 5th-century Athens who trained ambitious young men to win any argument, either side, for a fee. Plato loathed them, and his contempt stuck to the word for 2,400 years — which is why *sophistry* still means "rhetoric for hire" rather than "wisdom."

Same root, and the shifts are worth knowing: **philosophy** (*love of* wisdom), **sophomore** (*sophos* + *moros*, fool — a "wise fool"), and **sophisticated**, which originally meant *adulterated, tampered with*.

# **substantiate** *(v.)* — **suhb-STAN-shee-ayt**

### Means
To **support a claim with evidence** — to show that an assertion, charge, or rumour actually holds up.

It presupposes a claim already floating in the air and under suspicion: you substantiate *allegations*, *rumours*, *charges*, *a story*. Compare its neighbours — **verify** is a neutral fact-check, **corroborate** means a *second* independent source agrees with the first, while substantiate is the burden falling on the person who spoke: put something solid under what you said, or it collapses. Courtroom, newsroom and HR-investigation register; a boss saying "can you substantiate that?" is not curious, they are challenging you.

### Trick to lock it in
> You mostly meet it as ***un*substantiated rumour** — so *substantiate* is simply giving the rumour the substance it was missing.

Learn the negative collocation first and the positive verb defines itself: evidence is the substance, and a claim without it has none.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal dismissed the complaint, finding that the witness could not **substantiate** a single one of her allegations.
2. He keeps saying the place is haunted, but he's never **substantiated** it with anything better than a creaky door.

### In the wild
In HR and police case files, the closing verdict is literally one word: the complaint is recorded as **substantiated** or **unsubstantiated** — evidence found, or not.

### Where it comes from
Latin **substantia** = *"substance"*, from **sub-** (*under*) + **stare** (*to stand*) — what **stands under** a thing and holds it up. To substantiate is to put a foundation under a claim so it stands.

*Stare* is a GRE workhorse: **circumstance**, **constant**, **stable**, **status**, **stationary**, **obstacle** — all things standing.

# **wily** *(adj.)* — **WEYE-lee**

### Means
Crafty; skilled at getting what you want by tricks, feints and clever manoeuvring rather than by force or honesty.

Tone separates it from its neighbours: **devious** and **duplicitous** are accusations, but **wily** carries a grudging half-smile — the wily negotiator, the wily old fox who outmanoeuvred everyone in the room. It implies experience and quick wits, and it's usually applied to someone playing a weaker hand well. Calling a villain wily is almost a compliment to his brain.

**wily ≠ wiry.** *Wily* = full of tricks (mind). *Wiry* = lean, thin and tough (body). One letter apart, and both love the word *old*: the wily old lawyer, the wiry old climber.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Wily** is just **wile** + y — someone made *of* wiles.

A *wile* is a single trick, so a wily person is one whose whole method is tricks — which is why he wins by outsmarting rather than overpowering.

### In sentences
1. The **wily** ambassador conceded the small point loudly, having already secured the large one in private.
2. Don't play cards with my grandmother — she's **wily**, and she'll have your money in twenty minutes.

### In the wild
**Wile E. Coyote** — the name is the word. Nothing but schemes, traps and mail-order contraptions, never brute force.
