# **apprehension** *(n.)* — **ap-ruh-HEN-shuhn**

### Means
**1. (usual sense)** uneasy anticipation — dread about something that hasn't happened yet.
**2. (legal/police)** the **arrest** or seizure of someone.
**3. (formal, older)** **understanding**; a mental grasp of something ("beyond human apprehension").

Sense 1 is forward-facing and restrained. *Fear* is here and now; *terror* is overwhelming; *anxiety* is often free-floating and clinical. **Apprehension** attaches to a specific coming event — the scan results, the audit, the flight — and stays civil enough for a board meeting. Someone who is apprehensive still shows up; they just show up with a knot in the stomach.

On the GRE, senses 2 and 3 are the ones being tested. If "apprehension" appears in a passage and dread makes no sense, try *arrest* or *comprehension*.

### Trick to lock it in
> Ap-**PRE**-hension — the fear turns up **pre**-, before the thing does.

The stressed middle syllable marks it as anticipatory dread, not fear of what's already in the room.

### In sentences
1. The delegates awaited the verdict with visible **apprehension**, conversation dwindling to nothing as the clock passed midnight.
2. I had this weird **apprehension** about the flight all week, and then it was the smoothest one I've ever taken.

### In the wild
Every police report and crime drama: *"the suspect was apprehended near the border."* That's sense 2 — the seizing — sitting in plain sight in language you hear constantly.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prehendere** = *"to seize, to grasp"*, with **ad-** (*toward*). All three senses are one act of grabbing: you grab a criminal, you grab an idea, and a coming misfortune grabs you.

The root pays out widely: **comprehend** (grasp fully), **prehensile** (a tail that grips), **reprehensible** (fit to be seized *back* — i.e. blamed), **apprentice** (one who lays hold of a trade), **prison** and **surprise** (over-taken).

# **ardent** *(adj.)* — **AR-duhnt**

### Means
Burning with feeling — intensely enthusiastic, devoted, or in love. An **ardent** fan, an **ardent** supporter, an **ardent** admirer.

Compare the neighbours: *passionate* is the plain everyday word; *zealous* hints the person has gone too far; *fervent* leans religious or pleading. **Ardent** is warmer and more flattering than any of them — it's the word a profile-writer uses when the devotion is admirable. Note the shape of the heat: it points *toward* something you're committed to, never *at* someone in anger. You can be an ardent opponent of a policy, but you cannot be ardent with rage.

⚠️ **ardent ≠ arduous.** *Ardent* = burning with passion (Latin *ardere*, to burn). *Arduous* = steep, gruelling, exhausting (Latin *arduus*, high and hard). An ardent climber attempts an arduous route.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ardent burns *for*, never *at*.**

The heat is always devotion to something — a cause, a person, a team — so the word flatters the ardent one rather than accusing them.

### In sentences
1. Her **ardent** defence of the department's funding won over even the trustees who had arrived intending to cut it.
2. He's an **ardent** Arsenal fan — he'll drive four hours for a match he could watch at home.

### In the wild
Darcy's disastrous first proposal in *Pride and Prejudice*: *\"You must allow me to tell you how **ardently** I admire and love you.\"* — the line the 2005 film hangs its whole rain scene on.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ardēre** = *"to burn."* It gives you **ardour** (burning enthusiasm), **arson** (burning property), and, at the cooler end, **ardent spirits** — the old name for strong liquor, because it burns.

# **axiomatic** *(adj.)* — **ak-see-uh-MAT-ik**

### Means
Self-evident; true on its face, so taken as a starting point rather than argued for. Also, literally: *of or based on axioms*.

*Obvious* is casual and can be a bit rude ("that's obvious"). **Axiomatic** is cool and formal — it doesn't say *you should have known*, it says *this is where the argument begins*. Watch how it actually gets used: "it is axiomatic that…" is usually a move, a way of ruling something out of debate before anyone can dispute it. And it isn't *dogmatic* — a dogma is imposed from outside; an axiom is granted because no one sees a reason to withhold it.

⚠️ **axiomatic ≠ idiomatic.**
- **axiomatic** = needs no proof
- **idiomatic** = phrased the way a native speaker would actually say it

### Trick to lock it in
> **Axio*matic* ≈ auto*matic* — accepted automatically, no proof required.**

The claim goes through on its own, without anyone stopping to justify it.

### In sentences
1. The judgment rests on a principle the court treated as **axiomatic**: that no one may profit from his own wrongdoing.
2. Round here it's **axiomatic** that you don't touch the thermostat.

### In the wild
School geometry: Euclid's axioms — a straight line can be drawn between any two points, etc. — are the handful of statements you're allowed to *assume* so that everything else can be proved.

### Where it comes from
Greek **axios** = *"worthy"* → **axiōma**, "something thought worthy of acceptance." An axiom is a claim worthy enough to be let in without a ticket.

Same root: **axiom**, **axiology** (the study of value), and the chemists' **axial**? — no, that one's unrelated (Latin *axis*).

# **cease** *(v.)* — **SEES**

### Means
To come to an end, or to bring something to an end.

*Stop* is the everyday word and works on anything, including a single motion — *stop the car*. **Cease** is its formal cousin, and it wants something **ongoing**: fire, trading, operations, breathing, rain, publication. It carries the tone of an announcement or an order — treaties, legal notices, official statements. "Cease immediately" is what an authority writes; "cut it out" is what a person says. It also leans final: things that cease tend not to resume.

Note the fixed phrase **never ceases to** — *he never ceases to amaze me* — one of the few places the word sounds casual.

⚠️ **cease ≠ seize.** *Cease* (SEES) is to **stop**; *seize* (SEEZ) is to **grab**. Near-opposites, one letter apart — and the spelling breaks the "i before e" rule in *seize*, which is why people mix them up on the page.

### Trick to lock it in
> **The de-CEASED has ceased.** And **in-cessant** noise never does.

Both cousins point the same way: *deceased* is the formal word for stopped-for-good, and *incessant* is literally "not ceasing" — the register stays official in every one of them.

### In sentences
1. Trading on the exchange **ceased** at noon and did not resume for three days.
2. He **never ceases to** amaze me — third time this month he's locked himself out.

### In the wild
A **cease and desist** letter — the lawyer's opening move, telling you to stop doing the thing *and* never start again. And every news **ceasefire**: the fire ceases, the war doesn't necessarily end.

### Where it comes from
Latin **cedere** = *"to go, to yield, to give way"* → **cessare**, "to leave off." Yielding ground is the common thread across a big GRE family: **cede** (hand over), **concede**, **recede**, **precede**, **secede**, **cessation**, **incessant**, **deceased**.

# **conducive** *(adj.)* — **kuhn-DOO-siv**

*British: kuhn-DYOO-siv. Stress never falls on the first syllable.*

### Means
Tending to make something happen; creating the conditions in which a desired outcome can develop.

It describes a **setting, not a cause**. A quiet room doesn't produce concentration — it's conducive to it, meaning it clears the way. That's the difference from *causes* or *produces*: conducive is enabling, indirect, background. It's also grammatically clingy — the word barely exists without **to** after it, and what follows *to* is almost always something wanted (learning, sleep, recovery, growth). Register is formal-to-managerial: it lives in policy documents, school prospectuses, and doctors' advice rather than in heated speech.

⚠️ **conducive ≠ conductive.**
- **conducive** = *helps bring about* (an atmosphere conducive to trust)
- **conductive** = *carries heat or electricity* (copper is conductive)

One shapes outcomes, the other carries current.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nothing is just *conducive* — it's conducive *to* something. The word points forward, like an arrow.**

If you can't finish the sentence with "…to what?", you've used the wrong word.

### In sentences
1. The committee concluded that overcrowded wards were not **conducive** to recovery.
2. Three flatmates, one bathroom, and a broken heater — hardly **conducive** to revision.

### In the wild
Sleep-hygiene advice everywhere: *"make your bedroom conducive to sleep"* — cool, dark, no screens. The word does nothing but set up conditions, which is exactly what it means.

### Where it comes from
Latin **con-** (*together*) + **dūcere** (*to lead*) — literally *leading toward*. Conditions lead you to the result.

That root is a GRE workhorse: **conduct, induce, deduce, seduce, produce, abduct, aqueduct, ductile** — all leading, drawing, or channelling something somewhere.

- **Opposites** · [Health-giving vs. quietly poisonous](groups.md#antonym__health-giving-vs-quietly-poisonous) — with *salubrious, salutary, efficacious, benign, deleterious, detrimental, pernicious, noxious, insidious, virulent*

# **corporeal** *(adj.)* — **kor-POR-ee-uhl**

### Means
Having a **physical body**; material and touchable rather than spiritual, ghostly, or abstract.

It's the word you reach for when the *existence of a body* is the point in question — theology (soul vs. flesh), ghost stories (can it be touched?), law (corporeal property you can seize, as against an incorporeal right like a patent). *Physical* is the everyday word and carries no argument; *corporeal* is formal and always implies a contrast lurking nearby with something that has no body.

⚠️ **corporeal ≠ corporal.**
- **corporeal** = *having a body* (a corporeal being)
- **corporal** = *inflicted on the body* — corporal punishment — or an army rank

Both from *corpus*, but only *corporeal* means "made of flesh."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Corpor-eal = corpse + real.** If it has a corpse, it's real — it's got a body.

The nasty little image fixes the point that *corporeal* is specifically about possessing flesh, not merely about being real.

### In sentences
1. Medieval theologians argued endlessly over whether angels take **corporeal** form or merely appear to.
2. He looked so pale walking in that I half-wondered if he was still **corporeal**.

### In the wild
In *Harry Potter*, the mark of a truly skilled wizard is a **corporeal** Patronus — one that takes a full animal body — as opposed to the thin silver wisp most students manage.

### Where it comes from
Latin **corpus, corporis** = *"body."* One root, a huge family: **corpse**, **corpulent** (fat), **corps** and **corporal** (military bodies), **corporation** (a legal "body" of people), **corpuscle** (a tiny body in the blood), **incorporate** (take into the body).

And the direct opposite is built in: **incorporeal** = bodiless.

# **doctrinaire** *(adj. / n.)* — **dok-truh-NAIR**

*Stress lands on the last syllable, French-style — not DOK-trin-air.*

### Means
**Adj.:** insisting on a theory or doctrine rigidly, without regard for practical consequences. **Noun:** a person who does that.

The sting isn't that the belief is wrong — it's that reality is never allowed to test it. A *dogmatic* person won't question their beliefs; a **doctrinaire** person won't adjust them to the case in front of them. Hence the standard collocation: *a doctrinaire Marxist*, *doctrinaire free-market policy* — theory arriving fully formed, evidence optional. Nobody has ever called themselves doctrinaire; it is always someone else's diagnosis.

**doctrinaire ≠ doctrinal.**
- **doctrinal** = *relating to doctrine* — neutral (*a doctrinal dispute between two churches*)
- **doctrinaire** = *enslaved to doctrine* — an insult

A doctrinal difference is a topic; a doctrinaire opponent is a problem.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Doctrin-aire** — like a million**aire**, he's rich in doctrine and spends none of it on reality.

The *-aire* suffix marks a person defined by what they're loaded with, and this one's entire fortune is theory.

### In sentences
1. The reforms failed less from bad intentions than from a **doctrinaire** refusal to let local conditions modify the plan.
2. He's not a bad teacher, just so **doctrinaire** about phonics that he'd rather blame the kid than the method.

### In the wild
A stock epithet in political commentary, almost always bolted to an ideology: *a doctrinaire Marxist*, *doctrinaire libertarian*, *doctrinaire free-market economics*. If you see the word, an -ism is usually the next one along.

### Where it comes from
Latin **docēre** = *"to teach."* The family is huge and free: **doctrine** (what's taught), **doctor** (originally a *teacher*, not a physician), **document**, **indoctrinate**, and **docile** — literally *teachable*.

Note the pair at either end of that list: **docile** = too easily taught; **doctrinaire** = taught once and never again.

- **Lookalike** · [dogmatic / doctrinaire / dogged](groups.md#lookalike__doctrinaire-dogged) — with *dogmatic, dogged*

# **eclectic** *(adj.)* — **ih-KLEK-tik**

*Also eh-KLEK-tik — but the stress is never on the first syllable.*

### Means
Drawn from a **wide variety of sources**, styles, or traditions rather than following one school.

The word carries approval, and the reason is **deliberateness**. An eclectic library isn't a jumble — someone picked each thing on its own merits, ignoring the boundaries between genres. Say a collection is *random* and you're calling it thoughtless; say it's *eclectic* and you're crediting the taste behind it. It also quietly denies originality: an eclectic thinker borrows widely instead of inventing a system.

⚠️ **eclectic ≠ eccentric.**
- **eclectic** = gathered from *many* sources
- **eccentric** = *off-centre*, odd, deviating from the norm

An eclectic wardrobe pulls from six decades; an eccentric one is just strange.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hiding inside e-**clect**-ic is **elect** — you *elect* the best bits from everywhere.

That buried verb is the whole nuance: eclectic means *chosen*, never merely mixed.

### In sentences
1. The architect's style was frankly **eclectic**, marrying Gothic tracery to a steel frame without apology.
2. His playlist is so **eclectic** it went from Gregorian chant to drill in two songs.

### In the wild
A restaurant menu or a bar's music policy described as "eclectic" — the standard compliment for a lineup that refuses to sit in one genre.

### Where it comes from
Greek **eklektikos**, *"selective"* — from **ek** (out) + **legein** (to pick, gather). The ancient Eclectics were philosophers who took doctrines from every school rather than joining one.

Its Latin cousin **legere** (to choose, read) pays out across the exam: **select, elect, elite, collect, legible, lecture**.

# **equanimity** *(n.)* — **ek-wuh-NIM-uh-tee**

*Also ee-kwuh-NIM-uh-tee; both are standard.*

### Means
Calmness and evenness of mind, especially **under pressure** — composure that holds when things go wrong.

The word carries the strain with it. Nobody praises your equanimity on a quiet Sunday; it's invoked when there was provocation, bad news, or humiliation and the person did **not** wobble. It also isn't numbness — an equanimous person feels the blow and stays level anyway, which is why it reads as a virtue rather than a temperament. Register is formal and admiring; "she took the verdict with equanimity" is a compliment paid at a slight distance.

⚠️ **equanimity ≠ magnanimity.** Both end in *-animity* ("-mindedness"), but **equanimity** is an *even* mind (calm under fire) and **magnanimity** is a *great* mind (generous, forgiving to a rival). You keep your equanimity; you show magnanimity to someone else.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Equ**-animity = an **equal** mind: the level stays the same however hard the ground shakes.

"Level" only means anything when something is trying to tip you, which is why the word needs a crisis to appear in.

### In sentences
1. He received the news of his dismissal with an equanimity that unsettled the board more than anger would have.
2. Three flights cancelled and she just shrugged — I've never seen anyone handle an airport with that much equanimity.

### In the wild
It's the standard English rendering of the Buddhist *upekkhā*, so it's everywhere in meditation apps like Headspace and Calm — sessions literally titled "equanimity," meaning: notice the discomfort, don't get dragged by it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **aequus** ("even, equal") + **animus** ("mind, spirit") = *even-mindedness*.

The **animus** half is the profitable one — it gives you a free GRE cluster: **unanimous** (one mind), **magnanimous** (great spirit), **pusillanimous** (*pusillus*, tiny — a tiny spirit, i.e. cowardly), **animosity** (spirit turned hostile).

# **exorbitant** *(adj.)* — **ig-ZOR-buh-tuhnt**

*The x is a soft "gz", not "ks" — ig-ZOR, never eks-OR.*

### Means
Far beyond what is reasonable or customary — **grossly excessive**.

In practice it attaches to **prices, fees, rents, salaries, demands, claims** — things measured in money or scale. *Expensive* is a fact; **exorbitant** is a verdict, and an indignant one: it says the amount has left the range any fair person would accept. Slightly formal, common in journalism and complaint letters, and almost always spoken by the person being charged, never the person charging.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ex-ORBIT-ant** — the price has left orbit.

*Orbit* is sitting right there in the middle of the word, and that's literally the metaphor: a normal price stays in its track, an exorbitant one flies off it.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal found the landlord's demands **exorbitant**, some four times the prevailing rent for comparable flats.
2. Forty quid for a burger and a Coke? That's **exorbitant**, mate.

### In the wild
The word the internet reaches for every time Ticketmaster's checkout page adds its **exorbitant** service fees — a near-fixed collocation with "fees."

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** (*out of*) + **orbita** (*a wheel-track, a rut*) — literally **"off the track."** Originally a legal term for a case that fell outside the ordinary course of law; the sense narrowed to prices that fall outside the ordinary range.

Same **orbita/orbis** (*circle, wheel*) gives you **orbit**, **orbital**, and **orb**.

# **fickle** *(adj.)* — **FIK-uhl**

### Means
Changing your mind, loyalty, or affection constantly and for no good reason — devoted today, indifferent tomorrow.

*Changeable* is a neutral description; **fickle** is a mild accusation. It carries a whiff of shallowness: the fickle person isn't reasoning their way to a new position, they're just following whatever they happen to feel this week. That's why it attaches so naturally to lovers, fans, voters, markets, and fate — anything whose favour you can't count on. Note the direction of blame: calling someone fickle means *you* were let down by them.

⚠️ **fickle ≠ finicky.** *Fickle* = changes what it wants. *Finicky* = knows exactly what it wants and rejects everything else. A fickle eater orders something different every visit; a finicky eater sends it back.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fickle** rhymes with **tickle** — they go with whatever tickles them today.

The rhyme carries the nuance: it's whim, not judgement, that moves them.

### In sentences
1. Public taste is **fickle**, and the studio that reads it correctly one year is bankrupt the next.
2. She's so **fickle** — best friends in September, doesn't text her back by October.

### In the wild
Juliet, waving Romeo off into exile: *"O Fortune, Fortune! all men call thee **fickle**."* She's actually *hoping* Fortune lives up to the word — if it's that changeable, it might change back and send him home.

### Where it comes from
Old English **ficol** = *"deceitful, treacherous"* (kin to *befician*, "to deceive"). The original sense was outright dishonesty, and that's why the modern word still stings — a fickle person hasn't merely changed, they've broken faith.

# **figurative** *(adj.)* — **FIG-yuh-ruh-tiv**

### Means
**1.** Not literal — using metaphor, simile, or image to mean something other than the plain words. *"Drowning in paperwork"* is figurative; you are dry.
**2.** In art: **representational** — depicting recognisable people and things, as opposed to abstract.

The word is the workhorse antonym of *literal*, and it's descriptive rather than approving: calling language figurative just classifies it, it doesn't praise it. Note the split personality — in a grammar context it means "not to be taken at face value," but in a gallery it means almost the opposite, "actually shows a real thing." GRE passages use both senses, so let the surrounding subject decide which one is live.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Figurative = full of *figures* — figures of speech, or figures on a canvas.**

The whole word is just *figure* + *-ative*, and which kind of figure you mean — a turn of phrase or a human form — is exactly what splits the two senses.

### In sentences
1. The manifesto's talk of "cleansing fire" was **figurative**, though its critics chose to read it otherwise.
2. When I said I'd kill for a coffee, that was **figurative**, obviously.

### In the wild
Gallery and auction labels lean on sense 2 constantly — Lucian Freud and Francis Bacon are filed as **figurative painters**, meaning you can see a body in the paint, unlike a Rothko.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fingere** = *"to shape, mould, form"* → **figura**, "a shape." A figure is a shape given to a thing — a body, a number, a phrase.

Same root, wide family: **figment** (something moulded in the mind), **fiction**, **effigy**, **feign** (to shape a false face).

# **flustered** *(adj. (also v., to fluster))* — **FLUS-terd**

### Means
Rattled — agitated and muddled to the point where you fumble your words or lose your thread.

It is a *small, brief* loss of composure, usually from surprise, embarrassment, or being put on the spot. Panic implies real danger; **flustered** implies only that someone caught you off guard and you went pink and started over. It is a gentle, almost affectionate word — you can be flustered by a compliment. Note the pressure comes from *outside*: things fluster you, and the flustered person is trying (and visibly failing) to stay smooth.

⚠️ **flustered ≠ blustered.** One letter, opposite postures: the **flustered** person is quietly rattled and losing control; the **blustering** person is loudly bullying and *pretending* to have it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fluster = flutter + fussed.**

Both hidden words are right there in the sound, and both are small, flappy, harmless agitations — not real distress.

### In sentences
1. Cross-examined on a detail she had not anticipated, the witness grew visibly **flustered** and contradicted her earlier account.
2. Don't get **flustered** — they're just asking where the file is.

### In the wild
Hugh Grant built an entire career on it — the stammering, apologising, permanently **flustered** Englishman of *Four Weddings and a Funeral* and *Notting Hill*.

# **gullible** *(adj.)* — **GUH-luh-buhl**

### Means
Easily tricked or deceived — believing whatever you're told, with no instinct to check it.

The word carries a faint sneer. Calling someone **gullible** isn't observing that they were misled once; it labels them a standing easy mark, someone con artists find. *Naive* blames inexperience and can be almost tender; *credulous* is the cool, formal version, used of beliefs and audiences ("a credulous public"). **Gullible** is the playground-blunt one, and it's always at the believer's expense.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Gull**ible has your **gull**et in it — it goes straight down, no chewing.

The gullible person swallows the story whole rather than biting into it first.

### In sentences
1. The scheme relied on a steady supply of **gullible** investors, each persuaded that the returns were merely unusual rather than impossible.
2. He's so **gullible** he'll believe you if you tell him the WiFi is stronger near the window.

### In the wild
The oldest prank in the schoolyard: *"You know 'gullible' isn't in the dictionary?"* — and someone always goes to check.

### Where it comes from
From **gull**, an old English word for *a dupe* — and, as a verb, *to cheat someone*. It survives in that verb: to be **gulled** is to be taken in. So a gullible person is, literally, gullable — able to be gulled.

# **idiosyncratic** *(adj.)* — **id-ee-oh-sin-KRAT-ik**

*The noun shifts the stress: idiosyncrasy = id-ee-oh-SIN-kruh-see.*

### Means
Peculiar to one individual — a habit, style, or reaction that belongs to *this* person or thing and follows no general rule.

Tone matters: **idiosyncratic** is the polite, often admiring word. *Eccentric* implies a performance others notice; *erratic* implies you can't be relied on; **idiosyncratic** just says the pattern is yours alone — a novelist's idiosyncratic punctuation, a chef's idiosyncratic knife grip. It also has a straight technical life: an idiosyncratic drug reaction, or idiosyncratic risk in finance, means "specific to this one case, not the population."

⚠️ **idiosyncratic ≠ idiopathic.** Both open with *idio-*, both hang around medicine.
- **idiosyncratic** = specific to *this individual*
- **idiopathic** = of *unknown cause* (idiopathic hypertension: nobody knows why)

### Trick to lock it in
> **IDIO- = "my own," as in *idiom*.** An idiom is a language's private quirk; an idiosyncrasy is a person's.

Nobody calls an idiom a mistake — it's just how that language does it, which is exactly the non-judgmental flavour of *idiosyncratic*.

### In sentences
1. Her prose style is **idiosyncratic** to the point of unmistakability: no semicolons, no adverbs, and paragraphs that stop mid-thought.
2. He's got this **idiosyncratic** thing where he won't take a call unless he's pacing.

### In the wild
Standard vocabulary in investing: **idiosyncratic risk** is the risk attached to one company (a CEO scandal, a failed drug trial) as opposed to *systematic* risk, which hits the whole market. Diversification kills the idiosyncratic kind — that's the entire pitch for index funds.

### Where it comes from
Greek **idios** = *"one's own, private"* + **syn-krasis** = *"mixing together"* — literally **your own personal blend**, from the old medicine of mixed humours.

*Idios* is worth banking: **idiom** (a language's own way of putting it), **idiopathic** (cause private to that patient), and **idiot** — originally just a *private citizen*, someone holding no public office, hence a layman, hence ignorant.

# **incidental** *(adj. / n.)* — **in-si-DEN-tuhl**

### Means
**Adj.:** occurring as a minor accompaniment to something more important; secondary, subordinate, beside the point.
**Noun (usu. plural, *incidentals*):** the small extra costs attached to a main expense.

The key is *attachment*: an incidental thing genuinely belongs to the main event — it comes with it, arises from it — but it isn't what matters. Saying a detail is incidental is a quiet way of demoting it: not false, not irrelevant, just not worth the attention it's getting. Compare *trivial*, which dismisses a thing outright, and *peripheral*, which puts it at the edge; *incidental* keeps the thing firmly inside the picture and still says "never mind that."

⚠️ **incidental ≠ accidental.**
- **incidental** = *minor and attendant* — it came along with the main thing
- **accidental** = *unintended* — nobody meant it to happen

Damage can be accidental (no one meant it) or incidental (a side effect of doing the main job). "Incidental contact" in sport is contact that's part of the game; "accidental contact" is a mistake.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Incidental** = a side ***incident***: it happens *with* the main event, not *to* it.

The word wears *incident* on its face, and that's the whole nuance — a small attached happening, not a mishap and not an irrelevance.

### In sentences
1. The report treats the environmental damage as **incidental** to the project's real purpose, which is precisely the assumption the inquiry rejected.
2. Sure, I learned some Spanish on the trip, but that was **incidental** — I was there for the food.']

### In the wild
Check into a hotel and they put a hold on your card **for incidentals** — the minibar, the parking, the room-service tray. Not the room you came for; the little things that ride along with it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **incidere** = *"to fall upon"* (**in-** "on" + **cadere** "to fall") — an incidental thing *falls alongside* the main one.

**cadere** is a workhorse root: **incident, coincide, cadence** (falling voice), **decadent** (fallen away), **cascade, cadaver** — and **accident**, something that simply *falls out* unplanned. Same falling, different angle: the accident falls by chance, the incidental falls in company.

# **ingrained** *(adj.)* — **in-GRAYND**

*Also spelled engrained; same pronunciation, same meaning.*

### Means
Fixed so deeply — into a habit, belief, or surface — that it can't be scrubbed out. Used of prejudices, customs, reflexes, and literal dirt.

The word carries **time and repetition**: nothing is ingrained overnight. A belief you merely hold is a belief; one you've held so long it moves your hands before your mind catches up is ingrained. It also carries **resistance** — you say "ingrained" precisely when you're conceding how hard the thing will be to shift, which is why it so often turns up next to *prejudice*, *habit*, *corruption*, and *grime*.

⚠️ **ingrained ≠ innate.**
- **innate** = there from birth, in the genes
- **ingrained** = worked in later, by years of repetition

An ingrained habit was *learned*. That's the whole distinction the GRE is testing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **In the grain — not on the surface.**

Dirt on the surface wipes off; dirt down in the grain of the wood is part of the wood now, and so is an ingrained habit.

### In sentences
1. Reform failed because the corruption was **ingrained** in the ministry's every procedure, not confined to a few officials.
2. Forty years in the army and he still can't slouch — the posture's **ingrained**.

### In the wild
Cleaning-product advertising runs on this word: every bathroom spray and washing powder promises to lift **ingrained** dirt and grime — the stuff that's got *into* the surface, not sitting on it.

### Where it comes from
From **"dyed in grain"** — cloth coloured right through the fibre with kermes dye instead of tinted on the outside. The dye was in the grain, so it never washed out.

Behind it is Latin **granum** = *"grain, seed,"* which pays out elsewhere: **granule**, **granary**, **garner**, and — via *pomegranate*, the seed-packed fruit — **grenade**, a bomb named for looking like one.

# **insolent** *(adj.)* — **IN-suh-luhnt**

### Means
Openly disrespectful in a bold, contemptuous way — rude with an attitude of superiority behind it.

Rudeness aimed **upward**. Insolence needs a hierarchy to offend: a servant to a master, a student to a teacher, a private to an officer, a teenager to a parent. The offence isn't the words alone but the *refusal to defer* — the shrug, the smirk, the tone. And the word is usually chosen by the person being disrespected, which makes it faintly pompous: calling someone insolent announces that you expected deference and didn't get it.

⚠️ **insolent ≠ indolent.** One letter apart, both GRE regulars: **insolent** = disrespectful; **indolent** = lazy. The insolent employee talks back; the indolent one never gets up.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Insolent** begins like **insult** — an insult delivered *upward*, with a shrug.

Any insult is rude, but the insolent one is aimed at someone who outranks you and expected better.

### In sentences
1. The magistrate found his **insolent** silence more offensive than any answer he might have given.
2. One more **insolent** remark like that and you're off the team.

### In the wild
In *The Phantom of the Opera*, the Phantom's furious line at Raoul — **"Insolent boy! This slave of fashion, basking in your glory!"** — is the word in its natural habitat: a self-styled superior enraged that an inferior didn't bow.

### Where it comes from
Latin **insolens** = *in-* ("not") + **solere** ("to be accustomed") — literally *not behaving as one is accustomed to*. The insolent person breaks the customary deference.

Same root, unexpectedly: **obsolete** (*ob-* + *solere*) — something that has fallen out of the accustomed use.

# **lampoon** *(v. / n.)* — **lam-POON**

### Means
**Verb:** to publicly mock a person or institution with ridicule.
**Noun:** the piece of writing, cartoon, or sketch that does it.

Satire can be dry and impersonal; a lampoon has a **target with a name**. It's aimed, broad, and unmistakably rude — the goal is to make the audience laugh *at* someone until the dignity comes off. *Parody* imitates a style, *satire* exposes a vice, *lampoon* humiliates a person. Note also that it's almost always public and performed: you lampoon a president in a magazine, not a colleague over lunch.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Lampoon rhymes with harpoon** — you don't gently tease the target, you spear it in public.

The rhyme carries the nuance: a lampoon is thrown *at* a named victim, and everyone watches it land.

### In sentences
1. The pamphlet **lampooned** the prime minister as a bloated toad in a powdered wig, and the print run sold out in a day.
2. Every year the students put on a show that **lampoons** the teachers, and every year one teacher takes it far too personally.

### In the wild
**National Lampoon** — the magazine that spun off *Animal House* and the *Vacation* films. The name is borrowed from **The Harvard Lampoon**, the college humour paper that has been mocking the powerful since 1876. The brand *is* the definition.

### Where it comes from
French **lampon**, thought to come from **lampons!** — *"let's drink!"* — the shouted refrain of 17th-century French drinking songs, which were where the rude verses about local bigwigs got sung.

That's the register in one word: a lampoon is tavern mockery, not drawing-room wit.

# **lavish** *(adj. / v.)* — **LAV-ish**

### Means
**Adj.:** given or existing in great, unstinting abundance — a **lavish** banquet, **lavish** praise, a **lavish** budget.
**Verb (+ *on*):** to bestow something in generous excess — *she lavished attention on the new puppy*.

The word measures **scale, not virtue**. *Generous* compliments the giver's heart; *lavish* comments on the sheer volume coming out — which is why it can be warm (a lavish welcome) or quietly damning (a lavish lifestyle on a civil servant's salary). Next to *extravagant*, which almost always means wasteful, *lavish* stays admiring more often than not; next to *opulent*, which is about the look of wealth sitting there, *lavish* is about wealth being **poured out**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Lavish = LAVA-ish.** It doesn't trickle — it pours, and it keeps coming.

The image of an overflow rather than a gift is exactly the nuance: what makes something lavish is the volume, not the kindness behind it.

### In sentences
1. The prosecution detailed a **lavish** lifestyle — three homes, a yacht, and a wine cellar no salary could explain.
2. Her grandmother **lavishes** food on anyone who walks through the door; you don't leave that house hungry.

### In the wild
Open any fraud or celebrity-downfall story and the phrase is waiting for you: *"funded a **lavish** lifestyle."* It's practically the word's home address in journalism — abundance with a raised eyebrow.

### Where it comes from
Latin **lavāre** = *"to wash"* → Old French **lavasse**, a **downpour of rain**. Lavish is rain, not a raindrop.

Same root, whole family: **lavatory**, **lotion**, **launder**, **ablution** — and **lava**, the stream that washes down the mountainside.

# **lugubrious** *(adj.)* — **loo-GOO-bree-uhs**

*Also heard as loo-GYOO-bree-uhs.*

### Means
Mournful, dismal, funereally sad — **gloomy in a heavy, drawn-out, over-the-top way**.

The word almost always carries a raised eyebrow. *Sad* is a feeling; *lugubrious* is a **performance** of sadness — the drooping face, the sighing voice, the funeral-organ delivery. Writers reach for it when the gloom is so thick it tips into comedy: a lugubrious waiter, a lugubrious ballad, a lugubrious basset hound. Call a genuinely grieving person lugubrious and you're mocking them, so it's a word applied from the outside, at a slight distance.

**lugubrious ≠ salubrious.** Same *-ubrious* ending, opposite worlds: **salubrious** = *healthy, wholesome* (a salubrious climate); **lugubrious** = *dripping with gloom*. A salubrious spa, a lugubrious funeral.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Loo-GOO** — all that **goo** in the middle: gloom poured on thick and slow.

The word itself sags through four sluggish syllables, which is exactly the laid-on-too-thick sadness it describes.

### In sentences
1. The organ wheezed out a **lugubrious** hymn while the mourners shuffled past in the rain.
2. Stop being so **lugubrious** — you lost one tennis match, not the will to live.

### In the wild
**Eeyore** in *Winnie-the-Pooh* is the walking definition — the flat grey voice, the drooping ears, the "thanks for noticing me." Nobody has ever been more lugubrious.

### Where it comes from
Latin **lugēre** = *"to mourn"* — the verb for grieving at a funeral. Nothing in the word's shape tells you that, so it's worth the twenty seconds: **lugubrious = mourning-grade gloom**, not ordinary sadness.

# **macabre** *(adj.)* — **muh-KAHB-ruh**

*Also just muh-KAHB. Never "ma-KAY-ber" — the final -re is French.*

### Means
Disturbing because it dwells on **death, corpses, and bodily decay** — grim, ghoulish, gruesome.

The word carries a whiff of *theatre*. Morbid describes a mind that broods on death; macabre describes the thing itself put on display — a skull-shaped candle, a hanging scene played for laughs, a museum of anatomical specimens. It's the register of Halloween, gothic fiction, and black comedy, so it can be said with relish: **macabre humour** is a compliment in a way *morbid humour* isn't quite.

### Trick to lock it in
> **macabre / cadavre** — one sound apart, and both keep the corpse in the room.

French *cadavre* (corpse) rhymes it into place, and the shared shape reminds you that macabre isn't vaguely dark — it's specifically about death made visible.

### In sentences
1. The exhibition's **macabre** centrepiece was a chandelier assembled from the bones of the monastery's dead.
2. His jokes at the funeral were **macabre**, but honestly, the whole room needed the laugh.

### In the wild
Saint-Saëns' **Danse Macabre** — the skeletons-fiddling-at-midnight piece wheeled out every Halloween and in a hundred film scores. The word's whole flavour is in that music: death, but dancing.

### Where it comes from
English lifted it straight from the Old French phrase **danse macabre**, the medieval "Dance of Death" — a stock image of grinning skeletons leading pope, king, and peasant alike to the grave. That's why the word still implies death *staged* rather than merely feared.

# **morose** *(adj.)* — **muh-ROHS**

*The -ose hisses, never buzzes: -ROHS, not -ROHZ.*

### Means
Gloomy, sullen, and **ill-humoured with it** — sunk in a bad mood and closed off to anyone trying to lift it.

Sad and morose are not interchangeable. **Sad** is pain you feel; **morose** is a mood other people have to put up with. It's sourness turned outward — the one-word answers, the refusal to be cheered, the black cloud parked at the end of the table. Calling someone morose carries a faint edge of blame: they're not just unhappy, they're being difficult about it.

**morose ≠ morbid.**
- **morose** = sullen, gloomy *mood*
- **morbid** = unhealthy *fascination* with death, disease, the gruesome

A morbid person can be perfectly cheerful about the autopsy photos.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's a **rose** hidden inside *morose* — hand him one and he still won't smile.

The word isn't plain sadness but gloom that resists comfort, which is exactly why it grates on everyone nearby.

### In sentences
1. After the verdict he grew **morose**, taking his meals alone and answering letters in a single line.
2. Don't mind Dad — he goes **morose** for a week every time his team gets relegated.

### In the wild
**Eeyore** in *Winnie-the-Pooh* is the picture of morose — the flat monotone, the drooping tail, the way every attempt to cheer him up bounces straight off.

### Where it comes from
Latin **mōrōsus** = *"peevish, set in one's ways,"* from **mōs, mōris** = *"custom, habit, one's own will."* The original sense was a person rigidly wedded to their own manner — hence prickly and hard to please.

That root also gives **moral**, **morals**, **mores**, **morality**.

🚫 **Not from *mors*, "death."** That's the *mort-* family — *mortal, morbid, mortuary, moribund*. Look-alike syllable, unrelated word.

# **officious** *(adj.)* — **uh-FISH-uhs**

### Means
Pushily eager to help, advise, or enforce rules **when nobody asked** — meddling dressed up as service.

The sting is the mismatch between how small the person's authority is and how large they act. A helpful colleague is helpful; an **officious** one plants himself between you and the photocopier to explain the form you already filled in. It's not cruelty, it's self-importance — the busybody, the clipboard, the jobsworth. Always an insult, never a compliment, and it usually says the person enjoys the power more than the task.

⚠️ **officious ≠ official.** *Official* = actually holding the authority. *Officious* = acting as if you do. The security guard is official; the neighbour who tells you where to park is officious.

### Trick to lock it in
> **An officious man keeps his *office* in his head, not on the door.**

The word literally contains **office**, and that's the joke — he behaves like a functionary without ever having been given the job.

### In sentences
1. The undersecretary was **officious** to the point of obstruction, demanding stamped copies of documents no statute required.
2. Some **officious** guy at the gym kept correcting my form — mate, you don't work here.

### In the wild
**Dwight Schrute** in *The Office* — "Assistant to the Regional Manager," volunteer sheriff's deputy, self-appointed enforcer of rules nobody voted on. Same root, exact meaning.

### Where it comes from
Latin **officium** = *"duty, service"* → *officiosus*, originally **"obliging, dutiful"** — a compliment. English soured it: too much duty, offered too freely, becomes interference.

Same root gives **office, official, officiate, officer** — the whole vocabulary of people with jobs, and one word for the man who only thinks he has one.

# **ramification** *(n.)* — **ram-uh-fi-KAY-shuhn**

### Means
A **consequence** of an action or decision — specifically one of the many that spread outwards from it. (Literal sense, rarer: a branch or branching structure.)

A *consequence* can be single and neutral; a **ramification** is one strand in a spreading web of them, and the word carries a warning that you probably haven't traced them all. It also runs formal and slightly ominous — boardrooms, courts, policy papers. Note that it lives almost entirely in the **plural**: you consider the *ramifications*, and they are legal, financial, political, or diplomatic far more often than they are pleasant.

⚠️ **ramification ≠ ratification.**
- **ratification** = formal *approval* of a treaty or agreement
- **ramification** = the *fallout* from it

The treaty's **ratification** was swift; its **ramifications** took decades.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nobody ever says *"the delightful ramifications."* The word only turns up after **serious**, **legal**, **financial**, or **far-reaching**.

The company it keeps tells you the tone: heavy, formal, and braced for bad news.

### In sentences
1. The ruling's **ramifications** extend well beyond the two parties before the court, unsettling contract law across the industry.
2. Sure, you can skip the deposit — but have you thought about the **ramifications**?

### In the wild
Any accountant or lawyer you ever hire will ask whether you've considered the **tax ramifications** — it's one of the few places the word shows up in ordinary speech, and always as a caution.

### Where it comes from
Latin **rāmus** = *"branch."* To **ramify** is to branch out — so a ramification is literally a branch growing off your decision, then branching again. That's why the plural is the natural form: one act, a whole tree of outcomes.

# **serene** *(adj.)* — **suh-REEN**

### Means
Calm, clear and untroubled — of a sky, a sea, a face, a temperament.

Other calm-words describe an inner state; **serene** describes calm that *shows*. It's the visible, unclouded surface: no ripple, no cloud, nothing agitating the picture. Where *placid* can hint at dullness and *calm* is merely the absence of storm, serene is elevated, almost luminous — the word you reach for describing a monk, a lake at dawn, or someone smiling politely while everything around them burns. It's also faintly grand: European princes are still styled *Your Serene Highness*.

⚠️ **serene ≠ sere.** *Sere* (**SEER**) means **dried up, withered** — sere grass in August. Same first four letters, opposite mood, and both turn up on the GRE.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Serene** rhymes with **clean** — a sky wiped clean of cloud, a face wiped clean of worry.

The rhyme carries the nuance: serenity is about a *surface with nothing on it*, which is why the word works equally for weather and for people.

### In sentences
1. Hours after the verdict she remained **serene**, as though the sentence had been passed on someone else entirely.
2. Everyone else was panicking about the deadline and he just sat there, **serene**, eating a sandwich.

### In the wild
The **Serenity Prayer** — *\"grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change\"* — recited at every AA meeting and printed on a million fridge magnets.

### Where it comes from
Latin **serēnus** = *"clear, cloudless"* — originally a weather word, only later applied to people. That's why serene still feels like describing a sky.

Same root: **serenity**, and **serenade** — music for a clear, calm evening.

🚫 **Not *serendipity*.** That comes from *Serendip*, an old name for Sri Lanka, via a Persian fairy tale. Similar opening, unrelated word.

# **supplant** *(v.)* — **suh-PLANT**

### Means
To **take the place of something or someone else — by pushing them out.**

*Replace* is neutral bookkeeping: the old thing left, the new thing moved in. *Supplant* has a loser in it. The thing supplanted was in possession, and is now dispossessed — by a rival, a newer technology, a younger favourite. Often there's a whiff of scheming (the courtier who supplants the king's advisor), but it's just as at home in cool analytical prose about markets and ideas. The one constant: displacement, not mere succession.

⚠️ **supplant ≠ supplement.** *Supplant* **replaces** the original; *supplement* **adds to** it. One letter apart, near-opposite in meaning — the GRE knows this.

### Trick to lock it in
> **sup-PLANT** — you plant your foot *under* his and trip him out of his spot.

The hidden *plant* is literal — Latin *planta* is the sole of the foot, and supplanting is tripping someone up to stand where they stood.

### In sentences
1. Within a decade, the vernacular had **supplanted** Latin as the language of serious scholarship.
2. The intern learned the new system in a week and quietly **supplanted** half the team.

### In the wild
Business writing runs on this verb: *Netflix supplanted Blockbuster*, *streaming supplanted radio*. Notice it's never used for a friendly handover — always for the one that got wiped out.

### Where it comes from
Latin **supplantāre** = *"to trip up"* — from **sub-** (*under*) + **planta** (*the sole of the foot*). To supplant someone was to hook your foot under theirs and bring them down.

That *planta* also gives you **plantar** (as in plantar fasciitis — the sole of your foot) and **plant** itself (something pressed into the ground by foot).

# **tacit** *(adj.)* — **TAS-it**

*Short *a*, as in cat — not "TAY-sit."*

### Means
Understood, accepted or agreed **without being put into words**.

The key is that something *was* communicated — just never spoken. Silence here isn't emptiness, it's a signal: you stayed quiet, and that counted as a yes. So *tacit* carries a faint whiff of complicity, which is why it pairs so naturally with things people would rather not say aloud — **tacit approval** of a shady deal, a **tacit admission** of guilt. Compare *implicit*, which is colder and more logical (implied by the content itself, no audience required); *tacit* needs two parties and an understanding between them.

**tacit ≠ taciturn.** Same root, different target: **tacit** describes a *thing* (an agreement, consent) that went unspoken; **taciturn** describes a *person* who habitually says little. A taciturn man can give you tacit consent by saying nothing at all.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tacit never travels alone** — it's always tacit *approval*, tacit *consent*, tacit *agreement*, tacit *understanding*.

Every one of its partner-nouns is a form of saying yes, which tells you *tacit* means agreement-by-silence, not just silence.

### In sentences
1. The committee's failure to object was taken as **tacit** endorsement of the new policy.
2. Nobody ever agreed out loud that I'd do the dishes — it was just a **tacit** thing once he started cooking.

### In the wild
**Tacit knowledge** is standard business and academic vocabulary for the stuff a veteran employee knows but can't write down — how to ride a bike, how to read a room. It walks out the door when they quit, precisely because nobody ever said it aloud.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tacēre** = *"to be silent."* It quietly hands you three GRE words: **tacit** (unspoken but agreed), **taciturn** (a person of few words), and **reticent** (*re-* + *tacēre* — holding back what you could say).

# **transcend** *(v.)* — **tran-SEND**

### Means
To **rise above and beyond** a limit, a category, or ordinary experience — to go past something so completely that its rules no longer apply to you.

Compare the neighbours: *surpass* and *exceed* are competitive and measurable — you beat a number, a rival, a record, and you stay on the same scale. **Transcend leaves the scale behind.** A film that surpasses other action films is still an action film; a film that transcends the genre is no longer judged as one. It carries a lift of admiration and often a whiff of the spiritual or philosophical — the self, death, language, the physical world are the things people say get transcended.

⚠️ **transcend ≠ transgress.** Both start *trans-* ("across"), but **transcend** = *climb beyond* (rise above a limit); **transgress** = *step across* (violate a rule). One elevates you, one gets you in trouble.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ascend** gets you to the top of the ladder; **transcend** means you no longer need the ladder.

Both words are the same *-scend* ("climb"), but *trans-* takes you clean out of the system rather than to the top of it.

### In sentences
1. Her late work **transcends** portraiture altogether; the sitter is almost incidental to what the paint is doing.
2. It started as a school project, but honestly it kind of **transcended** that — people are using it for real now.

### In the wild
**Transcendental Meditation** — the practice the Beatles flew to Rishikesh in 1968 to study with the Maharishi, and the reason half the *White Album* exists. The whole promise in the name: get *beyond* ordinary thought.

### Where it comes from
Latin **trans** (*beyond*) + **scandere** (*to climb*) — literally "to climb beyond."

That *-scend* is a workhorse: **ascend** (climb up), **descend** (climb down), **condescend** (climb *down* to someone's level — which is exactly why it's an insult), **ascendancy** (having climbed to dominance).

# **treatise** *(n.)* — **TREE-tis**

*Ends in a hissing -s, not a -z: TREE-tis, never "TREE-tize".*

### Means
A long, formal, **systematic** written work that covers one subject thoroughly and methodically.

Size and rigour are the whole point. An **essay** explores, an **article** reports, a **tract** preaches a short partisan message — a **treatise** sets out to be *the* definitive handling of its topic, chapter by chapter, from first principles. It implies authority and exhaustiveness, and it implies weight on the shelf.

Because of that heft, it's also the standard word for gentle mockery: any document far longer than the occasion deserved gets called a treatise.

⚠️ **treatise ≠ treaty.** Same root, different worlds: a **treatise** is a book that *handles a subject*; a **treaty** is a signed agreement between states. Nations sign treaties; scholars write treatises.

### Trick to lock it in
> A trea**tise** **treat**s one subject — and treats it to death.

To "treat of" a topic is to handle it, and a treatise handles it so completely that nothing is left to say.

### In sentences
1. Hume was twenty-eight when he published his **treatise** on human nature, three volumes of it, and it fell, in his words, dead-born from the press.
2. He replied to a one-line email with a four-page **treatise** on why the meeting should be moved.

### In the wild
Hume's *A Treatise of Human Nature* — the go-to example of the form: a young philosopher trying to settle an entire field in one enormous book.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tractāre** = *"to handle, to drag about"* (from *trahere*, to pull). To *treat* a subject is literally to get your hands on it.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **tract** (a pulled-out stretch of land, or a pamphlet), **extract**, **contract**, **retract**, **tractor**, **traction**, **protracted**.
