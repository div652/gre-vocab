# **appease** *(v.)* — **uh-PEEZ**

### Means
To calm or pacify someone **by giving them what they want** — concessions, apologies, tribute. Also, in a milder sense, to satisfy an appetite: *appease hunger, appease curiosity*.

*Placate* and *mollify* just describe soothing someone. **Appease** almost always tells you the price: you bought the quiet, and you bought it by yielding. That's why the noun **appeasement** is close to an accusation — it says the peacemaker was weak, and that the bully will be back for more.

### Trick to lock it in
> app-**EASE** — you put *them* at ease by giving up something of *yours*.

It never means winning someone over by argument; it means purchasing calm with concessions, which is why the word carries a whiff of cowardice.

### In sentences
1. The board **appeased** the striking staff with a one-off bonus, hoping the structural grievances would quietly go away.
2. I made him a sandwich just to **appease** him for ten minutes.

### In the wild
**Appeasement** is the word history pinned on Neville Chamberlain — handing Hitler the Sudetenland at Munich in 1938 and flying home promising "peace for our time." A year later, war. Ever since, calling a policy "appeasement" means calling it a surrender.

### Where it comes from
Old French **apaisier** = *"to bring to peace,"* from Latin **pax, pacis** (peace).

That root pays off widely: **pacify**, **pacifist**, **pacific** (calm — Magellan named the **Pacific** Ocean for its flat water), **pact** (a settled agreement), and even **pay** — Latin *pacare* meant to pacify a creditor by settling up. Which is exactly what appeasing is: paying someone off so they'll calm down.

# **arbitrary** *(adj.)* — **AHR-bi-trair-ee**

*British: AHR-bi-truh-ree — the third syllable collapses to a schwa.*

### Means
Decided by personal whim or preference rather than by reason, rule, or system — and, at full strength, **power used without check or accountability**.

Two settings, and the GRE tests both. In maths or logic it's neutral bookkeeping: *let n be an arbitrary integer* just means "any one, chosen without constraint." Everywhere else it's an **accusation**: to call a rule, a fine, or a deadline arbitrary is to say nobody can point to the principle behind it. Push it further and it turns political — *arbitrary rule*, *arbitrary arrest* — where the complaint is not randomness but **unchecked authority**, a ruler answerable only to his own say-so. That second sense is the one students miss when they file the word under "random."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Arbitr**ary hides the **arbiter** — the referee's call stands, whether or not there's a rule behind it.

An arbitrary decision is one backed by nothing but the decider's authority, which is why the word slides so easily from "random" to "tyrannical."

### In sentences
1. The court struck down the regulation as **arbitrary and capricious**, finding no evidence the agency had weighed any evidence at all.
2. Why is the cutoff 500 words? Feels totally **arbitrary**.

### In the wild
Article 9 of the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights**: *"No one shall be subjected to arbitrary arrest, detention or exile."* Not random arrests — arrests nobody has to justify.

### Where it comes from
Latin **arbiter** = *"judge, one called in to decide."* An *arbitrary* ruling is one resting on the arbiter's personal judgment alone — no rulebook above him.

That root pays out across the GRE: **arbiter** (the decider), **arbitrate** / **arbitration** (settle a dispute outside court), **arbitrary** (deciding by nothing but one's own will).

# **archaic** *(adj.)* — **ar-KAY-ik**

*The *ch* is a hard **k** — never "arch-AY-ic."*

### Means
Belonging to a much earlier age; **so old-fashioned it has dropped out of active use**.

The key isn't age, it's *disuse*. A cathedral is ancient, not archaic; the word **thou** is archaic. Something archaic has been superseded and now survives as a leftover — a law nobody repealed, a fax machine, a form of address nobody uses.

Tone flips with the target. Applied to a language or a historical period ("Archaic Greek"), it's a neutral technical label. Applied to an institution, a rule, or someone's opinions, it's a **put-down**: not just dated, but embarrassingly overdue for replacement.

⚠️ **archaic ≠ arcane.**
- **archaic** = *outdated*, no longer used
- **arcane** = *obscure*, understood by only a few

Arcane knowledge is hidden; archaic knowledge is just expired. A ritual can be both.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Archaic = archaeology minus the -ology** — if you'd need a dig to find it in use, it's archaic.

The word points at something buried and out of circulation, not merely old and still standing.

### In sentences
1. The committee spent a year sweeping **archaic** provisions from the statute book, including a ban on driving cattle through the town at noon.
2. Their expenses system still runs on paper forms — it's completely **archaic**.

### In the wild
Open any dictionary entry for **thou**, **whence**, or **betwixt** and you'll see the usage label *archaic* — that tag is the word doing its day job.

### Where it comes from
Greek **arkhaios** = *"ancient,"* from **arkhē** = *"beginning."*

That root spreads wide: **archaeology** (study of beginnings), **archetype** (the original pattern), **archive** (where the first records go) — and via *arkhein*, "to rule/lead," also **monarch**, **anarchy**, **hierarchy**.

# **clamorous** *(adj.)* — **KLAM-uh-ruhs**

*British spelling: clamourous / clamour. First syllable is "clam", not "clay".*

### Means
Loud and noisy — specifically the noise of many voices **making a demand**: protest, complaint, insistence.

The demand is what separates it from its neighbours. *Raucous* is rowdy for fun; *strident* is one harsh voice; *vociferous* is loud and forceful but can be a single person; **clamorous** is a mass of noise pressing for something — a crowd at the gates, the press outside a courthouse, a room of hands in the air. Mildly disapproving: the speaker usually finds the din excessive, even when the cause is fair. It extends to non-human noise (clamorous gulls, clamorous bells) and to metaphor (clamorous demands for reform).

**clamorous ≠ calamitous.** *Clamorous* = deafeningly noisy. *Calamitous* = disastrous. A clamorous meeting is merely loud; a calamitous one ends careers.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Clamorous** ≈ **claim**-orous — a crowd that isn't just loud, it's *claiming* something.

Same Latin root as *claim*, and it pins the nuance: clamorous noise always has a demand inside it.

### In sentences
1. The gallery grew **clamorous** as the verdict was read, and the judge threatened to clear the room.
2. Ten minutes into the outage the support channel was **clamorous** — everyone shouting the same question at once.

### In the wild
British news writes it constantly in noun form: *"a growing clamour for the minister's resignation."* Nobody ever clamours quietly — the word only comes out when a crowd wants something.

### Where it comes from
Latin **clāmāre** = *"to shout."* It hands you a whole shelf of words: **claim** (shout that it's yours), **exclaim**, **proclaim** (shout forth), **acclaim** (shout for), **declaim**, **reclaim** — and **clamour** itself, the shouting of many.

🚫 **Not *glamorous*.** That's Scots *glamour*, a corruption of *grammar* (once meaning occult learning). One letter apart, no relation.

# **dearth** *(n.)* — **DERTH**

*Rhymes with earth, not with dear — one syllable.*

### Means
A **scarcity** of something needed or wanted — too little of it to go round.

Not zero: a dearth is a *shortage*, where "absence" or "void" would mean none at all. It is formal, faintly literary, and almost always followed by *of* + an abstract noun — a dearth of evidence, of talent, of imagination. It also carries an implied judgement: the speaker thinks there *ought* to be more. **Lack** is flat and neutral; **dearth** editorialises.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dear**th — when something is scarce, it becomes **dear** (costly).

The word is literally *dear* + *-th*, the same suffix that turns *warm* into *warmth*, so a dearth is a state of pricey scarcity, not outright nothing.

### In sentences
1. The commission's report cited a **dearth** of reliable data on long-term outcomes.
2. There's a real **dearth** of decent coffee round here — it's petrol-station stuff or nothing.

### In the wild
It's a headline-writer's staple: "a dearth of affordable housing," "a dearth of qualified nurses." If you've read an op-ed complaining that there isn't enough of something, you've met it.

### Where it comes from
Old English **dēore** = *"costly, precious"* + the suffix **-th**, which turns an adjective into the noun for its state: *warm → warmth, long → length, true → truth, strong → strength*. A dearth was originally a time when grain was **dear** — scarce, so expensive. The price meaning faded; the scarcity stayed.

# **explicable** *(adj.)* — **EK-spli-kuh-buhl**

*Also stressed on the second syllable — ik-SPLIK-uh-buhl; both are standard.*

### Means
Capable of being explained or accounted for.

The word is quieter than it looks. **Explicable** doesn't mean *obvious* or *clear* — a thing can be baffling on the surface and still be explicable, because the claim is only that a satisfying account exists once you dig. It carries a faint tone of deflation: people reach for it to take the mystery *out* of something ("her behaviour was entirely explicable, given the pressure she was under"). Note also that the negative twin, *inexplicable*, is far commoner in speech; when the bare positive turns up, it's usually formal, argumentative, and defending a plain explanation against a dramatic one.

### Trick to lock it in
> You almost never meet it alone — it comes in as **"perfectly explicable"**, mopping up someone else's mystery.

Lock the collocation and you lock the tone: the word's job is to say *calm down, there's a reason*.

### In sentences
1. The discrepancy in the accounts, alarming at first sight, proved **explicable** as a rounding error carried forward across three quarters.
2. Sure, he stormed out — which is pretty **explicable** when you've been in that meeting for four hours.

### In the wild
Standard move in science and audit writing: a result is dismissed as **"explicable by chance alone"** — the polite way of saying *nothing to see here*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** (*out*) + **plicāre** (*to fold*) — to unfold something, spread the pleats flat so it can be read.

That fold-root is a GRE goldmine: **complicate** (fold together), **implicate** (fold someone in), **replicate** (fold back again), **pliable** (foldable), plus *deploy, display, employ* — all folding and unfolding in disguise.

# **hyperbole** *(n.)* — **hye-PER-buh-lee**

*Four syllables, never "HYE-per-bohl" — it ends "-ee", like catastrophe, epitome and Penelope.*

### Means
Deliberate, extravagant **exaggeration** used for effect and **not meant to be taken literally** — "I've told you a million times," "this bag weighs a ton."

Because the excess is out in the open, hyperbole isn't deception: nobody is supposed to believe it, so it sits closer to rhetoric than to lying. The word is faintly critical when you apply it to someone else's claim ("that's hyperbole") — you're saying they've overstated their case, not that they're dishonest. In literary or rhetorical talk it's fully neutral, just the name of a device.

⚠️ **hyperbole ≠ hyperbola.** *Hyperbole* is the exaggeration; a *hyperbola* is the open curve from geometry. Same Greek root, different subjects — and note **hyperbolic** does double duty for both.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Hyper**bole is **hyper** — thrown so far over the top that no one is meant to believe it.

The visible excess is the point, which is why hyperbole counts as style rather than dishonesty.

### In sentences
1. The senator's warning of "the end of civilisation itself" was **hyperbole**, and every reporter in the room knew it.
2. Calling a delayed train "the worst day of my life" is peak **hyperbole**, mate.

### In the wild
Bruno Mars, "Grenade" — *"I'd catch a grenade for ya"* — a whole chart-topping song built out of hyperbole.

### Where it comes from
Greek **hyper** (*over*) + **ballein** (*to throw*) = "to throw beyond." Overshoot the mark.

**ballein** is a quietly huge GRE root: **ballistic** (thrown), **symbol** (thrown together), **parable** and **parabola** (thrown alongside), **problem** (thrown in front of you), **emblem**, **metabolism**.

# **immutable** *(adj.)* — **ih-MYOO-tuh-buhl**

### Means
Unchangeable — not just *unchanged*, but **incapable of being changed**, by anyone, ever.

That's the whole force of the word. "Unchanging" describes what has happened so far; **immutable** describes what *cannot* happen. It carries a note of law, physics, or scripture — the laws of nature, the terms of a contract, God, death, the past. Calling something immutable is a claim about its nature, so it's often used ironically or bitterly about things people wrongly believed were fixed: "the supposedly immutable rules of the market."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Immutable = you can't *mutate* it.** The word is sitting right there in the middle.

Strip off *im-* ("not") and *-able*, and what's left is **mutate** — so an immutable thing is one no force can mutate.

### In sentences
1. The court held that the treaty's borders were **immutable**, no matter which party won the coming election.
2. Look, the deadline's **immutable** — I can't move it, my boss can't move it, stop asking.

### In the wild
Any programmer says this daily: Python strings and Git commits are **immutable** — you never edit one, you only make a new one. Same for blockchain's selling point: an immutable ledger.

### Where it comes from
Latin **mūtāre** = *"to change"* — one of the highest-yield GRE roots. It gives you **mutate**, **mutation**, **mutable** (the plain opposite: changeable, fickle), **permutation** (a rearrangement), **transmute** (change one substance into another — lead into gold), and **commute** (literally *change together* — hence changing a sentence to a lighter one, and changing your location daily).

# **indefatigable** *(adj.)* — **in-duh-FAT-i-guh-buhl**

### Means
Incapable of being tired out — able to keep going at something long after everyone else has flagged.

This is a compliment, and a fairly grand one. *Tireless* is plain description; **indefatigable** is admiration, usually for sustained effort over months or years rather than one long night — an indefatigable campaigner, researcher, advocate, fundraiser. Note what it isn't: not strength, not speed, not talent. It's **stamina of will**. It also carries a faint edge of "this person will not let the matter drop," so it can be used dryly about someone whose persistence you find exhausting.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in-de-FATIGUE-able** — the word *fatigue* is sitting right there in the middle, wearing a hat and a tail.

Strip the *in-* and the *-able* and you're left with fatigue: not-able-to-be-fatigued.

### In sentences
1. For forty years she was the **indefatigable** advocate of a cause almost no one else thought worth defending.
2. He's **indefatigable** — three flights, two time zones, and he still turned up to the 9am asking questions.

### In the wild
The Royal Navy has kept an **HMS Indefatigable** on the books since 1784 — Britain names warships for the quality it wants them to have (*Indomitable*, *Invincible*, *Dreadnought*): the ship that cannot be worn down.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fatigāre** = *"to tire out."* Add *in-* ("not") and *-able*: not tire-out-able.

Same root gives you **fatigue** (tiredness — and metal fatigue, where the metal itself gets worn out) and army **fatigues**, originally the clothes worn for tiring menial work.

# **indolent** *(adj.)* — **IN-duh-luhnt**

### Means
Habitually **averse to exertion** — lazy as a settled disposition rather than a single lapse.

*Lazy* is a blunt everyday accusation; **indolent** is the drawing-room version — cooler, more formal, and it describes a *temperament*, not a bad afternoon. It also carries a whiff of comfort and ease: the indolent man isn't struggling against sloth, he's perfectly content. Writers use it half-admiringly of heat, luxury and long summers (*an indolent stretch by the pool*), which *slothful* — with its sin-and-sermon weight — never allows.

**Medicine keeps a second sense:** an *indolent* tumour or ulcer is **slow-growing and causing little pain**.

⚠️ **indolent ≠ insolent.**
- **indolent** = won't get up
- **insolent** = mouths off when he does

One syllable apart, opposite vices: indolence is doing nothing, insolence is disrespect.

### Trick to lock it in
> **In-dolent = no pain** — he won't take pains, and he feels none about it.

The Latin *dolor* ("pain") sits inside the word, which is why indolence is lazy *without guilt* — and why a doctor's "indolent" growth is the painless, slow one.

### In sentences
1. Two generations of **indolent** heirs had spent the fortune without once adding to it.
2. He's not tired, he's just **indolent** — the man has never voluntarily stood up.

### In the wild
Oncologists say it every day: **indolent lymphoma** — the slow, quiet kind you monitor rather than attack. Same idea as the lazy heir, just applied to cells.

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** (*not*) + **dolēre** (*to feel pain, to grieve*) → *indolens*, "**feeling no pain**." Originally medical; the "lazy" sense came from *not taking pains*.

That root pays out elsewhere: **condolence** (grieving *with* you), **doleful**, **dolorous**, and the name **Dolores** (Our Lady of Sorrows).

# **insular** *(adj.)* — **IN-suh-ler**

*Also heard as IN-syuh-ler in British English.*

### Means
Literally **of an island**; on the GRE, almost always the figurative sense: **cut off from outside influence, and narrow because of it** — interested only in one's own circle, incurious about anything beyond it.

The word attacks *range*, not intelligence. A brilliant scholar can be insular. It also carries a hint of **complacency** — the insular don't feel deprived by their isolation; they think their small world is the whole world. Cooler and more clinical than *bigoted* or *provincial*: it diagnoses a condition rather than throwing an insult, which is why it survives in serious writing about communities, academic fields and countries.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Insul**ar minds are **insul**ated — nothing from outside gets in.

Insulation isn't hostile, it's just sealed — which is exactly the temperature of the word: not aggression, but a closed system that never lets a foreign idea through.

### In sentences
1. The department had grown **insular**, citing only its own faculty and dismissing work published anywhere else.
2. Her hometown is lovely but pretty **insular** — three generations of the same families, and everyone else is a tourist.

### Where it comes from
Latin **insula** = *"island."* An island is land with the world held at arm's length — that's the whole word.

One root, a useful family: **isle** and **isolate** (to make into an island), **insulate** (wrap it off from its surroundings), **peninsula** (*paene* "almost" + *insula* = *almost an island*), and even **insulin** — named for the *islets* of Langerhans, little islands of cells in the pancreas.

# **intransigent** *(adj.)* — **in-TRAN-si-juhnt**

*The middle is soft: *-si-juhnt*, not *-sy-gent*.*

### Means
Refusing absolutely to compromise, negotiate, or shift position.

This is stubbornness **at the bargaining table**. *Obstinate* describes a temperament; *intransigent* describes a stance — someone who has been offered terms and will not move an inch. It carries a political, diplomatic weight (unions, parties, peace talks) and usually a note of exasperation from whoever is trying to reach a deal. Occasionally it flips admiring — an intransigent defender of principle — but the default is a complaint.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in- + trans-**: he will not **cross over** — there is no **transaction** to be had.

The word hides *transact*, so it isn't general grumpiness — it's the specific refusal to do a deal.

### In sentences
1. Three weeks of mediation collapsed against an **intransigent** minority that would not concede a single clause.
2. He's **intransigent** about the seating plan — I've offered him four versions and he just says no.

### In the wild
It's newsroom boilerplate for stalled talks: *\"both sides remained intransigent\"* is the standard line in reporting on strikes, ceasefires, and budget standoffs.

### Where it comes from
Latin **transigere** = *"to drive through to a settlement"* (*trans-* across + *agere* to drive) — the same *agere* behind **agent**, **agenda**, **act**, **exigent**. **Transaction** is a deal driven through; **in-transigent** is the man who won't.

The modern word arrived via Spanish *los intransigentes*, the radical faction of 1873 who refused to enter any coalition — which is why it still smells of politics.

# **intrepid** *(adj.)* — **in-TREP-id**

### Means
Fearless — but specifically **unshaken by danger, hardship or the unknown**, and staying that way over time.

*Brave* describes one act; *intrepid* describes a temperament. It belongs to people who go somewhere difficult and keep going: explorers, war correspondents, mountaineers, whistleblowers. Unlike *foolhardy* or *reckless*, it carries no hint of stupidity — the nerve is admired, not scolded. And it's often used with an affectionate half-smile ("our **intrepid** reporter braved the queue at the DMV"), which no other word in this family does.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nobody is intrepid sitting on the sofa** — the word demands a noun that's *going somewhere*: intrepid explorer, intrepid reporter, intrepid traveller.

Lock it to the journey and you get the nuance for free: sustained nerve heading into the unknown, not one flash of courage.

### In sentences
1. Shackleton's **intrepid** crew spent two winters on the ice and lost not a single man.
2. Ten minutes into the hike it started hailing, and our **intrepid** leader just pulled his hood up and kept walking.

### In the wild
The **USS Intrepid**, the WWII aircraft carrier now moored on the Hudson as New York's *Intrepid Sea, Air & Space Museum* — navies name ships for the quality they want in the crew.

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** (*not*) + **trepidus** (*alarmed, trembling*). Literally **"not trembling."**

That gives you the pair free: **trepidation** = the trembling, **intrepid** = the absence of it. If you feel trepidation about the exam, the intrepid candidate next to you does not.

# **irreverent** *(adj.)* — **ir-REV-uh-runt**

### Means
Showing a **lack of due respect for things other people treat as sacred** — religion, authority, tradition, national heroes, the dead.

The tone is usually **cheeky rather than hostile**. *Disrespectful* is a complaint; *blasphemous* is a charge; *irreverent* is very often a compliment — the irreverent comedian, the irreverent obituary, the irreverent guide to Shakespeare. It implies wit and nerve, someone who knows exactly which altar they're refusing to kneel at and is enjoying it. Turn it negative only by context: irreverence at a funeral is a failure of manners, not a style.

⚠️ **irreverent ≠ irrelevant.** Near-identical on the page and a classic misread.
- **irreverent** = *disrespectful, cheeky*
- **irrelevant** = *beside the point*

An irreverent remark can be devastatingly relevant.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Irreverent won't kneel — but it's grinning, not snarling.**

The word marks refusal of reverence, and that refusal is played for wit far more often than for insult.

### In sentences
1. His **irreverent** history of the monarchy treats four centuries of kings as a sequence of badly behaved houseguests.
2. Don't take her to the memorial — she gets **irreverent** the second things turn solemn.

### In the wild
**Deadpool** is the word's home turf — every review reached for *irreverent*, because the film mocks the sacred cows of superhero cinema while obviously loving them. That affectionate-mockery mix is the word exactly.

### Where it comes from
Latin **verērī** = *"to stand in awe of, to fear."* Add *re-* (intensive) and you get **revere** — and the whole family: **reverence**, **reverent**, **reverend** (literally *"one who must be revered"*), **revered**. Stick *ir-* on the front and you have the person who won't.

🚫 **Not *relevant*.** That's Latin *relevāre*, *"to raise up, lighten"* — same root as *elevate, alleviate, lever, levity*. Different word entirely.

# **loathe** *(v.)* — **LOHDH**

*One syllable, and the ending is voiced — *dh* as in **this**, not *th* as in **thin**.*

### Means
To hate something **intensely**, with disgust — hatred that comes with a physical recoil.

*Dislike* is a preference; *detest* is a cold, considered verdict; **loathe** is visceral. It puts revulsion in the room: the thing is not just bad, it's repellent, and you want it away from you. Because it's so strong, it's also the standard verb for comic exaggeration — nobody says "I detest Mondays," but everybody loathes them.

⚠️ **loathe ≠ loath.**
- **loathe** *(verb, voiced)* = to hate with disgust
- **loath** *(adjective, unvoiced)* = **reluctant**, unwilling — *"I am loath to interrupt."*

She was **loath** to speak to the man she **loathed**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **breath → breathe, cloth → clothe, loath → loathe.** The silent **e** stretches the vowel, voices the ending, and turns it into a verb.

If it ends in **-the**, it's an action you *do* — and loathing is very much something you do to a thing.

### In sentences
1. He came to **loathe** the ceremonial dinners — the toasts, the flattery, the practised warmth of men who despised one another.
2. I **loathe** coriander. It tastes like soap and I will die on this hill.

### In the wild
*Wicked* — Galinda and Elphaba's duet **"What Is This Feeling?"**, whose chorus is nothing but the word: *"Loathing — unadulterated loathing."* Two roommates, instant mutual revulsion. That's the exact temperature of the word.

### Where it comes from
Old English **lāð** = *"hateful, hostile"* → the verb **lāðian**, to hate.

That one root splits into the pair that trips people up: **loathsome** (disgusting) and **loathe** kept the hatred, while the adjective **loath** kept only the milder old sense of *being set against* something — hence "reluctant."

# **malign** *(v. / adj.)* — **muh-LINE**

*The g is silent — it rhymes with *line*. (It comes back in **malignant**: muh-LIG-nunt.)*

### Means
**Verb:** to speak evil of someone — to say damaging, hostile things about them, usually behind their back and usually unfairly.
**Adjective:** evil in nature or effect — *a malign influence*.

Criticism is aimed at the work; **maligning** is aimed at the person, and it's cumulative — a steady drip of ill-speaking rather than one bad review. Unlike *slander*, it carries no legal machinery; unlike *vilify*, it doesn't have to be loud. And it leans hard toward the undeserved: when someone is maligned, the sentence usually implies they didn't have it coming.

⚠️ **malign ≠ malinger.**
- **malign** = *badmouth* someone
- **malinger** = *fake illness* to dodge work

Both start *mal-* and both are GRE regulars; only one involves a fictitious backache.

### Trick to lock it in
> **MAL + ALIGN** — you quietly *align* everyone against him.

The word literally hides *align*, which catches the slow, behind-the-back work of turning a room's opinion bad.

### In sentences
1. Historians have **maligned** the last emperor for two centuries, largely on the word of the men who deposed him.
2. Don't **malign** the guy — he actually covered your shift twice last month.

### In the wild
Sports and tech writing runs on one phrase: **\"the much-maligned midfielder,\"** *\"the much-maligned Vista.\"* Notice you never read *rightly maligned* — that \"much-\" is doing quiet defence work, hinting the pile-on was unfair.

### Where it comes from
Latin **malignus** = *malus* ("bad") + the root of *gignere* ("to be born") — literally **"born bad."** Its exact twin is **benign**, "born good."

*Malus* is a GRE goldmine: **malice**, **malevolent**, **malady**, **malfeasance**, **dismal**, even **malaria** (*mal'aria*, "bad air").

# **malleable** *(adj.)* — **MAL-ee-uh-buhl**

### Means
Of metal: able to be **hammered or pressed into new shapes** without cracking. Of people, minds, or opinions: **easily shaped by outside influence.**

The shaping is always done *to* the thing by someone else. *Flexible* and *adaptable* credit the person with adjusting on their own; **malleable** hands the credit to whoever is holding the hammer. So it runs warm when the material is young and unformed (a child's malleable mind, a malleable early draft) and cold when the shaper has an agenda — a *malleable* board member, a *malleable* witness, is a person being used.

### Trick to lock it in
> **MALLE**able = **MALLET**-able — soft enough that a mallet decides its shape.

The mallet is the point: something else swings, and the malleable thing takes whatever form it's given.

### In sentences
1. The new recruits were deliberately kept exhausted and isolated, the better to render them **malleable**.
2. He's a great first hire — smart, no bad habits yet, totally **malleable**.

### In the wild
Gold leaf on domes and picture frames exists because gold is the most **malleable** metal there is — a goldbeater can hammer a single gram out to a sheet you can see daylight through.

### Where it comes from
Latin **malleus** = *"hammer."* Same root gives you **mallet**, **maul**, and the **malleus** — the little hammer-shaped bone in your middle ear.

🚫 **The *mal-* here is not "bad."** *Malevolent, malign, malice* come from Latin *malus* (evil); *malleable* is pure hammer, no menace.

# **neophyte** *(n.)* — **NEE-uh-fyte**

*Stress falls hard on the front; not "nee-OFF-it."*

### Means
A **beginner** — specifically someone **newly admitted** to a faith, field, craft or organisation.

The weight sits on *newly arrived*, not merely *unskilled*. A **novice** is the plain institutional term (a novice nun, a novice driver); an **amateur** is about not being paid; a **neophyte** has just crossed a threshold and been let in, and is being watched by the people already inside. It's a bookish word, and it usually carries a faint condescension from the initiated — you write that someone is a neophyte, you rarely say it to his face.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-phyte** sounds like **fight**: the neophyte is **new to the fight** — just enlisted, not yet bloodied.

He's been signed up and admitted, which is exactly the point — the ignorance is *inside* the group, not outside it.

### In sentences
1. Among the veteran traders on the floor she was a **neophyte**, tolerated but not yet consulted.
2. Don't hand the new guy the Thanksgiving turkey — he's a total **neophyte** in the kitchen.

### In the wild
**Neo** in *The Matrix* — Greek *neos*, "new," and the entire film is his initiation: unplugged, taken in by people who already know, and told he understands nothing yet. That's a neophyte in one character.

### Where it comes from
Greek **neos** (*new*) + **phyton** (*plant*) — literally **"newly planted."** Early Christian writers used it for the fresh convert: a seedling just put in the ground, roots not yet taken.

Both halves pay off elsewhere: **neo-** gives *neologism, neonatal, Neolithic*; **-phyte** gives *epiphyte, zoophyte, phytoplankton*.

# **plastic** *(adj. (also n.))* — **PLAS-tik**

### Means
**Adj.:** capable of being **shaped or moulded** — and, of a person or mind, **easily influenced, impressionable**.

Forget the noun. On the GRE, *plastic* almost never means the material in your kitchen drawer; it means the *property* that material was named for. A plastic substance takes a new shape and **keeps** it. A plastic young mind absorbs whatever it's given.

Tone shifts with the subject: applied to brains, childhood, or an art form, it's neutral or admiring (*a plastic medium*). Applied to someone's convictions, it turns faintly contemptuous — *plastic* implies someone else's hands did the moulding. A separate colloquial sense is purely derogatory: fake, mass-produced, insincere — *a plastic smile*.

⚠️ **plastic ≠ elastic.** An **elastic** thing springs *back*; a **plastic** thing stays *bent*. Bend a ruler and let go — elastic. Bend a spoon — plastic.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Plasticine** — the modelling clay. *Plastic* is the clay stage, not the finished toy.

The word names the moment when something can still be pressed into any shape at all.

### In sentences
1. The infant brain is remarkably **plastic**; pathways severed by injury are often rerouted within months.
2. He's got no politics of his own — he's **plastic**, he just agrees with whoever spoke last.

### In the wild
**Plastic surgery** has nothing to do with plastic — the name predates the material. It's *moulding* surgery: reshaping flesh.

### Where it comes from
Greek **plassein** = *"to mould, to form."* The material got the name because it's mouldable — the adjective came first.

Same root: **plasma** (formed substance), **protoplasm**, **plaster**, **plastique** (mouldable explosive), and **-plasty** in *rhinoplasty*.

# **platitude** *(n.)* — **PLAT-i-tood**

*British: PLAT-i-tyood.*

### Means
A **flat, stale remark offered as though it were profound** — "everything happens for a reason," "at the end of the day, we're all human."

The sting isn't staleness alone; it's the **pretence of depth**. A cliché is just worn out and can be harmless. A platitude wears the face of wisdom, usually with a moral tint, and asks you to nod along. Calling something a platitude is an accusation: you had nothing to say, so you said something that sounded wise. It's the standard charge against politicians' condolences and corporate mission statements.

⚠️ **platitude ≠ plenitude.** *Plenitude* is **abundance, fullness** (*plenty*) — a compliment. A platitude is empty; a plenitude is full.

### Trick to lock it in
> **PLAT- is flat.** It sounds deep, but it lands flat.

The word literally carries *flat* inside it, which is exactly the gap it names — profound in delivery, flat in content.

### In sentences
1. The eulogy dissolved into **platitudes** about journeys and better places, saying nothing the man had actually been.
2. Spare me the **platitudes** — just tell me whether I still have a job.

### In the wild
Every corporate values slide — *"We put people first," "We embrace change"* — is the natural habitat of the platitude; that's the word critics reach for the moment a leader says a lot and means nothing.

### Where it comes from
French **plat** = *"flat"* (as in a **flat**tering nothing), built to rhyme with *attitude*. Same root runs through **plate, platter, plateau, platform, plaza** — and **platypus**, Greek for *"flat-foot."* Spot *plat-* and think flat.

# **prescient** *(adj.)* — **PRESH-uhnt**

*Also heard as PREE-shee-uhnt; both are accepted. The noun is prescience (PRESH-uhns).*

### Means
Knowing — or appearing to know — what is going to happen before it happens; showing unusual foresight.

The praise here is for **analysis, not magic**. *Prophetic* and *clairvoyant* carry a whiff of visions and the supernatural; *prescient* credits someone who read the evidence early and read it right. It attaches to memos, essays, warnings, films, investments — sober things.

It is also almost always **retrospective**. You don't call a forecast prescient while it's still a forecast; you call it prescient once reality has caught up and vindicated it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pre-sci-ent** = *pre-science*: you had the knowledge early — but the compliment only arrives late.

The *sci* is the same *sci* as in *science* and *omniscient*, and nobody is ever called prescient until events have proved them right.

### In sentences
1. His 1998 memo warning that the bank's models understated correlated risk now reads as unnervingly **prescient**.
2. Mum was **prescient** about that guy — she called it in week one and we all laughed.

### In the wild
This is the word film critics reach for when a story turns out to have predicted real life — *Network*, *The Truman Show*, half the *Black Mirror* episodes. If a review says a film "feels prescient," it means the world went and proved it right.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae-** (*before*) + **scīre** (*to know*) — literally "knowing beforehand."

*Scīre* is a workhorse root: **science**, **conscience** (knowing-with yourself), **omniscient** (all-knowing), **conscious**, and the GRE-friendly **nescient** (*not* knowing — ignorant).

# **pristine** *(adj.)* — **pris-TEEN**

*Stress the second syllable: pris-TEEN, not PRIS-teen (you'll hear the front-stressed version in casual American speech, but the exam-safe one is pris-TEEN).*

### Means
In its **original, unspoiled condition** — untouched, undamaged, as good as the day it was made; hence also **spotlessly clean**.

The key is *history*, not appearance. **Immaculate** means no stain on it; **pristine** means nothing ever happened to it. A jacket scrubbed for three hours is immaculate; a jacket still in the box is pristine. That's why the word attaches so naturally to wilderness, coastlines, snowfall, and second-hand goods — things whose value lies in never having been used, walked on, or touched.

### Trick to lock it in
> pris-**TEEN** rhymes with *clean* and *unseen* — pristine is clean **because** it's been unseen.

Nobody has handled it, so there was never anything to clean off — that's the difference between pristine and merely tidy.

### In sentences
1. The expedition mapped a **pristine** stretch of reef, untouched by trawlers and unvisited by divers.
2. He kept the record sleeve **pristine** — never even slid it out of the plastic.

### In the wild
Every used-car ad and eBay listing that says **"pristine condition, one owner"** — the seller is claiming the thing has no history, not that they gave it a good wash.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prīstinus** = *"former, earliest, original"*, built on **pri-** *"before"*. English borrowed it in the 1500s meaning simply *original*, and only later drifted to *clean* — which is why "pristine" still carries "as it was at the start."

Same **pri-** family: **prior, prime, primal, primitive, primary**.

# **reproach** *(v. / n.)* — **ri-PROHCH**

### Means
**Verb:** to express disapproval to someone for a fault or failure. **Noun:** that expression of blame — or the disgrace attached to it.

The feeling underneath is **hurt, not authority**. A boss rebukes you; a mother, a friend, a conscience *reproaches* you — quietly, with the injury showing. It carries "you let me down" rather than "you broke a rule," which is why a reproach can be delivered by a look and nothing else.

The noun survives mostly in the frozen phrase **beyond reproach** — so clean that no one can find anything to blame you for.

⚠️ **reproach ≠ reprove.** *Reproach* is emotional — the speaker is wounded. *Reprove* is corrective — the speaker is fixing your behaviour. You reproach a friend for forgetting; a teacher reproves a pupil for talking.

### Trick to lock it in
> **re-PROACH** — you *approach* the person with their fault and hold it right up to their face.

The word literally hides *approach*, and that closeness is the point: a reproach is personal and delivered face-to-face, never issued from on high.

### In sentences
1. His diaries **reproach** no one but himself, page after page of quiet self-accusation.
2. Don't give me that look — I know, I know, spare me the **reproach**.

### In the wild
**"Beyond reproach"** is the phrase you meet in references, obituaries and press statements — *"her conduct in office was beyond reproach."* If you can only remember one collocation, remember that one; it's how the noun actually appears.

### Where it comes from
Old French **reprochier**, from Latin **re-** (back) + **prope** (*near*) — to bring a thing back near someone, to cast it up in their face.

That root pays off elsewhere: **approach**, **proximity**, **approximate**, **propinquity** (nearness, a GRE regular), **rapprochement** (a coming-back-together of nations).

🚫 **Not related to *reprobate*** (a depraved scoundrel), which is Latin *reprobare*, "to reject" — same prefix, different word.

# **robust** *(adj.)* — **roh-BUST**

*Stress on the second syllable; some British speakers say ROH-bust.*

### Means
Strong and sturdy — but specifically **strong enough to keep working under stress**: a robust body, a robust economy, a robust argument, a robust system.

The word is about *withstanding*, not about size or force. A weightlifter is *powerful*; a system that survives bad input is *robust*. Nor is it *rigid* — a robust thing bends and keeps going where a brittle thing snaps. On the GRE it usually turns up in technical or economic prose (robust growth, robust findings, a robust defence of a theory), where it's a quiet compliment: this thing has been tested and held.

Two spillover senses worth knowing: of flavour, **full-bodied and strong** (robust coffee, a robust red wine); of debate or exercise, **vigorous, hearty, no-holds-barred**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Robust = won't *bust*.**

The word literally ends in *bust*, and that's exactly the test — pile pressure on a robust thing and it refuses to break.

### In sentences
1. The committee demanded a **robust** methodology — results that would survive being run again with different data.
2. He's eighty and still chopping wood; the man's absurdly **robust**.

### In the wild
Coffee packaging. Every bag promising a "rich, **robust** flavour" is selling you the full-bodied sense — strong, dark, no thinness to it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **rōbur** = *"oak"*, and by extension *"strength, hardness"*. **Rōbustus** meant literally *"oaken"* — built of oak, hence tough.

Same root gives you **corroborate**: *con-* (together) + *rōborāre* (to strengthen) — to strengthen a claim by putting more evidence beside it.

# **salubrious** *(adj.)* — **suh-LOO-bree-uhs**

### Means
**Health-giving** — good for the body, said above all of *places*: air, climate, sea breezes, a mountain village, a spa town.

It's a word about **environments, not habits**. A diet is healthy; a coastline is salubrious. The register is genteel and slightly old-fashioned, which is why it survives most vigorously in the negative: *"a less than salubrious part of town", "an insalubrious hotel"* — and there it has quietly slid from *unhealthy* to *seedy, disreputable*. So the positive sense praises the air, and the negative sense insults the neighbourhood.

⚠️ **salubrious ≠ salutary.**
- **salubrious** = physically wholesome — *air, climate, surroundings*
- **salutary** = producing a good *effect*, usually a corrective one — *a salutary lesson, a salutary shock*

Salubrious feels pleasant. Salutary often doesn't — it's the unpleasant thing that does you good.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Salu**brious shares its front end with **salute** — and a salute is a wish for your health.

A salute only *wishes* you well; salubrious surroundings actually do the work.

### In sentences
1. Victorian doctors sent consumptive patients to Davos for its cold, dry, **salubrious** air.
2. The rent was cheap, but let's just say the area wasn't the most **salubrious**.

### In the wild
Estate agents and travel writing keep it alive: a listing boasts of a **salubrious** neighbourhood, and a reviewer damns a bar as *"less than salubrious"* — the same word doing praise and sneer.

### Where it comes from
Latin **salūs, salūtis** = *"health, safety, well-being."* One root, a useful family: **salute** and **salutation** (originally *wishing someone health*), **salutary** (health-producing), and the toast in Italian — *salute!*

# **sanction** *(v. / n.)* — **SANK-shuhn**

### Means
**Verb:** to **officially approve** or authorise. **Noun (1):** that official approval. **Noun (2):** a **penalty** imposed to force compliance — usually plural: *sanctions*.

It is its own opposite, and the GRE loves that. Both senses come from the same idea: an authority laying down a binding rule, and either blessing what fits it or punishing what doesn't.

Register matters more than you'd think. *Sanction* is never casual approval — a friend doesn't sanction your haircut. It implies a body with the standing to permit: a government, a church, a governing council, a licensing board. And when it bites, it's coercive rather than merely punitive — sanctions are meant to *change behaviour*, not just express displeasure.

### Trick to lock it in
> Singular verb = permission; plural noun = punishment. *He sanctioned the raid* / *They imposed sanctions.*

Grammar disambiguates the contronym far more reliably than context does — if you see "sanctions" sitting there as a plural noun, it's the penalty sense almost every time.

### In sentences
1. The committee declined to **sanction** the expedition, judging the route too hazardous to bear the institution's name.
2. Trade **sanctions** kicked in overnight and suddenly nobody could ship anything.

### In the wild
Any news bulletin about Russia, Iran or North Korea — "fresh **sanctions**" is one of the most-spoken phrases in international news, and it's always the punishment sense.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sanctus** = *"holy, made binding"* (from *sancīre*, to consecrate). A sanction was originally a decree made sacred — untouchable, enforceable.

That root pays for itself: **saint**, **sacred**, **sanctuary**, **sanctify**, **sacrosanct**, and the GRE favourite **sanctimonious** (holier-than-thou).

# **sedulous** *(adj.)* — **SEJ-uu-luhs**

*Also heard as SED-yuu-luhs; the first syllable is never "seed."*

### Means
Diligent in a **steady, painstaking, keep-at-it** way — persistent care applied over time.

The word praises *stamina and attention to detail*, not brilliance or speed. A sedulous researcher checks every footnote; a sedulous student grinds through the list nightly. It's bookish and slightly old-fashioned, so it carries a whiff of the patient plodder — nearly a synonym for *assiduous*, but where *industrious* suggests energy and output, **sedulous** suggests unhurried thoroughness.

⚠️ **sedulous ≠ sedentary.** They look like relatives and mean near-opposites: *sedentary* = sitting around, barely moving; *sedulous* = working away without letting up. Unrelated roots.

### Trick to lock it in
> **SEJ**-uu-luhs — the same sound that sits inside **schedule** (SKEJ-uul): the sedulous one just works the schedule, day after day.

It's not flair, it's turning up and doing the hours.

### In sentences
1. Decades of **sedulous** cataloguing by a single curator turned a shed of loose papers into a national archive.
2. She's not the smartest in the class, but she's **sedulous** — three hours a night, every night, no exceptions.

### In the wild
Robert Louis Stevenson said he learned to write by **"playing the sedulous ape"** — patiently copying Hazlitt, Lamb and the rest until the style stuck. The phrase survives as the standard tag for painstaking imitation.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sēdulus** = *"sincere, diligent,"* from **sē dolō** — *"without guile."* Honest work, no shortcuts: the sense travelled from *not cheating* to *taking real pains*.

🚫 **Not from *sedēre*, "to sit"** — that root gives *sedentary, sediment, session, reside*. Sedulous only looks like it belongs to that family.

# **soporific** *(adj. / n.)* — **SOP-uh-RIF-ik**

*First syllable rhymes with top, not soap.*

### Means
**Adj.:** sleep-inducing, drowsy-making. **Noun:** a drug that puts you to sleep.

Two lives in one word. In medicine it's flat and technical — a soporific is a sedative, no judgement attached. Everywhere else it's a polite weapon: calling a lecture, a novel, or a minister's speech *soporific* is a dry, faintly amused way of saying it was unbearably dull, without ever stooping to the word "boring." Register is bookish and a little arch, which is exactly why it stings.

### Trick to lock it in
> **So poor** a speech, it's ter**rific** at knocking you out.

The sound of the word hands you both halves — *so-por-ific* — and the sarcasm of "terrific" matches the word's genteel-insult tone.

### In sentences
1. The court's judgment, some four hundred pages of procedural history, had a **soporific** effect on all but the most dutiful reader.
2. Two chapters of that thing and I was gone — genuinely **soporific**, better than melatonin.

### In the wild
Beatrix Potter, *The Tale of the Flopsy Bunnies*: "It is said that the effect of eating too much lettuce is *soporific*." The rabbits promptly fall asleep in the grass — the word's most famous outing, and in a children's book.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sopor** = *"deep sleep"* + **-ficus** (*facere*, "to make") — literally *sleep-making*.

The **-fic** half is the reusable part: **terrific** (fear-making), **horrific**, **prolific**, **beneficent**, **soporific**. Spot *-fic* and you're looking at a word that *makes* something.

# **stern** *(adj. / n.)* — **STERN**

### Means
**Adj.:** severe, unsmiling, unwilling to bend — of a person, a face, a rule, or a warning.
**Noun:** the back end of a ship (an unrelated word that happens to be spelled the same).

Strictness plus a grim expression: *strict* describes the rules you enforce, *stern* describes the face you enforce them with. It sits below *harsh* and *cruel* on the scale, and usually carries authority rather than malice — a stern parent, a stern judge, a stern rebuke — so it can even be faint praise, implying someone who holds a line others would let slide. Say a face is stern and you mean it gives nothing away and forgives nothing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Stern** rhymes with **learn** — a stern look isn't cruelty, it's a lesson.

The rhyme keeps the disciplinary edge attached: sternness is severity aimed at correcting you, not at hurting you.

### In sentences
1. The committee issued a **stern** rebuke to the minister, stopping just short of demanding his resignation.
2. Mum gave me one **stern** look across the table and I put the phone away.

### In the wild
The most-replayed shot in *Titanic*: the **stern** rearing straight up out of the water before the ship goes down — that's the noun, the rear end of the hull.

### Where it comes from
Two different words wearing the same coat.

**Adjective:** Old English **styrne** = *"rigid, severe"* — from the same root family as **stare**, the fixed, unblinking look. Stern people don't move their faces.

**Noun:** Old Norse **stjórn** = *"steering"* — the rear is where the steering gear lived. Same root gives **steer** and **starboard** (*steer-board*, the side the oar hung on).

# **tendentious** *(adj.)* — **ten-DEN-shuhs**

### Means
Written or argued with a **deliberate slant** — designed to promote a particular cause or conclusion rather than to inform fairly.

The word attaches to *things*, not temperaments: a tendentious history, a tendentious footnote, a tendentious survey question. And it carries a specific accusation — the piece **poses as neutral analysis** while quietly steering you. Calling something *biased* points at the author's blind spot; calling it *tendentious* says the slant was engineered. It's the standard weapon of the academic book review: polite in sound, lethal in effect.

**tendentious ≠ contentious.**
- **tendentious** = *slanted* toward a cause
- **contentious** = *causing arguments*, or itself quarrelsome

A tendentious report is one-sided; a contentious one starts a fight. A dull, careful paper can be tendentious, and a scrupulously fair one can be contentious.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tendentious** has a **tendency** built into it — the argument is leaning before the evidence arrives.

Same root, same meaning: it *tends* one way on purpose.

### In sentences
1. Reviewers dismissed the biography as **tendentious** — every ambiguity in the archive resolved, implausibly, in the subject's favour.
2. "Do you still think the manager is doing a bad job?" is a **tendentious** question and you know it.

### In the wild
Wikipedia has an actual conduct policy called **"Tendentious editing"** — for editors who technically follow the rules while bending every article toward one side.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tendere** = *"to stretch, to aim."* A tendentious text is aimed — pointed at a conclusion in advance.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **tendency**, **tension**, **extend**, **intend**, **contend**, **portend**, **pretend**, and **tent** (stretched cloth).
