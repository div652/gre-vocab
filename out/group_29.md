# **abscond** *(v.)* — **ab-SKOND**

### Means
To **leave secretly and in a hurry** — typically to dodge arrest, and typically with money or property that isn't yours.

This isn't plain fleeing. *Flee* can be innocent (people flee fires); *abscond* presumes guilt. The word carries a whiff of the courtroom and the ledger: bail is skipped, a treasurer vanishes, a trustee disappears with the fund. It also usually takes **with** — you abscond *with* something. Someone who merely runs away has escaped; someone who absconds has escaped **and taken the cash**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ABS**ent + **CON** — the con man goes absent, and so does the money.

The two syllables hand you both halves of the meaning: the disappearance and the crime behind it.

### In sentences
1. The firm's treasurer **absconded** to a jurisdiction with no extradition treaty, taking eleven years of client deposits with him.
2. My flatmate **absconded** with the deposit and left me to explain it to the landlord.

### In the wild
It's live legal vocabulary, not just exam vocabulary: parole boards and bail courts formally class someone who skips supervision as an **absconder**, and that's the word that shows up in the news when a fraudster boards a flight.

### Where it comes from
Latin **abscondere** = *ab-* (away) + *condere* (to stow, to hide) — literally *to stash yourself away*.

Same *condere* gives **recondite**, "hidden away, obscure" — the knowledge equivalent of absconding.

# **apogee** *(n.)* — **AP-uh-jee**

*Soft *g*, as in *gee whiz* — not "AP-oh-ghee".*

### Means
Literally, the point in an orbit **farthest from the Earth**. Figuratively, the **highest point** something reaches — of a career, an empire, a style, a reputation.

What sets it apart from *peak* or *height* is the arc. An apogee is the top of a **trajectory**: something climbed to get there, and gravity is already pulling it back. Say a band's 1997 album was their apogee and you've implied the decline without stating it. Register is formal and slightly grand — it belongs in essays and obituaries, not in conversation about your Tuesday.

⚠️ **apogee ≠ perigee.** *Apogee* = farthest out; *perigee* = closest in. A "supermoon" is a **perigee** moon — near, so it looks huge.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ap**ogee sounds like **ap**ex — but an apex is the top of a *shape*, an apogee the top of a *flight*.

The sound gets you to "highest point"; the difference reminds you that an apogee is reached by travelling, and travel continues downward.

### In sentences
1. The Mughal empire reached its **apogee** under Akbar; every reign after it was a slow subtraction.
2. He peaked in the second year — that promotion was the **apogee**, and he knows it.

### In the wild
Watch any rocket launch stream — SpaceX, Blue Origin, ISRO — and the commentators call out **apogee** as the moment the vehicle stops climbing and starts coming back down.

### Where it comes from
Greek **apo-** = *"away from"* + **gaia / gē** = *"earth."* Literally *"away from the earth."*

Both halves pay rent: **apo-** gives you *apostasy* (standing away from the faith), *apostrophe*, *apathy*; **gē** gives you *geology, geography, geometry* — and **perigee**, *peri-* = "near, around," as in *perimeter*.

# **aspersion** *(n.)* — **uh-SPER-zhuhn**

*Also heard as uh-SPER-shuhn; both are standard.*

### Means
A **damaging insinuation** — a remark meant to sully someone's reputation or integrity.

It almost never travels alone or in the singular: you **cast aspersions on** someone. And the word is usually deployed *defensively*, by the person denying it — "I cast no aspersions on my colleague's honesty" — which is precisely how the doubt gets planted. An accusation is out in the open and can be answered; an aspersion is a smear, sprinkled sideways, too vague to rebut. Formal, faintly legal, and always hostile.

⚠️ **aspersion ≠ asperity.**
- **aspersion** = a **slur** on someone's character
- **asperity** = **harshness** of tone or manner

He answered with *asperity*; he answered by casting *aspersions*. Unrelated roots, one letter apart at a glance.

### Trick to lock it in
> **a-sper-*shun*** — sprinkle enough dirt and people start to **shun** him.

The buried "shun" carries the point: no single remark accuses him of anything, but the accumulated sprinkling is what makes people back away.

### In sentences
1. The defence objected that counsel was casting **aspersions** on a witness who had not yet been heard.
2. Look, I'm not casting **aspersions** on your cooking — I just noticed the smoke alarm going off.

### In the wild
Live in courtroom and parliamentary speech, where "I cast no aspersions on the honourable member" is the standard way to insult someone while technically staying polite.

### Where it comes from
Latin **aspergere** = *"to sprinkle on"* (*ad-* "on" + *spargere* "to scatter"). The original English sense was literal — the priest's **aspersion** of holy water. The mud-slinging sense is the same gesture with worse contents.

*spargere* also gives you **sparse** (thinly scattered), **disperse**, and **intersperse**.

# **bawdy** *(adj.)* — **BAW-dee**

### Means
Indecent about sex — but *comically* so. Bawdy humour is coarse, loud, and openly rude about bodies and appetites.

The key is that it's **funny, not filthy**. *Obscene* offends, *lewd* leers, *pornographic* arouses — **bawdy** wants a laugh, and usually gets one from a whole room. It's the register of tavern songs, limericks, and Shakespeare's puns, so the word carries a whiff of indulgent amusement even when the speaker pretends to disapprove.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **bawdy** joke is a **body** joke — told for a laugh, not a leer.

Near-rhyme with *body* fixes both the subject matter and the cheerful, unserious tone that separates *bawdy* from *obscene*.

### In sentences
1. The tavern scenes are frankly **bawdy**, and the Victorian editors quietly cut every one of them.
2. Grandma had three drinks and started telling **bawdy** stories about the war.

### In the wild
Any school edition of Shakespeare — the footnotes forever explaining that some innocent-looking line is a **bawdy** pun. It's the standard word critics use for his dirty jokes.

### Where it comes from
From **bawd** — a brothel-keeper or procuress (a *bawdy house* was legal-speak for a brothel). So *bawdy* is literally "of the bawd": the talk you'd hear in that establishment.

# **chagrin** *(n. / v.)* — **shuh-GRIN**

*Not "CHAG-rin" — the ch is sh, and the stress lands on the second syllable, rhyming with "begin."*

### Means
The sting of **wounded pride** — embarrassment, annoyance and disappointment mixed together, felt when you've failed, been outdone, or been proved wrong.

Not guilt and not shame: those concern wrongdoing, while chagrin concerns *dignity*. Losing an argument to a junior colleague is chagrin; lying about it is shame. It's a civilised, restrained word — you rarely howl with chagrin, you swallow it — and it lives above all in the frozen phrase **"much to my chagrin,"** where it's often faintly self-mocking.

### Trick to lock it in
> **cha-GRIN** — the tight little **grin** you put on when you've just been shown up.

The word hides *grin* because chagrin is the feeling you have to smile politely through, which is exactly its restrained, pride-bruised register.

### In sentences
1. To the ambassador's evident **chagrin**, the treaty he had spent four years negotiating was rewritten in a single afternoon.
2. Much to my **chagrin**, the six-year-old solved the puzzle in about nine seconds.

### Where it comes from
French **chagrin** = *grief, vexation* — the same word as French *chagrin*, a rough granular leather (English **shagreen**) once used as a rasp for smoothing wood. Something that **chafes**: chagrin is the constant fine abrasion of a bruised ego, not a clean wound.

# **collude** *(v.)* — **kuh-LOOD**

*Noun form: collusion, kuh-LOO-zhun.*

### Means
To **cooperate secretly** with someone for a dishonest, fraudulent, or illegal purpose.

You cannot collude alone: the word requires two or more insiders **and** a victim outside the arrangement. Where *conspire* suggests a plot with a target and a plan, *collude* suggests a quiet understanding — often between parties who are supposed to be **rivals or independent**: two bidders on the same contract, an auditor and the firm he audits, a landlord and the "independent" surveyor. It's the vocabulary of regulators and courts rather than thrillers, which is why it sounds coldly damning: no smoke-filled room needed, just a nod.

### Trick to lock it in
> **co-** needs a partner, **-lude** means they're *playing* — collusion is a game two people play on a third.

The prefix tells you it takes at least two, and the *lud-* tells you someone outside the deal is being played.

### In sentences
1. The commission found that the three suppliers had **colluded** for a decade, carving the market into territories and never underbidding one another.
2. You two **colluded** on this, didn't you? Nobody books the same terrible restaurant twice by accident.

### In the wild
**"No collusion"** — Trump's endlessly repeated line through the Russia investigation put the noun into every news bulletin for two straight years.

### Where it comes from
Latin **com-** (*together*) + **lūdere** (*to play*) → *collūdere*, "to play along together."

That **lud-/lus-** root is a GRE goldmine, and every member keeps the sense of *play*: **prelude** and **interlude** (played before/between), **ludicrous** (fit only for play), **delude** (play someone false), **elude** (play your way out), **allude** (play toward a thing without naming it), **illusion** (a trick of play).

# **commiserate** *(v.)* — **kuh-MIZ-uh-rayt**

### Means
To express sympathy or sorrow with someone over their misfortune — to share in their bad luck out loud.

The word is horizontal, not vertical. You *console* someone from a position of strength and you *pity* them from above, but you **commiserate** as a fellow sufferer — same layoff, same lost match, same terrible manager. It also carries a faint social warmth: commiserating usually means talking, often over a drink, and it fixes nothing by design. Note the grammar, which encodes the meaning: you commiserate **with** a person, **over** or **about** the disaster.

⚠️ **commiserate ≠ commensurate.**
- **commiserate** *(kuh-MIZ-uh-rayt)* = share in someone's misery
- **commensurate** *(kuh-MEN-suh-rit)* = proportionate, in measure with — *"a salary commensurate with experience"*

Same prefix, different second half: *miser* (wretched) vs *mensura* (measure).

### Trick to lock it in
> **Co-miser-ate** — there's a **miser** in the middle, and the two of you are equally poor in luck.

The *co-* is load-bearing: you climb down into the misery beside them rather than dispensing comfort from above, which is why the word almost always takes *with*.

### In sentences
1. After the verdict, the defence team retired to a back room to **commiserate** over eighteen months of wasted work.
2. We spent the entire train ride home **commiserating** about our boss.

### In the wild
British sport and quiz shows run on the pairing **"congratulations or commiserations"** — the winner gets one, the loser gets the other. That stock phrase is the whole meaning in a word: what you say to the person who just lost.

### Where it comes from
Latin **com-** (*with*) + **miser** (*wretched, pitiable*) — literally *to be wretched together*.

That root pays out widely: **misery**, **miserable**, **miserly** (a **miser** is, etymologically, a wretch), and the *Miserere* psalm — "have mercy."

# **conflagration** *(n.)* — **kon-fluh-GRAY-shuhn**

### Means
A **large, destructive fire** — one that spreads and takes out buildings, blocks, forests. By extension, a **war or conflict** on that scale.

Size is the whole point. A *fire* can be any size; a *blaze* is what the news calls a house fire; a **conflagration** is the kind that gets a name and a date — the Great Fire of London, Chicago 1871. It's formal and slightly historical in tone, and it carries the sense that control was lost: nobody puts a conflagration out quickly. Used figuratively — a political conflagration, a family conflagration — it keeps that shape: something local that spread until it had consumed everything near it.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hear **flagrant** sitting inside it — same blaze, different scale: a flagrant lie burns in plain sight, a conflagration burns the whole street.

Both words come from the same "burning" root, so if you can hear *flagrant* you can hear the fire — and the *con-* ("all together") tells you it's everything alight at once, not one candle.

### In sentences
1. By dawn the **conflagration** had taken four blocks, and the brigade had abandoned everything east of the river.
2. One offhand comment in the group chat and the whole thing was a **conflagration** by lunchtime.

### In the wild
Wildfire coverage reaches for it the moment a fire stops being one fire: reporters say *blaze* while it's in the hills and switch to **conflagration** once it jumps into neighbourhoods.

### Where it comes from
Latin **flagrāre** = *"to burn, blaze"*, with **con-** here meaning *thoroughly* — burning all over.

Same root gives you **flagrant** (an offence so blatant it's blazing) and the legal phrase **in flagrante delicto**, literally *"while the crime is still blazing"* — caught in the act.

# **contretemps** *(n.)* — **KON-truh-tahn**

*Kept French: the final -ps is silent. Plural is spelled identically but voiced — KON-truh-tahnz.*

### Means
An **inconvenient mishap** — or a **small, awkward dispute** — that arrives at exactly the wrong moment.

Size is the whole point: a contretemps is *beneath* the level of a crisis. A cancelled flight, a seating-plan row, two ministers snapping at each other in a corridor. Calling something a contretemps is a way of shrinking it — the French word puts a napkin over the mess and implies the speaker is far too composed to be rattled by it. Used about a real catastrophe, it reads as sarcasm or as damage control.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Contre + temps = counter-tempo.** You're off the beat — a stumble, not a collision.

The mishap is defined by bad *timing* rather than by scale, which is why the word stays small and faintly amused.

### In sentences
1. A minor **contretemps** over precedence at the state dinner was smoothed over before the first course arrived.
2. We had a bit of a **contretemps** with the neighbours about the bins, and now nobody says hello.

### In the wild
Diplomatic journalism runs on this word: when two governments have a public spat too petty to call an incident, the papers call it a **contretemps** — the word itself is the de-escalation.

### Where it comes from
French **contre-temps** = *"against the time / off the beat."* It began as a **fencing** term: a thrust made on the wrong count, at the wrong instant.

Both halves pay rent elsewhere: **contra-** gives *contradict, contrary, counter, contraband*; **temps** ← Latin *tempus* gives *tempo, temporal, contemporary, extempore, temporary*.

# **conviction** *(n.)* — **kuhn-VIK-shuhn**

### Means
**1.** A belief held so firmly it's part of who you are. **2.** A formal verdict of guilt in court — and the criminal record it leaves.

A *conviction* outranks an *opinion* and outweighs a *belief*: it has moral stake in it, and abandoning it costs you something. You have opinions about films; you have convictions about right and wrong. Hence *the courage of one's convictions* — the assumption is that they may cost you.

There's a third, everyday use: conviction as **force of delivery**. "He said it without conviction" doesn't mean he was lying — it means nothing behind the words was pushing. On the GRE, the tell is context: a *deeply held* conviction is the belief; a *prior* conviction is the rap sheet.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's a **convict** hiding inside **conviction** — and a jury only convicts once it's *convinced*.

Both senses are the same event, one legal and one internal: the case has been argued, the verdict is in, and the matter is no longer open to debate.

### In sentences
1. Her pacifism was not a pose but a **conviction**, held through two wars and the loss of most of her friends.
2. He apologised, but you could tell his heart wasn't in it — no **conviction** behind a word of it.

### In the wild
Yeats, *The Second Coming*: **"The best lack all conviction, while the worst are full of passionate intensity."** Half the internet quotes it during elections, and it hangs entirely on the belief sense.

### Where it comes from
Latin **convincere** = *con-* (thoroughly) + **vincere** (*to conquer*) — literally "to defeat utterly," then "to defeat in argument, prove guilty."

**vincere** is a workhorse root: **victory, victor, invincible, evict, evince, vanquish, convince**. A conviction is a belief that beat all the others.

# **croon** *(v. / n.)* — **KROON**

### Means
To sing or murmur in a **low, soft, sentimental** voice — usually to soothe someone, or to seduce them.

Volume is the whole point: you can belt, you can croon, and they are opposite ends of one dial. A croon is intimate, close-up, almost private — a mother over a cot, a singer with his lips on the microphone. It carries warmth by default, but writers also use it with a raised eyebrow, because sustained sweetness curdles into schmaltz: *he crooned about her eyes for four straight verses.* The verb also stretches beyond music to any soft cooing speech — you can croon reassurance at a nervous dog.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Croon rhymes with moon** — and crooning is what you do *about* the moon, softly, at night, to someone half-asleep.

The rhyme fixes both the sound and the register: low, dark, sentimental — never loud.

### In sentences
1. The nurse **crooned** to the infant in a language no one else in the ward recognised.
2. He **crooned** the last chorus straight at her, and honestly it was a bit much.

### In the wild
**Crooner** is a real job description: Sinatra, Bing Crosby, Nat King Cole — and Michael Bublé is still billed as one. The whole style exists because microphones got sensitive enough that a singer could stop shouting and just murmur.

### Where it comes from
From Middle Dutch **kronen**, *"to groan, lament"* — and in Scots, **crune** meant the low bellow of cattle. The sentimentality is modern; the **lowness of the pitch** was there from the start.

# **depose** *(v.)* — **dih-POHZ**

### Means
Two distinct senses, both worth knowing:

**1. To remove someone from high office** — a king, a dictator, a party leader — by force or by formal act.
**2. (Legal) To take sworn testimony from someone** out of court, or to give it yourself.

Sense 1 is the GRE one, and it's specifically about **rank**. You depose a *ruler*, not a shop assistant; the word carries the weight of a throne or a presidency. It's also colder and more formal than its neighbours: *overthrow* implies violence, *oust* is scrappy and can apply to anyone, *abdicate* is the ruler stepping down willingly — **depose** is the neutral, almost procedural statement that the office was taken away. A palace coup, a vote of no confidence, and an armed revolt can all depose a leader.

Sense 2 turns up in every legal thriller: the lawyer **deposes** the witness, and the transcript is the **deposition**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **De-pose** = to *un-pose*: take the king down off his royal pose — or put your sworn words down on the record.

Both senses are literally "putting down": a monarch down from the throne, testimony down onto the page.

### In sentences
1. The generals **deposed** the emperor within a week, installing a nephew who could be relied upon to sign whatever they put in front of him.
2. They **deposed** the CEO over a WhatsApp group before he'd even landed.

### In the wild
In any legal drama — *Suits*, *Better Call Saul* — someone is always being **deposed** in a glass conference room: sworn in, recorded, no judge in sight. That's sense 2 in its natural habitat.

### Where it comes from
Latin **de-** (*down*) + **ponere** (*to put, to place*) — literally *to put down*.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **deposit** (put down money), **position**, **compose**, **impose**, **postpone** (put after), **component**, **exponent**, **repository**.

# **dowdy** *(adj.)* — **DOW-dee**

### Means
Shabby, drab, and out of fashion in appearance — dull clothes, dull grooming, no style.

The insult is aimed at *dullness*, not dirt or poverty. Something **dowdy** may be perfectly clean, well-made and expensive; it is simply joyless, shapeless, and a decade behind. It also carries a faint condescension and is aimed at women far more often than men — which is exactly why the GRE likes it as a tone question. Cousins split cleanly: **shabby** = worn out, **gaudy** = too much flash, **dowdy** = too little.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dowdy** rhymes with **cloudy** — nothing is *wrong* with the outfit, it's just overcast.

The rhyme fixes the nuance: dowdy clothes aren't torn or filthy, they're grey, flat and lifeless.

### In sentences
1. Her portrait shows a woman deliberately **dowdy**, as though plainness were a form of moral proof.
2. I turned up in a **dowdy** brown cardigan and everyone else was in sequins.

### In the wild
*The Devil Wears Prada* is ninety minutes of this word: pre-makeover Andy in the lumpy blue sweater is what **dowdy** looks like, and the whole film is about it being fixed.

# **détente** *(n.)* — **day-TAHNT**

*Often written without the accent: detente. Never "duh-TENT" — the final syllable is French and rhymes with "font".*

### Means
An **easing of hostility** between rivals — usually states, sometimes people — after a period of strain.

Détente is a *lowering of temperature, not a friendship*. Nobody has forgiven anybody; the two sides have simply stopped aiming. It's cool, diplomatic, headline vocabulary — you'd read it about superpowers, warring departments, or two relatives who have agreed not to bring up the will. Applied to a small human quarrel it lands drily, half-joking, because it dresses a squabble in the language of arms control.

⚠️ **détente ≠ entente.**
- **détente** = the *tension drops* between enemies
- **entente** = an *understanding*, a friendly alignment (the Triple **Entente**, the **Entente** Cordiale)

Enemies reach a détente. Friends form an entente.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dé-tente = de-tensed.** The bowstring is slackened — but the bow is still loaded.

That second half is the nuance: relaxing the pull is not the same as putting the weapon down.

### In sentences
1. After eighteen months of sanctions and recalled ambassadors, the summit produced a fragile **détente**.
2. Mum and my aunt have reached a **détente** — they still don't speak, but nobody's storming out of Christmas this year.

### In the wild
**Détente** is the standard name for the 1970s thaw in the Cold War — Nixon and Brezhnev, the SALT talks, the handshake photos. The missiles stayed in their silos the whole time, which is exactly what the word means.

### Where it comes from
French **détente** = *"a slackening, a release"* — originally the catch that releases a crossbow trigger. From Latin **tendere**, *"to stretch."*

That root is a GRE workhorse: **tension, tense, extend, distend, intense, portend, tendon, contend**. Anything stretched tight. Put **dé-** ("un-, away") in front and you get the stretch coming off.

# **echelon** *(n. (also v.))* — **ESH-uh-lon**

*The ch is sh, from the French — never "ETCH-uh-lon."*

### Means
A **level or rank within a hierarchy** — one tier of an organisation, and the people occupying it. (Military sense: troops or ships arranged in staggered parallel lines, each stepped back from the one ahead.)

*Level* is flat and neutral; **echelon** presumes a whole ladder of them, with power concentrated at the top. It's institutional vocabulary — governments, armies, corporations, sports leagues — and it almost always comes attached to the top of the structure: *the upper echelons*, *the highest echelons of the party*. Using it about a diner's staff would be a joke.

### Trick to lock it in
> You almost never hear *lower* echelons — the word is a rung, and it gets mentioned when someone's near the top of the ladder.

Lock the collocation "upper echelons" and you've locked both halves at once: the literal rung and the whiff of power that comes with it.

### In sentences
1. The report was circulated only within the upper **echelons** of the ministry, and never reached the officials who would have to implement it.
2. He started in the mailroom and clawed his way into the top **echelon** in six years.

### In the wild
In *Splinter Cell*, Sam Fisher works for **Third Echelon** — a covert tier buried inside the NSA's hierarchy. The name is doing exactly what the word means: naming a rank-level, not a place.

### Where it comes from
French **échelon** = *"rung of a ladder,"* a diminutive of **échelle** (ladder), from Latin **scala**.

That root pays off elsewhere: **escalate** (climb the ladder), **escalator**, **scale** (as in *scale a wall*), **scalar**. Every one of them is a ladder in disguise.

# **ennui** *(n.)* — **ahn-WEE**

*Never EN-yoo-eye — the word never got anglicised.*

### Means
A listless, settled **boredom** — dissatisfaction born of having nothing that interests you, and suspecting nothing ever will.

Plain boredom is situational and short: a delayed flight, a dull meeting. Ennui is a **climate, not a weather event** — chronic, faintly philosophical, and worn almost as a credential. It belongs to people with the leisure to be bored: aristocrats, poets, disaffected teenagers in black. Calling it ennui rather than boredom is itself a small act of self-flattery, and good writers use it knowing that.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ennui** is boredom that has been to Paris.

Same emptiness, but slower, grander, and quietly pleased with itself for feeling it.

### In sentences
1. A summer of country weekends left her in a state of **ennui** no invitation could cure.
2. Three months into the job it wasn't stress, it was **ennui** — I just stopped caring what the emails said.

### In the wild
Wes Anderson's *The French Dispatch* is set in a town called **Ennui-sur-Blasé** — "Boredom-on-Jaded." The joke only lands if you know the word, and knowing the joke locks the word in.

### Where it comes from
Latin **in odiō** — *"in hatred,"* as in *mihi in odio est*, "it is loathsome to me." Through Old French *enuier* this became two English words from one source: **ennui**, and its plainer twin **annoy**.

That's the whole scale in one root: what merely irritates you is an **annoyance**; what deadens you is **ennui**. Related through *odium*: **odious**, **odium**.

# **expatiate** *(v.)* — **ek-SPAY-shee-ayt**

*Four syllables, and the middle is "shee", not "tee" — never "ex-PAT-ee-ate".*

### Means
To speak or write **at length and in detail** on a subject — usually with *on* or *upon*: *he expatiated upon the virtues of the metric system.*

The word is bookish and faintly amused. **Elaborate** is neutral and often invited ("could you elaborate?"); **ramble** is openly rude; **expatiate** sits between them — it grants that the speaker has plenty to say while hinting they said rather more of it than anyone needed. You expatiate *at* an audience, not *with* them, and the verb almost always has a slight tilt of the eyebrow. It rarely appears in speech at all; when it does, it's the writer's word for a lecture nobody asked for.

⚠️ **expatiate ≠ expatriate.**
- **expatiate** = go on at length (*spatium*, space)
- **expatriate** = leave your native country (*patria*, fatherland)

An expat living in Lisbon may expatiate on Portuguese tax law, but the words share nothing but a look.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ex-SPACE-iate** — you take up a lot of **space**.

The word has *space* buried in the middle of it, and that is exactly what an expatiating speaker consumes — pages of it, minutes of it.

### In sentences
1. The preface **expatiates** upon the author's methods for some thirty pages before a single argument appears.
2. Don't ask him about his sourdough starter — he'll **expatiate** for an hour.

### Where it comes from
Latin **exspatiari** = *"to wander off, to spread out"* — from **ex-** (out) + **spatium** (*space, room, a racecourse*). Originally you expatiated by roaming physically; now you roam verbally.

**spatium** is a useful root to own: **space**, **spacious**, **spatial**, and the sense of *stretching out* in all of them.

🚫 **Not *patria*.** That's *expatriate, patriot, patriarch, repatriate* — fatherland words, no relation.

# **fraught** *(adj.)* — **FRAWT**

*The -gh- is silent; it rhymes with thought, not with fought-and-won.*

### Means
Two uses, and the GRE wants both.

**1. fraught *with* X** = loaded with, full of — and X is essentially always something bad: *danger, peril, difficulty, risk, contradiction*. You will not see "fraught with joy."

**2. fraught, standing alone** = tense, strained, anxious. *A fraught silence. A fraught relationship. Things got fraught.*

What links them is **latent trouble**, not present disaster. A fraught situation hasn't gone wrong yet — it is carrying the makings of going wrong. "Dangerous" states the threat outright; *fraught* says the threat is packed inside, waiting. And the standalone sense adds a human edge: people in a fraught room are stressed, wary, walking on eggshells.

### Trick to lock it in
> **fraught** rhymes with **taut** — the situation is pulled tight and one tug from snapping.

Fraught isn't broken yet; it's under tension, which is exactly why the word implies trouble stored up rather than trouble arrived.

### In sentences
1. Any attempt to redraw the border would be **fraught** with consequences the treaty's authors never anticipated.
2. We can invite both of them, but I'm telling you now, that table is going to be **fraught**.

### In the wild
Newsrooms run on it: *"a process fraught with difficulty," "a fraught relationship between the two leaders."* If you learn one collocation, learn **fraught with peril** — that pairing is half the word's exam life.

### Where it comes from
**Fraught is *freight*.** Middle Dutch **vracht** = *cargo*; Middle English *fraught* meant a ship **laden** with goods. The literal cargo sense died and the metaphor took over — so *fraught with danger* is, word for word, *carrying a hold full of danger*.

# **fulcrum** *(n.)* — **FUUL-kruhm**

*Also heard as FUL-kruhm, first syllable like *dull*.*

### Means
The fixed point on which a lever pivots — and, figuratively, the thing on which a whole situation turns.

Size is the point. A fulcrum isn't the biggest or strongest part of the system; it's the *small, still* part that makes enormous force possible. So calling someone the fulcrum of a negotiation says more than "important" — it says the whole weight rests on them, and shift them an inch and the outcome changes. Compare **linchpin** (holds things together, stops collapse) and **crux** (the hard central problem): a fulcrum is about **leverage**, not cohesion or difficulty.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ful**crum = *full* + *crumb* — a crumb-sized point bearing the full weight.

The fulcrum is tiny and never moves, yet everything the lever manages to lift depends on it.

### In sentences
1. A handful of undecided senators became the **fulcrum** of the entire bill: small in number, decisive in effect.
2. My gran is the **fulcrum** of that family — take her out and the whole thing tips over.

### In the wild
The bolt in the middle of a **seesaw** is a fulcrum — which is why shuffling toward the centre kills your leverage and the heavier kid pins you in the air.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fulcrum** = *"bedpost, support,"* from **fulcīre**, *"to prop up."* The word has barely changed job in two thousand years: it is still the thing propping everything else up.

# **imbroglio** *(n.)* — **im-BROHL-yoh**

*The g is silent — it's Italian gl, as in "million." Never "im-BROG-lee-oh."*

### Means
A **confused, tangled, acrimonious situation** — a messy dispute with several parties in it and no clean way out.

An imbroglio isn't just a fight; it's a *snarl*. A quarrel has two sides, a **fiasco** ends in failure, a **brouhaha** is loud but trivial — an imbroglio is complicated, embarrassing, and faintly scandalous, the kind of mess where nobody can even agree on what happened. The word carries a raised eyebrow: it's what journalists and diplomats call a scandal when they want to sound dry about it.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's a **broil** hiding in im-**brogl**-io — you get *embroiled* in one.

Same root, same idea: things get stirred up and tangled until you can't extract yourself.

### In sentences
1. The minister resigned rather than explain his part in the procurement **imbroglio**.
2. Two group chats, three versions of the story — the whole birthday-party thing turned into a full **imbroglio**.

### In the wild
A headline-writer's favourite: any time a scandal is too tangled to summarise, the press calls it "the *[name]* imbroglio" — the polite word for a mess nobody can untangle.

### Where it comes from
Italian **imbrogliare** = *"to tangle, to muddle."* English borrowed it twice: once straight (**imbroglio**) and once through French as **embroil**. The older sense of **broil** — not cooking, but a *brawl* — is the same family.

# **jocund** *(adj.)* — **JOK-uhnd**

*Short o, as in *hot* — not "JOKE-und," though you'll hear that too.*

### Means
Cheerful, light-hearted, in bright and buoyant spirits — of a person, a mood, a morning, a crowd.

This is sunlit cheerfulness, not loud fun. *Merry* is warmer and more social, *jolly* is rounder and more physical, *mirthful* implies actual laughter — **jocund** is a lightness in the air, and it belongs to poets. Nobody describes a work chat as jocund without smirking; the word arrives already dressed for verse.

**jocund ≠ jocular.**
- **jocund** = *cheerful* — the mood
- **jocular** = *joking* — the behaviour

A jocular colleague makes the quips; a jocund morning just feels good.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Jocund** = a **joke**'s good mood, with the joke removed.

The word carries the cheer that jokes produce, not the jesting itself — which is exactly the line between *jocund* and *jocular*.

### In sentences
1. The company sat late over the wine, jocund and unhurried, as though the war were somebody else's news.
2. He came back from that holiday weirdly jocund — humming in the kitchen, saying good morning to people.

### In the wild
Wordsworth's daffodils, the poem everyone half-remembers: *"A poet could not but be gay, / In such a **jocund** company."* The flowers are the jocund company — cheerful, dancing, doing nothing funny at all.

### Where it comes from
Latin **iucundus** = *"pleasant, delightful,"* from **iuvare**, *"to please, to help"* — the same root under **adjuvant** (a helping agent).

The twist worth keeping: the word got its spelling bent toward Latin **iocus**, *"joke"* — so *jocund* only **looks** like a cousin of *joke, jocular, jocose, jest*. It's a false family that stuck, and it's the reason the mnemonic works.

# **languish** *(v.)* — **LANG-gwish**

*The *gu* is a hard "gw" — LANG-gwish, not "LAN-gish."*

### Means
Two linked senses:
1. To **lose strength and vitality** — to droop, weaken, fade.
2. To be **left somewhere unpleasant, making no progress** — *languish in prison, languish in obscurity, languish at the bottom of the league*.

The verb is passive and slow. Nobody languishes in a moment; you languish for *years*, quietly, while something withers. It also carries a whiff of blame pointed elsewhere — a manuscript **languishes** in a drawer, a bill **languishes** in committee, because whoever should have rescued it didn't. Compare *suffer* (active pain), *deteriorate* (clinical), *rot* (contemptuous): **languish** is the neglected-and-fading one, faintly sad rather than harsh.

⚠️ **languish ≠ anguish.**
- **anguish** = sharp, acute agony (a noun, mostly)
- **languish** = slow fading from neglect

Anguish is the scream; languishing is the long silence after.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Lang**uish = *long* + *wish* — stuck a **long** time, **wish**ing someone would come get you.

The two halves give you both halves of the meaning: the duration and the helpless waiting.

### In sentences
1. The reform bill **languished** in committee for three sessions before anyone thought to call a vote.
2. That guitar's been **languishing** in my parents' garage since 2011.

### In the wild
Stock newsroom phrasing you've read a hundred times: a painting **languishes in an attic** for decades before the auction, a club **languishes at the bottom of the table**, a hostage **languishes in a cell**. Learn the collocations and the word comes with them.

### Where it comes from
Latin **languēre** = *"to be faint, weak,"* from the same root as **laxus** (*loose, slack*).

That gives you a cluster worth banking: **languid** (drooping, unhurried), **languor** (pleasant heavy tiredness), and the slackness family — **lax**, **relax**, **release**. Note the split: *languid* and *languor* can be sensual and rather nice; **languish** never is.

# **nadir** *(n.)* — **NAY-deer**

*Also NAD-eer; the older NAY-der is fading. Never NAH-dir.*

### Means
The **lowest point** — of a career, a mood, a market, a nation's fortunes. In astronomy, the point on the celestial sphere directly *beneath* you, opposite the zenith.

It is a superlative, and usually a retrospective one: you survey a whole arc and name the single deepest trough in it. "A low point" is casual and can be one of several; **the nadir** is a verdict, and there is only one. The register is formal and faintly grand — which is why it stings: it dignifies the collapse even as it measures it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nay, dear** — it does not get lower than this.

The sound gives you both halves of the word: the flat *nay* of rock bottom, and the finality — there's exactly one nadir, not a series of bad days.

### In sentences
1. The winter of 1943 marked the **nadir** of the regime's fortunes, and every general in the room knew it.
2. Eating cold beans over the sink at 2am was, I think, the **nadir** of my twenties.

### In the wild
Earth-observation satellites are described as looking **"at nadir"** — camera pointed straight down at the ground directly below. Every satellite image you've scrolled past on Google Maps is a nadir view.

### Where it comes from
Arabic **naẓīr as-samt** = *"counterpart of the zenith"* — the point diametrically opposite overhead. That same *as-samt* gave English **azimuth**, and a garbled copy of it gave **zenith**.

So one Arabic phrase hands you three words at once: **zenith** (top), **nadir** (bottom), **azimuth** (bearing). Medieval European astronomers took their star vocabulary wholesale from Arabic — as *algebra*, *algorithm* and *alcohol* also attest.

# **nimble** *(adj.)* — **NIM-buhl**

### Means
Quick and light in movement — and, just as often, quick and light in **thought**.

The praise is for speed and deftness, never for power. A nimble boxer slips the punch instead of trading blows; a nimble mind sidesteps an objection instead of grinding through it. It's a warmer, older word than **agile**, which has been half-eaten by corporate jargon, and broader than **deft**, which stays in the hands. Faintly complimentary in every use — nobody is nimble by accident.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nimble ≈ limber + nifty** — light and quick, never strong.

The word always praises evasion and finesse rather than force, whether the thing moving is a body or a brain.

### In sentences
1. The pianist's **nimble** left hand carried the bass line while the right wandered off into ornament.
2. She's **nimble** enough in an argument — you never land a punch, she's already three feet to the left.

### In the wild
*"Jack be nimble, Jack be quick, Jack jump over the candlestick"* — the nursery rhyme is doing the definition for you: nimble sits right next to *quick*, and the proof is a light little jump.

### Where it comes from
Old English **næmel** = *"quick to grasp,"* from **niman**, *"to take"* — nimble fingers are fingers quick to take. (The **b** was slipped in later for ease of saying it, exactly as in *thimble* and *humble*.)

Same root, opposite result: **numb** is literally *"taken"* — seized, and so robbed of feeling. Nimble hands take; numb hands can't.

# **ominous** *(adj.)* — **OM-uh-nuhs**

*Short o, as in hot — not "OH-min-us", even though omen has a long O.*

### Means
Giving the sense that something bad is about to happen; menacing in the way a **sign** is menacing.

The key is that *ominous* describes a forecast, not an attack. A growling dog is threatening; the sudden silence just before it growls is ominous. Nothing has happened yet — the word lives in the gap between the warning and the harm, which is why it attaches so naturally to weather, silence, a doctor's pause, a phone call at 3am. Faintly literary in flavour, but perfectly at home in speech.

⚠️ **ominous ≠ onerous.**
- **ominous** = *boding evil, threatening*
- **onerous** = *burdensome, heavy going* (Latin *onus*, "load")

An onerous contract exhausts you. An ominous one means you're about to be ruined.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's an **omen** sitting inside **omin**ous — and an omen never hits you, it only warns you.

That hidden word carries the nuance: the danger is still ahead, and the word points at the sign rather than the strike.

### In sentences
1. The dispatches from the frontier grew shorter and more **ominous** with each passing week.
2. My boss said "let's talk Monday" and walked out — which felt pretty **ominous**.

### In the wild
Stock music libraries have a category literally tagged **"ominous"** — the low string drone under a trailer as the screen goes dark. You've heard the word's exact meaning scored a hundred times.

### Where it comes from
Latin **omen, ominis** = *a sign, a foretelling* — straight into English as **omen** and **ominous**.

The same root gives you **abominable**, from *ab-ominari*, "to shrink away from as a bad omen." The Abominable Snowman is, etymologically, a walking bad sign.

# **outlandish** *(adj.)* — **owt-LAN-dish**

### Means
So strange or extravagant that it seems to belong to no normal world — bizarre, wildly unconventional, faintly absurd.

The word judges from a comfortable distance. *Grotesque* recoils, *outrageous* is offended, but **outlandish** mostly raises an eyebrow: it says *this is too much, and not quite serious*. It attaches to things that overreach — outlandish claims, outlandish prices, an outlandish hat — and it carries a whiff of amused disbelief rather than moral alarm. Calling a theory outlandish dismisses it as too weird to bother refuting.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Out-land-ish** — it looks like it wandered in from *out of the land*, some other land entirely.

The three chunks sit right there on the surface, and they tell you the exact flavour of strange: not evil, just unmistakably *not from around here*.

### In sentences
1. The witness offered an **outlandish** account of the evening that no other testimony supported.
2. He turned up in an **outlandish** neon suit and acted like nobody would notice.

### In the wild
**Outlander** — the Gabaldon novels and the Starz series — is the same *out-land* root: the title means *foreigner*, the woman who does not belong to this place or century.

### Where it comes from
Old English **ūtlendisc** = *"of a foreign land"* (**ūt** *out* + **land** + **-ish**). It first meant simply **foreign**, and then drifted the way suspicion drifts: what comes from elsewhere looks odd, and *odd* swallowed *foreign* entirely.

Compare **barbarian** (Greek: someone whose speech sounds like *bar-bar*) — languages keep building "weird" out of "not from here."

# **propitious** *(adj.)* — **pruh-PISH-uhs**

### Means
Favourable; offering good conditions for success. Applied to moments, times, circumstances, weather, signs.

It describes the **setup, not the score**. *Lucky* is a verdict on what already happened; *propitious* is a forecast — the conditions have tilted your way and nothing has been attempted yet. The word is formal and carries a faint residue of omens and gods: you don't say a sandwich is propitious, you say the moment is. Its natural companions are *moment*, *time*, *circumstances*, *omen*.

⚠️ **propitious ≠ propitiate.**
- **propitious** *(adj.)* = the gods are **already** favourable
- **propitiate** *(v.)* = to **make** them favourable — appease, buy off

You propitiate an angry client; you wait for a propitious moment to send the invoice.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pro**pitious — the odds are **pro** you. It's a weather report, not a result.

The *pro-* tells you something is leaning in your favour, and "weather report" fixes the tense: propitious always points forward, at conditions, before the attempt.

### In sentences
1. The generals waited for a more **propitious** moment to advance, when the fog had lifted and the ground had dried.
2. Empty flight, upgrade at the gate, sun in Delhi — pretty **propitious** start to the trip.

### In the wild
Indian weddings run on this word: before a venue is booked or a card is printed, someone is hunting the calendar for a **propitious** date — the *muhurat*. Same instinct as the Latin: find the moment the heavens are leaning your way.

### Where it comes from
Latin **propitius** = *"favourable, gracious"* — said of gods inclined toward you: **pro-** (forward, toward) + **petere** (to seek, to go at).

**petere** pays off across the exam: *petition* (formally seek), *appetite* (seeking toward), *compete* (seek alongside), *impetuous* (rushing at), *centripetal* (seeking the centre), and *propitiate* (make the god lean your way).

# **prurient** *(adj.)* — **PRUUR-ee-uhnt**

*Three syllables — not "PROOR-yent."*

### Means
Having or catering to an **excessive, unwholesome interest in sex** — the leer, the appetite for dirty detail.

The word judges the *watcher*, not the act. Nobody is prurient for having sex; you're prurient for wanting to hear about it. So it attaches to the onlooker, the tabloid reader, the "just curious" questioner — and it's an accusation every time, delivered in a cold, formal register (courts, critics, editorials) rather than shouted. **Lewd** and **obscene** describe the material; **prurient** describes the itch the material is designed to scratch.

⚠️ **prurient ≠ puerile.** Both are sneers at bad taste, but **puerile** (PYOO-uh-ryl) = *childish, silly*; **prurient** = *dirty-minded*. A fart joke is puerile. Reading someone's divorce filings for fun is prurient.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Prurient** is *pure* with the **r** shoved in — hands clean, mind filthy.

The word never accuses you of doing anything, only of wanting to look.

### In sentences
1. The biographer dwells on the marriage with a **prurient** attention that tells us more about him than about his subject.
2. Don't pretend it's research — you're scrolling her photos out of **prurient** nosiness.

### In the wild
It's a load-bearing legal term in the US: material counts as obscene only if it "appeals to the **prurient** interest" — the Miller test. Every ratings fight and banned-book case runs through that one word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prurire** = *"to itch, to crave."* A prurient interest is literally an **itch** — which is why the medical word for itching is **pruritus**. Keep the itch and the whole nuance follows: the itch is in the observer, and it wants scratching.

# **sadistic** *(adj.)* — **suh-DIS-tik**

### Means
Cruel in a particular way: **taking pleasure in someone else's pain**, humiliation or fear.

Plain cruelty can be careless, cold, or a means to an end — the bully wants your lunch money. **Sadistic** cruelty *is* the end: the suffering is the reward, and the person is savouring it. That makes it the heaviest word in the family, at home in courtrooms and criminal profiles. Colloquially it also runs as hyperbole — a **sadistic** exam schedule, a **sadistic** spin instructor — where the joke is precisely that they seem to be *enjoying* your misery.

### Trick to lock it in
> **SAD** is hiding inside **sadistic** — but it's your sadness, and he's delighted by it.

The word isn't cruelty alone; it's cruelty plus pleasure, so if nobody's enjoying it, it isn't sadistic.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal found the guards' treatment of prisoners not merely negligent but **sadistic**, calculated to prolong distress for its own sake.
2. He scheduled the meeting for 7 a.m. on a Monday and I swear he had a **sadistic** little smile about it.

### In the wild
In true-crime documentaries, "sadistic" is never loose language — profilers and prosecutors use it as a technical classification, meaning the offender was motivated by the victim's suffering itself.

### Where it comes from
From the **Marquis de Sade**, the 18th-century French aristocrat and novelist who was jailed for decades and wrote at length about cruelty as pleasure. His name became the condition: **sadism, sadist, sadistic**.

Its mirror is an eponym too — **masochistic**, from the Austrian writer **Leopold von Sacher-Masoch**, who wrote about taking pleasure in *receiving* pain. Two men, two novels, one compound: **sadomasochism**.

🚫 **Not from *sad*.** Pure coincidence — English *sad* is Old English *sæd*, "full, sated." Useful hook, false relative.

# **zenith** *(n.)* — **ZEE-nith**

*Chiefly ZEE-nith in the US; British speakers commonly say ZEN-ith.*

### Means
The **highest point** — literally, the point of the sky directly overhead; figuratively, the peak of someone's power, fame, or fortune.

Where *peak* is flat and everyday and *pinnacle* suggests a hard-won summit, **zenith borrows its grandeur from the sky** — it's the word for empires, careers, and reputations, not for stock prices this week. It also carries an arc: something rose to get here, and the phrasing *"at the zenith of his power"* almost always means the narrator knows what came afterwards. Used of the present it flatters; used of the past it quietly announces the decline.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Z** is where the alphabet tops out and stops — the **zenith** is where the climb tops out and stops.

The word marks a summit already reached, so whatever comes next is descent rather than more rise.

### In sentences
1. At the **zenith** of its influence the company set prices that three continents simply paid.
2. He hit his **zenith** at twenty-four and has been dining out on it ever since.

### In the wild
Weather and astronomy use it literally every day: the sun is **at its zenith** at local noon — straight overhead, as high as it will get, and sinking from that moment on.

### Where it comes from
Arabic **samt al-ra's** = *"the path over the head."* Medieval scribes copying the Latin misread *samt* as *senit* — and the typo became the English word.

That same *samt* gives you **azimuth** (a compass direction), and its opposite point in the sky, **nadir**, is Arabic too (*naẓīr*, "counterpart"). Three GRE-friendly words straight out of Arabic astronomy: **zenith** overhead, **nadir** underfoot, **azimuth** around the horizon.
