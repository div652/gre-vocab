# **attribute** *(v. / n.)* — **uh-TRIB-yoot (v.) / A-truh-byoot (n.)**

*The stress moves: verb uh-TRIB-yoot, noun A-truh-byoot. Saying the noun with verb stress is the classic slip.*

### Means
**Verb:** to assign something to its source — a cause, an author, an owner. *The painting is attributed to Caravaggio. She attributed the delay to weather.*
**Noun:** a quality that belongs to something as part of what it is. *Patience is his best attribute.*

The verb is a claim about **origin**, and it quietly admits uncertainty: you attribute a painting when you can't prove who painted it. It's neutral about praise or blame — you can attribute a triumph or a disaster. The noun is stronger than "trait": an attribute is felt to be **essential**, not incidental — a mercy attributed to God, strength as an attribute of a character in a game.

⚠️ **attribute ≠ impute.** Both assign something to a person, but **impute** almost always assigns something *discreditable*, and usually unfairly — *they imputed dishonest motives to her*. Attribute is even-handed; impute points a finger.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A**-truh-byoot is a **thing** — nouns take the stress up front (**RE**cord, **CON**duct, **PRO**test); push the stress back and it becomes a verb.

English shifts stress leftward to make a noun and rightward to make a verb, and *attribute* obeys the rule exactly.

### In sentences
1. Scholars now **attribute** the anonymous treatise to a minor clerk in the royal chancery, though the ascription remains contested.
2. Don't **attribute** it to bad luck — you didn't start studying until Sunday night.

### In the wild
The internet's favourite joke about **misattribution**: a serene photo captioned *"Don't believe everything you read on the internet just because there's a picture with a quote next to it." — Abraham Lincoln.* That's the verb in a nutshell — a quote assigned to a source that can't have said it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** ("to") + **tribuere** ("to allot, assign"), from **tribus**, *tribe* — originally to divide something out among the tribes.

That root pays out widely: **tribute** (what you pay over), **contribute** (give along with), **distribute** (deal out), **retribution** (paying back), **tributary** (a river paying into a larger one).

# **calamitous** *(adj.)* — **kuh-LAM-ih-tuhs**

### Means
Causing or amounting to **disaster** — ruin on a scale that can't be walked back.

*Unfortunate* is a shrug, *disastrous* is the everyday workhorse, **calamitous** is the heavy, formal one — the word historians and editorial writers use for the decision that wrecked a bank, a campaign, a fleet. It points at **consequences**, not feelings: a thing is calamitous because of the wreckage it left, not because it felt bad at the time. It also carries a faint note of judgement — someone usually *caused* the calamity.

⚠️ **calamitous ≠ calumnious.**
- **calamitous** = *disastrous*
- **calumnious** = *slanderous* (from **calumny**, a false, malicious accusation)

One ruins your life; the other ruins your name.

### Trick to lock it in
> **There is no such thing as *mildly* calamitous.**

The word has only one setting — total ruin — which is why it never takes a softening adverb the way *bad*, *unfortunate*, or even *disastrous* will.

### In sentences
1. The decision to fortify the northern passes proved **calamitous**, costing the empire both its army and its grain supply.
2. Buying at the top of the market was, in hindsight, absolutely **calamitous**.

### In the wild
**Calamity Jane** — the Wild West markswoman, and Doris Day's 1953 musical. The nickname was the point: trouble arrived with her.

# **censor** *(v. / n.)* — **SEN-ser**

*Sounds exactly like sensor — no audible difference.*

### Means
**Verb:** to inspect material and **cut out or suppress** whatever an authority finds objectionable — obscene, seditious, or secret.
**Noun:** the official who does the cutting.

Censoring is **suppression by power** — a state, a studio, a school board, an army. It isn't editing for quality; it's removal for control. The content is *made to disappear*.

⚠️ **censor ≠ censure.**
- **censor** = *delete* the message
- **censure** = *formally condemn* the person for it

A senator can be censured. A senator's speech can be censored.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cens*or* is an agent, like edit*or* — a man who cuts. Cens*ure* is like in*jure* — it wounds a reputation.**

The censor deletes what you said; the censure is the public scolding for having said it.

### In sentences
1. Wartime officials **censored** the soldiers' letters, cutting place names out of the page before the mail went home.
2. She **censored** herself mid-sentence, remembering that her boss was standing right behind her.

### In the wild
Every time Spotify offers you the **"Clean"** version next to the *Explicit* one, you're looking at a censored track — the same song with words surgically removed.

### Where it comes from
Latin **censēre** = *"to assess, to judge."* The Roman **censor** was a magistrate with two jobs: take the **census**, and police public morals — he could strike your name off the rolls for bad conduct.

That one root gives you a family: **census** (counting), **censor** (suppressing), **censure** (condemning), **censorious** (fault-finding).

🚫 **Not *sensor*.** That's Latin *sentīre*, "to feel" — *sense, sentiment, sentient, consent*. Identical sound, unrelated word.

# **champion** *(v. / n.)* — **CHAM-pee-uhn**

### Means
**Verb (the GRE sense):** to **fight publicly on behalf of** a cause, person, or idea — to defend and promote it.
**Noun:** a winner; or a defender who fights for someone else's cause.

The verb is the one that gets tested, and the everyday "winner" sense hides it. To champion something is stronger than to *support* it and more active than to *endorse* it: you attach your name and take the hits. A senator can quietly support a bill, but the senator who **champions** it is the one on the floor arguing for it week after week. Always positive about the causes' defender — though the GRE cheerfully has people champion terrible causes too.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **champ** fights. To **champion** a cause is to fight *for* it — not just to clap for it.

The "champ" sitting inside the word keeps the verb combative, which is exactly what separates championing from mere approving.

### In sentences
1. Throughout her tenure on the bench she **championed** the rights of defendants too poor to afford counsel.
2. He's been **championing** that scruffy little café for years — half the street eats there because of him.

### In the wild
Corporate and NGO language runs on this verb — *\"we champion diversity,\"* *\"championing open source.\"* Every time an organisation wants to sound like it's fighting for something rather than merely permitting it, it reaches for **champion**.

### Where it comes from
Latin **campus** = *"field"* → Late Latin **campiō**, *"a fighter in the field."* In medieval trial by combat, if you were too old, too ill, or too female to fight, you hired a **champion** to fight in your place — which is precisely why the verb still means *to fight on someone else's behalf*.

Same root: **camp**, **campaign** (originally an army taking the field), **campus**.

# **compound** *(v. / n. / adj.)* — **kuhm-POWND (v.) / KOM-pownd (n., adj.)**

*The stress flips: you kuhm-POWND a problem, but you live in a KOM-pownd.*

### Means
**Verb:** literally, to **put together** from parts — but the sense the GRE wants is **to make an already bad thing worse by adding to it**.
**Noun:** a mixture of combined elements (chemistry); also a walled-off group of buildings.
**Adjective:** made of more than one part — *a compound fracture, a compound sentence*.

*Aggravate* and *worsen* just say the trouble grew. **Compound** says a **second, separate act** was piled onto the first — lying about the crash *compounds* the crash. That's why it lands as quiet blame: the original problem may have been bad luck, but compounding it was a choice. It also carries the maths of **compound interest** — the damage feeds on itself and accelerates.

⚠️ **compound ≠ confound.** To **compound** a problem is to *worsen* it; to **confound** someone is to *baffle* them (or to *refute* an expectation — *the results confounded the sceptics*).

### Trick to lock it in
> **Compound interest** — the money grows on top of itself. Compound a mistake and the mistake starts earning interest.

The word never means a single loss; it means a loss that keeps generating more loss.

### In sentences
1. The airline's refusal to explain the delay only **compounded** the passengers' anger.
2. Don't **compound** it by texting him at 2am — you've done enough damage for one night.

### In the wild
News coverage keeps the odd noun sense alive: a raid on a **compound** in Abbottabad, the Kennedy **compound** at Hyannis Port — a walled cluster of buildings, not a chemical.

### Where it comes from
Latin **compōnere** = *"to put together"* (**com-** together + **pōnere** to place). One root, a whole shelf of words: **compose, component, composite, deposit, postpone, exponent, position**.

🚫 The walled-enclosure **compound** is a different word entirely — Malay **kampung**, *"village"*, brought back by traders and reshaped to look like the Latin one.

# **deter** *(v.)* — **dih-TER**

### Means
To stop someone from doing something by making the prospect of it frightening or costly — **prevention through fear of consequences**.

The mechanism matters: you don't *deter* someone with a good argument, you deter them with a threat, a risk, a price. *Dissuade* works on reasons; *deter* works on nerve. Note also that the target is usually the **action**, not the person's opinion — a deterred burglar still wants your TV, he just isn't willing to pay for it. It's the standard verb of security, policing, and defence policy, and it stays cool and neutral even when what's doing the deterring is a fence, a fine, or a warhead.

⚠️ **deter ≠ defer.**
- **deter** = *frighten off* an action
- **defer** = *postpone* it, or *yield* to someone's judgment (*I defer to the expert*)

One letter, opposite jobs: bad weather **deterred** the climbers; the climbers **deferred** the ascent.

### Trick to lock it in
> **deTERror** — hiding inside *deter* is **terror**, because that's the tool it uses.

If nobody's afraid of anything, nobody's been deterred — you've only persuaded them.

### In sentences
1. Steep fines have done little to **deter** illegal dumping along the riverbank.
2. Don't let one bad review **deter** you — just send the thing out again.

### In the wild
The Cold War ran on **deterrence**: neither side attacked because the retaliation would be unsurvivable. Same logic, smaller scale, on every "CCTV in operation" sign — the camera isn't there to catch you, it's there to put you off.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dē-** (*away*) + **terrēre** (*to frighten*) — literally *to frighten away*.

That *terrēre* is the big prize: **terror, terrify, terrible, terrific** (originally "causing terror"), and **deterrent**. Once you hear the terror in *deter*, the definition is automatic.

# **dexterous** *(adj.)* — **DEK-struhs**

*Also spelled dextrous; the three-syllable DEK-ster-uhs is equally correct.*

### Means
Skilful and quick, above all **with the hands** — a surgeon, a pickpocket, a pianist. Extended sense: mentally nimble, handling a tricky situation with ease.

This is plain praise for **fine motor control and speed**, with no hint of trickery in it — a dexterous fraudster is skilled at the *doing*, not sly by definition. Next to **adroit** (which drifts toward social and verbal cleverness) and **deft** (which adds lightness and grace), *dexterous* stays closest to the fingers.

### Trick to lock it in
> **DEK**-struhs — the card sharp who works the **deck** without you seeing his hands move.

The stressed syllable *is* "deck," which pins the word to fast, precise fingerwork rather than general cleverness.

### In sentences
1. A **dexterous** watchmaker can seat a hairspring thinner than a human hair without once resting his wrist.
2. He's weirdly **dexterous** with chopsticks for a guy who can't tie his own shoelaces.

### In the wild
**Dexter's Laboratory** — the boy genius named straight from Latin *dexter*, forever doing delicate precision work with tiny instruments.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dexter** = *"right, right-handed"* — and the right hand was the skilled, lucky one.

The pairing is the payoff: **dexter** (right) gave us *dexterous, dexterity, **ambidextrous*** ("right-handed on both sides"), while **sinister** (left) gave us *sinister* — the left hand was the clumsy, ill-omened one. Same prejudice built into French: **adroit** is literally *à droit*, "to the right."

# **dictum** *(n.)* — **DIK-tuhm**

*Plural is dicta (DIK-tuh), not "dictums," in formal and legal use.*

### Means
A **formal pronouncement from someone with the authority to make it** — or, more loosely, a **pithy saying that states a general truth**.

What separates it from *maxim*, *adage* and *proverb* is that a dictum **has a speaker behind it**. Proverbs are anonymous and worn smooth by centuries of use; a dictum is attributable — a judge's, a critic's, a founder's — and it carries the weight of that person's standing. "Less is more" is Mies van der Rohe's dictum; "look before you leap" is nobody's. It also leans official: in law, *obiter dicta* are the judge's remarks made in passing, authoritative in tone yet not actually binding.

**dictum ≠ diktat.** A **dictum** is an authoritative *saying* — you may quote it or ignore it. A **diktat** is a harsh order *imposed* on someone who has no choice (originally, terms forced on a defeated nation). Same Latin root, very different pressure.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dict**um is **dict**ated — it comes *down* from rank, never *up* from folklore.

That buried **dict-** ("say") is the same one in *dictate*, and it's the tell that a dictum always has an authority attached, unlike an anonymous proverb.

### In sentences
1. The court's ruling turned on a single **dictum** from a 1932 opinion that no one had cited in decades.
2. His whole management style boils down to one **dictum**: never send an email you wouldn't want screenshotted.

### In the wild
Any lawyer will tell you the first thing you learn reading a judgment is to separate the **holding** from the **dicta** — the part that binds from the part the judge just felt like saying.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dīcere** = *"to say"*; **dictum** = *"the thing said."*

One of the highest-yield roots on the exam: **dictate, dictator, dictionary, verdict** (*truly said*), **edict, contradict, predict, indict, benediction, malediction, valedictorian**.

# **dutiful** *(adj.)* — **DOO-ti-fuhl**

*British speakers say DYOO-ti-fuhl.*

### Means
Doing exactly what duty, custom, or family obligation requires — obedient, conscientious, reliably correct in conduct.

The word almost never describes passion. A **devoted** son visits because he wants to; a **dutiful** son visits because it's Sunday. Praise, yes — but cool praise, and in modern usage it tips easily into faint praise or quiet mockery: *dutiful applause*, *a dutiful nod*, *a dutiful phone call home*. The action is performed correctly and the feeling is missing. Watch the noun it attaches to: obligations (*dutiful wife, dutiful heir*) or hollow gestures (*a dutiful laugh*).

### Trick to lock it in
> **Duty-full** — full of duty, empty of desire.

The word is literally *duty* + *full*, and that's the whole nuance: the obligation is fully discharged, the enthusiasm isn't there.

### In sentences
1. A dutiful heir, he managed the estate for thirty years without once admitting he had wanted to be a painter.
2. She gave the joke a dutiful laugh and went back to her phone.

### In the wild
The Prodigal Son parable: the famous brother is the one who ran off, but the **dutiful** one is the elder brother who stayed home, did everything right, and sulked when the party was thrown for someone else — obedience without joy, in one character.

### Where it comes from
**Duty** comes from Old French *deü*, "that which is owed," from Latin **dēbēre** — *to owe*. So dutiful means "full of what you owe," which is exactly why it sounds like a debt being paid rather than a love being felt.

That root pays out elsewhere: **debt**, **debit**, **debenture**, **indebted** — and **endeavour**, from *en devoir*, "to put oneself in duty."

# **empathetic** *(adj.)* — **em-puh-THET-ik**

*empathic (em-PATH-ik) is the same word, preferred in psychology writing.*

### Means
Able to **enter another person's feelings** — to register what they're experiencing from the inside, rather than merely noting it.

It's warm and approving, and it implies *skill*, not just goodwill: an empathetic doctor, manager, or listener is credited with an ability. That's why it has become the favourite compliment of HR decks and leadership profiles — which also means it can sound faintly corporate if you overuse it. Note too that it says nothing about *acting*: you can be empathetic and do nothing. **Compassionate** adds the impulse to help.

⚠️ **empathetic ≠ sympathetic.**
- **empathetic** = feeling **with** you — I share the emotion
- **sympathetic** = feeling **for** you — I'm sorry it happened, from where I stand

A sympathetic nod at a funeral; an empathetic friend who has buried a parent too.

### Trick to lock it in
> **em-PATH-etic — you get *in* their PATH and walk it.**

The hidden word *path* is the whole distinction: empathy walks the other person's road, while sympathy calls out to them from your own.

### In sentences
1. The novel's power lies in its **empathetic** rendering of a mind unlike the reader's own.
2. She's the one you call when things go wrong — she's just weirdly **empathetic**, like she already knows.

### In the wild
Atticus Finch in *To Kill a Mockingbird*: *"You never really understand a person until you climb into his skin and walk around in it."* That sentence is the definition of empathetic, and it's built on the same image as the trick.

### Where it comes from
Greek **en-** (*in*) + **pathos** (*feeling, suffering*) — literally *feeling-into*.

One root, a whole GRE cluster: **sympathy** (feeling *with*), **antipathy** (feeling *against*), **apathy** (*no* feeling), **pathos**, **pathetic**, **telepathy**, and medical **pathology** (the study of suffering).

### Also sits in

- **Root family** · [pathos (feeling) + anti- (against): feeling against, feeling nothing, feeling into](groups.md#root__pathos) — with *antipathy, apathy*

# **ensue** *(v.)* — **en-SOO**

*British speakers often say en-SYOO.*

### Means
To **follow as a consequence** — to happen next, and happen *because* of what just happened.

Two things pin it down. First, it never takes an object: things ensue, you can't ensue anything. Second, it's a narrator's word — the register of reports, minutes, and news copy ("a scuffle ensued"), not of conversation. *Follow* is plain sequence; **ensue** insists on causal link and adds a faint formality, as if someone is writing the incident up afterwards.

⚠️ **ensue ≠ ensure.** *Ensue* = to follow as a result (no object). *Ensure* = to make certain of (always takes an object). Ensure the doors are locked, or chaos will ensue.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Look at what actually ensues: chaos, panic, silence, a lengthy legal battle.** Nothing good ever ensues.

The word's stock companions are all aftermath, so remember it as the verb for what spills out *after* the trouble starts.

### In sentences
1. When the treaty collapsed, three decades of intermittent border war **ensued**.
2. He knocked over the candle, and you can guess what **ensued**.

### In the wild
**"Hilarity ensues"** — the stock phrase in film blurbs and Netflix synopses for the chaos that follows the setup. It's so overused it's become a joke, which is exactly why it sticks.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sequī** = *"to follow"* (via Old French *ensivre*, "to follow upon"). That root is a GRE goldmine: **sequence, consequence, sequel, subsequent, pursue, prosecute, obsequious** (following your boss around), **non sequitur** (a statement that doesn't follow).

# **fathom** *(v. / n.)* — **FADH-uhm**

*The th is soft, as in this — not as in thin.*

### Means
**Verb:** to understand something fully after real effort — to get to the bottom of it.
**Noun:** a nautical unit of depth, six feet.

The verb keeps the sounding-line inside it: what you fathom is *deep*, murky, hard to reach. You **understand** a bus timetable; you **fathom** a motive, a grief, a decision that makes no sense. And it lives mostly in the negative — *I can't fathom why she said it* — so the word usually reports failure at the bottom of something, not success. Neutral in register, but faintly weary; the speaker has already tried.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fathom** = depth. So *can't fathom it* = **can't touch the bottom.**

Both senses are one idea — a fathom measures how deep the water is, and to fathom something is to reach the bottom of it, which is why the word shows up most often when you can't.

### In sentences
1. The commission never **fathomed** how the order had been approved, only that no one would admit to signing it.
2. I still can't **fathom** why he drove four hours to a party he left after ten minutes.

### In the wild
The Little Mermaid opens with the sailors' shanty **"Fathoms Below"** — a song about how deep the ocean goes, which is exactly the literal sense the verb is built on.

### Where it comes from
Old English **fæthm** = *the span of a man's outstretched arms*, about six feet. Sailors measured depth by hauling up the sounding rope and counting it off in arm-spans — so a **fathom** became a unit of depth, and *to fathom* became to find the bottom.

# **frank** *(adj.)* — **FRANK**

### Means
Open and direct in speech — saying the true thing plainly, without softening or evasion.

Three near-neighbours, three different temperatures. **Honest** just means not lying. **Blunt** means you didn't bother with tact and probably bruised someone. **Frank** sits between: the subject is delicate — money, failure, someone's chances — and you deal with it head-on anyway. That's why it so often comes pre-announced ("to be frank," "let me be frank with you"): the speaker is flagging that comfort is about to be traded for truth. Being called frank is usually a compliment; being called blunt usually isn't.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Frank** rhymes with **blank** — a frank answer is **point-blank**.

Point-blank gives you both halves at once: aimed straight at the awkward thing, and fired with no cushion in between.

### In sentences
1. The report is unusually **frank** about the trial's failures, naming the two sites where the data was simply never collected.
2. I'll be **frank** — you're not getting that promotion this cycle, so let's talk about next year.

### In the wild
**"Frankly, my dear, I don't give a damn."** — Rhett Butler's exit line in *Gone with the Wind*, routinely voted the most famous line in film history. It's the word doing exactly its job: he's about to say something brutal, so he warns you first.

### Where it comes from
From the **Franks**, the Germanic tribe that conquered Gaul. Under Frankish rule only Franks held full liberty — so *frank* came to mean **free**, then *free-speaking*.

That root is worth owning: **France** (land of the Franks), **franchise** and **enfranchise** (to grant a freedom or a vote), **lingua franca** (a free-trade common tongue), and **to frank** a letter — stamp it to travel *free* of postage.

# **germane** *(adj.)* — **jer-MAYN**

### Means
Relevant to the matter at hand — genuinely bearing on the point under discussion.

*Relevant* is the flat, everyday word; **germane** is what you reach for when relevance itself is the argument. It lives in debate, committee rooms and courtrooms, and it turns up negated more often than not: *"That's not germane."* It also claims more than mere topical overlap — a fact can be about the same subject and still not be germane, because germane means it actually attaches to *this* question. In parliamentary procedure there is literally a germaneness rule: an amendment must relate to the bill it's amending, or it's thrown out.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Germ**ane points grow from the same **germ** as the argument; everything else is a weed.

The word hides *germ* — a seed — and a germane remark sprouts from the very question being asked, not from the soil next door.

### In sentences
1. The judge ruled the defendant's charitable donations were not **germane** to the question of whether he had signed the contract.
2. Great story, but is any of this **germane** to why the server went down?

### In the wild
Shakespeare put it in *Hamlet*, mocking a courtier's overblown talk of duelling: **"The phrase would be more germane to the matter if we could carry cannon by our sides."**

### Where it comes from
Latin **germanus** = *"of the same parents"* — a full brother or sister. So a germane point is **kin** to the topic, not a stranger wandering in. That's also the old phrase **cousin-german**, meaning a first cousin.

Behind it sits **germen**, *"sprout, bud"* — which gives you **germ**, **germinate**, **germinal**.

🚫 **Nothing to do with Germany.** That name has a separate, murky origin; the resemblance is an accident.

# **grumble** *(v. / n.)* — **GRUM-buhl**

### Means
**Verb:** to complain in a low, muttering, half-audible way. **Noun:** that kind of complaint — or a low rumbling sound (thunder, an empty stomach).

Volume is the whole point. A **protest** is aimed at someone; a grumble is aimed at nobody in particular and is meant to be *overheard* rather than heard. It implies grudging compliance — the grumbler does the thing anyway, muttering the entire time. That makes it mild and faintly comic: you can grumble about the weather, the queue, your knees. Nobody grumbles about an atrocity.

### Trick to lock it in
> **G + rumble.** A grumble is a complaint that *rumbles* — low, continuous, never quite a bang.

The word literally contains *rumble*, which fixes both senses at once: the muttered griping and the belly noise.

### In sentences
1. The backbenchers **grumbled** about the new tax but filed obediently through the voting lobby.
2. Stop **grumbling** and pass me the spanner.

### In the wild
Your stomach **grumbling** before lunch — same word, same low rolling noise, which is exactly why the complaining sense feels the way it does.

# **hidebound** *(adj.)* — **HYDE-bownd**

### Means
Rigidly attached to old habits, rules, or opinions; **unable to bend, and unwilling to try**.

The word is always an attack. *Traditional* is neutral, *conservative* can be a stated position — **hidebound** says the rigidity is unthinking: inherited rules followed because they were inherited, with progress blocked as a side effect. It lands hardest on institutions (a hidebound faculty, a hidebound army, a hidebound bureaucracy) rather than on a single stubborn person, and it implies the thing has been left behind by the world around it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Bound in your own hide** — the skin has stopped stretching, so nothing inside can grow.

The word is literal: something wrapped so tightly in its own covering that it cannot expand, which is exactly what an institution's old rules do to it.

### In sentences
1. The reforms died in committee, smothered by a **hidebound** faculty that treated the syllabus of 1955 as scripture.
2. He's not old-fashioned, he's **hidebound** — the man still won't let anyone email him a document.

### In the wild
In business and political journalism it travels as a fixed pair: **hidebound bureaucracy**, **hidebound traditions**, **hidebound institution**. Spot the noun and you've spotted the word.

### Where it comes from
Originally a **cattle term**. A starving cow's **hide** clings so tightly to its ribs that it cannot be pulled loose and the animal can barely move — such a beast was *hide-bound*. The picture of a body trapped inside its own skin transferred straight onto minds and institutions.

# **hypocrite** *(n.)* — **HIP-uh-krit**

*The stress jumps in the noun form: hypocrisy is hi-POK-ruh-see, not HIP-oh-crissy.*

### Means
Someone who **professes beliefs, standards or feelings they don't actually hold or live by** — especially one who condemns in others a fault they themselves commit.

Note what the charge is *not*: a hypocrite need not be lying about the principle. The stated principle is often perfectly sound — the failure is in the living of it, which is why "that's hypocritical" attacks the speaker rather than refutes the argument. It's aimed at a person, not a statement, and it's among the harshest accusations ordinary English allows: not merely *wrong* but **morally counterfeit**. Also does double duty as a plain conversational insult ("what a hypocrite") without losing its bite.

⚠️ **hypocritical ≠ hypercritical.**
- **hypocritical** = preaching what you don't practise
- **hypercritical** = *excessively* fault-finding, nitpicking

A hypercritical boss finds fault with everything. A hypocritical one finds fault with what he does himself.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hypo**crit**e hides **crit** — he *criticises* in you exactly what he does himself.

The word points at a double standard, not a simple lie: one rule for you, another for me.

### In sentences
1. He denounced the delegates as **hypocrites** who preached austerity abroad and voted themselves subsidies at home.
2. Total **hypocrite** — she lectured me for an hour about screen time, then scrolled through dinner.

### In the wild
The most quoted line in English on the subject is Jesus in Matthew 7:5 — *"Thou **hypocrite**, first cast out the beam out of thine own eye; and then shalt thou see clearly to cast out the mote out of thy brother's eye."* Fussing over the speck in someone else's eye with a plank in your own **is** the word.

### Where it comes from
Greek **hypokritēs** = *"a stage actor"* — literally one who answers/plays a part, from **hypo-** (*under*) + **krinein** (*to judge, decide, separate*). The hypocrite is performing a character; the real man is the one under the mask.

**krinein** is a high-yield GRE root: **critic**, **critique**, **criterion**, **crisis** (the moment of decision), **diacritic**.

# **impolitic** *(adj.)* — **im-POL-uh-tik**

### Means
Unwise, ill-judged, badly calculated — especially a thing said or done that damages your own position.

The buried word is **politic**, which on its own means *shrewd, prudent, expedient* ("it seemed politic to say nothing"). So *impolitic* faults your **judgement, not your manners**: the remark may be perfectly courteous and still cost you the deal. It's a cool, understated word — the sort of thing a diplomat or a leader-writer says instead of "stupid," which is exactly why it stings.

⚠️ **impolitic ≠ impolite.**
- **impolite** = *rude* — a failure of manners
- **impolitic** = *unwise* — a failure of strategy

You can be flawlessly polite and deeply impolitic.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Impolite loses you a friend; impolitic loses you the room.**

The extra syllable turns bad manners into a bad move.

### In sentences
1. It would be impolitic to raise the merger at a dinner honouring the very firm we intend to absorb.
2. Telling your new boss her pet project is a mess on day one — accurate, sure, but wildly impolitic.

### In the wild
Standard press shorthand for a gaffe: when coverage calls a politician's comment "impolitic," it means the words were true or harmless enough — but they handed the other side a weapon.

### Where it comes from
Greek **polis** = *"city"* → **politikos**, "to do with running the city." Running a city takes cunning, so **politic** drifted from *civic* to *shrewd*.

That root pays out everywhere: **politics, policy, police, metropolis** (mother-city), **cosmopolitan** (citizen of the world), **acropolis** (high city).

# **insidious** *(adj.)* — **in-SID-ee-uhs**

### Means
Doing harm **gradually and secretly** — the damage advances while it looks harmless, or while nobody is looking at all.

Two things make a thing insidious: it's **slow**, and it's **camouflaged**. A mugging isn't insidious; a debt that quietly compounds is. Compare *sinister*, which advertises menace, and *pernicious*, which is simply very destructive whether or not it hides. Insidious is the one where the alarm comes **too late** — you discover it by finding the wreckage. That's why it attaches so naturally to diseases, addictions, propaganda, and slow-rotting workplace cultures.

⚠️ **insidious ≠ invidious.**
- **insidious** = harming stealthily
- **invidious** = provoking resentment or envy (*an invidious comparison*, *an invidious distinction*)

Ranking your friends by attractiveness is invidious. Slipping something into their drink is insidious.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Insid**ious — it's already **inside** you before you notice it.

The hidden word gets both halves of the meaning: concealment plus the fact that it has already got in and started working.

### In sentences
1. The most **insidious** propaganda never argues with you; it simply repeats a premise until you stop hearing it as a claim.
2. Sitting all day is **insidious** — you feel fine for years, then your back gives out at thirty-five.

### In the wild
**Insidious** (2010), James Wan's horror franchise — the whole premise is a presence that has quietly got *inside* the house and the child long before anyone realises anything is wrong. The title is doing exactly what the word means.

### Where it comes from
Latin **insidiae** = *"an ambush"* — literally *in-* (in) + **sedēre** (to sit). Troops **sitting in wait** by the roadside. Every part of the modern sense is in that picture: hidden, patient, lethal on contact.

**sedēre** is a workhorse root: **sedentary** (sitting), **reside**, **preside**, **sediment**, **session**, and the GRE favourite **assiduous** (sitting *at* a task).

# **intermediary** *(n. / adj.)* — **in-ter-MEE-dee-eh-ree**

*Five syllables; British speech often flattens the ending to -dee-uh-ree.*

### Means
**Noun:** a go-between — a person, firm, or thing that stands between two parties and passes something along (messages, money, goods, negotiations).
**Adjective:** occupying the middle position; intervening.

An intermediary is defined by **position, not power**. A *mediator* actively works to settle a dispute; an *arbitrator* decides it; an intermediary may do nothing more than carry the message across. The word is neutral in law and business — banks, brokers, and agents are intermediaries by trade — but it turns faintly sour whenever the implication is that the middle party is taking a cut for adding nothing, which is why "cut out the intermediary" is a selling point.

⚠️ **intermediary ≠ intermediate.**
- **intermediary** = the *party* in the middle (a broker)
- **intermediate** = the *position* in the middle (an intermediate stage, intermediate French)

A broker is an intermediary at an intermediate stage of the deal.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Inter-MEDIA-ry** — the *media* sit in the middle, carrying the message between two sides without being either one.

The word literally has *media* buried in it, and like the media an intermediary transmits rather than decides.

### In sentences
1. The treaty was negotiated through a Swiss **intermediary**, since neither government would be seen speaking to the other.
2. Just send it straight to her — I'm not being your **intermediary** every time you two fall out.

### In the wild
In economics a bank is textbook-labelled a **financial intermediary**: it doesn't own your money or the borrower's loan, it just sits between savers and borrowers and clips a margin. Every "no middleman" ad is selling *dis*intermediation.

### Where it comes from
Latin **inter** (*between*) + **medius** (*middle*) — literally "in the middle of."

*Medius* is a workhorse root: **medium**, **median**, **mediate**, **medieval** (the Middle Ages), **Mediterranean** (the sea in the middle of the earth), and **mediocre** — literally "halfway up the mountain," i.e. middling.

# **passive** *(adj.)* — **PAS-iv**

### Means
**Not acting; accepting or absorbing what happens without resistance, initiative, or protest.** In grammar, the voice in which the subject is acted *upon* rather than acting ("the ball **was thrown**").

Applied to a person it carries a quiet reproach, because it measures you against something you could have done and didn't. A *calm* person is at peace, a *docile* one obeys, a **passive** one simply stands there. It's milder than *supine* or *servile* — but rarely a compliment, except in technical pairings like *passive income*, *passive smoking*, *passive solar heating*, where it means nothing more than "without active effort."

⚠️ **passive ≠ impassive.**
- **passive** = takes no **action**
- **impassive** = shows no **emotion**

A negotiator can be impassive — stone-faced — while working the phones hard. That's the opposite of passive.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pass**ive: your turn comes and you **pass** — the game goes on without you.

The "pass" hook catches the crucial part: a turn was available to you, which is why passivity gets blamed and mere calmness doesn't.

### In sentences
1. Faced with mounting evidence of fraud, the board remained **passive**, minuting its concerns and doing nothing further.
2. Don't be so **passive** — if you want Friday off, just go and ask for it.

### In the wild
**"Mistakes were made."** The classic political non-apology is also the textbook specimen of the **passive** voice: something happened, and no one did it. Every style guide holds it up as the sentence that hides its own actor.

### Where it comes from
Latin **patī, passus** = *"to suffer, to undergo"* — to be on the receiving end.

That root pays off widely: **patient** (one who undergoes), **compassion** (suffering *with*), **passion** — which originally meant *suffering*, which is why the Crucifixion is called the **Passion** of Christ, not a romance. **Passive** is the plain adjective of the family: you don't do, you're done to.

# **raillery** *(n.)* — **RAY-luh-ree**

*Also heard as RAL-uh-ree; both are accepted.*

### Means
Light-hearted teasing — joking at someone's expense in a way that's meant to amuse, not wound.

The whole force of the word sits in its **warmth**. *Mockery*, *derision* and *ridicule* are attacks; raillery is a social pleasure, exchanged between people who like each other and expect it to be returned. It's also **witty by definition** — clumsy jabs don't qualify. Register is polished and slightly old-fashioned: dinner tables, drawing rooms, and essays about them. Modern equivalent: **banter**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Raillery stays on the rails** — it teases without ever derailing into cruelty.

The moment it hurts, it stops being raillery and becomes mockery.

### In sentences
1. His after-dinner **raillery** spared no one at the table, yet no one left offended.
2. She put up with a solid ten minutes of **raillery** about her new haircut before firing back.

### In the wild
A Jane Austen word: the drawing-room sport of teasing a friend about who she's in love with, kept just short of rudeness, is exactly what her novels call raillery.

### Where it comes from
French **raillerie**, from **railler** *"to tease."* The same French verb gave English **rail** (v.) — *to rail against* something — which kept the heat and lost the humour. One root, two temperatures: **raillery** teases, **railing** rants. The old sense of **rally** (*"to rally someone about"* = to tease them) is the same word too.

# **ramble** *(v. / n.)* — **RAM-buhl**

### Means
**Verb:** to wander about with no fixed destination; or, of speech and writing, to go on at length, drifting from point to point.
**Noun:** a leisurely walk taken for its own sake.

The key is *aimless, but not lost* — a rambler is perfectly content, just going nowhere in particular. That's why the walking sense is warm (a Sunday **ramble** through the hills) while the talking sense is a soft insult: a **rambling** speaker isn't confused or dishonest, merely tiring. You accuse someone of rambling when they've buried the point, not when they've got it wrong — the complaint is about tedium and drift, never about error.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ramble** hides **amble** — a slow stroll with the R for *roam* stuck on the front.

Whether it's your legs or your sentences, an amble that roams has no destination — pleasant on a hillside, exhausting in a meeting.

2. Sorry, I'm **rambling** — what I actually meant was: don't sign it.

### In the wild
Led Zeppelin's **"Ramble On"** — the title *is* the definition: keep moving, no destination, wherever the road goes.

# **resent** *(v.)* — **rih-ZENT**

*Stress the second syllable. **RE-sent** (REE-sent, "sent again") is a different word entirely — same spelling, no hyphen, no connection.*

### Means
To feel bitter, aggrieved ill-will toward someone or something you consider unfair.

Resentment is **slow-burning and stored**. Anger flares and dies; resentment is kept — nursed quietly over months, often unspoken, and it always contains a claim of injustice ("I deserved better"). You can resent a person, a duty, a promotion someone else got, even a remark. And it says as much about the resenter as the offender: to resent is to be *stuck* on something, which is why "resentful" is faintly unflattering even when the grievance is fair.

### Trick to lock it in
> **re-sent** — you feel it again. And again. And again.

The *re-* is literal: resentment is a grievance replayed on a loop, which is exactly what separates it from a one-off flash of anger.

### In sentences
1. Passed over a third time, she **resented** not the appointment itself but the silence that surrounded it.
2. He says he's fine covering the shift, but you can tell he **resents** it.

### In the wild
The much-repeated line **"Resentment is like drinking poison and waiting for the other person to die"** — quoted everywhere from recovery meetings to Carrie Fisher's *Wishful Drinking*. It nails the key fact: resentment sits and corrodes *inside* the person holding it.

### Where it comes from
French **ressentir** = *"to feel back, feel again"* — **re-** + **sentir**, from Latin **sentīre**, *"to feel."*

That root is a GRE workhorse: **sentiment**, **sentient**, **consent** (feel *with*), **dissent** (feel *apart*), **sensation**, **resentment**.

# **residual** *(adj. / n.)* — **ri-ZIJ-oo-uhl**

### Means
**Adj.:** remaining after the greater part has gone, been removed, or been used up.
**Noun:** the leftover amount itself — and in statistics, the gap between what a model predicted and what actually happened.

The word implies a *process that already did its work*. Rain leaves **residual** damp; chemotherapy leaves **residual** tumour; a fight leaves **residual** resentment. So the quantity is usually small, often faint, and frequently the part nobody wanted — the bit that stubbornly failed to clear. Compare plain *remaining*, which is neutral about how the remainder got there, and *residue*, which is physical gunk you could scrape off. *Residual* is the clinical, engineering, accounting word: measured, quantified, and slightly clinical in tone even when applied to feelings.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Residual** = what still **resides** after everyone else has moved out.

The hidden word *reside* is literally the same root, and it captures the tone — not the main body of a thing, just the last tenant that wouldn't leave.

### In sentences
1. Two years after the recall, the company was still absorbing the **residual** costs of the defect.
2. The paint's dry, but there's a **residual** smell — crack a window.

### In the wild
In Hollywood, **residuals** are the payments actors and writers get every time a film or show gets rerun or streamed — precisely the leftover income that dried up under streaming, and the core grievance of the 2023 SAG-AFTRA and WGA strikes.

### Where it comes from
Latin **residēre** = *"to sit back, stay behind"* — *re-* (back) + *sedēre* (to sit).

That *sedēre* root is a GRE goldmine: **sedentary** (sitting), **sediment** (what sits at the bottom), **preside** (sit in front), **subside** (sit back down), **session**, **assiduous** (sitting at it doggedly).

# **resolve** *(v. / n.)* — **ri-ZOLV**

*Unlike record or rebel, the noun does not shift stress — it's ri-ZOLV both times, never REE-zolv.*

### Means
**Verb:** to settle something unsettled — a dispute, a doubt, a plot — or to decide firmly to do a thing. Technically: to break something down into its parts (*resolve a force into vectors*, *resolve two stars through a telescope*).
**Noun:** hard, settled determination.

The verb's quiet claim is **finality**: you don't *resolve* an argument you merely paused, and a story that resolves has no loose ends left. The noun is stronger and more dignified than *determination* — it names the firmness that survives *after* doubt has been faced, which is why it collocates with strain: *steely resolve*, *her resolve wavered*, *test his resolve*. You can be determined about lunch; resolve is reserved for things that cost something.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Re-solve = un-loosen the knot.** Untangle the mess (settle it, split it into strands) and what's left standing is your **resolve**.

The root means *loosen*, so every sense is a knot coming apart — a dispute, an image, a doubt — and the noun is what remains once the doubt is gone.

### In sentences
1. Sanctions were intended to break the government's **resolve**; eighteen months on, they had only hardened it.
2. We finally **resolved** the seating chart at 1 a.m., and honestly nobody won.

### In the wild
Your monitor's **resolution** — 1080p, 4K — is exactly this word's technical sense: how finely the screen can *separate* the image into distinguishable dots. High resolution = more loosened apart into detail.

### Where it comes from
Latin **re-** + **solvere** = *"to loosen, untie."* The knot-loosening idea runs through the whole family: **solve** (untie the problem), **dissolve** (loosen apart in liquid), **absolve** (untie you from blame), **soluble**, **solution**.

Note the shape: a *solution* of salt and a *solution* to a puzzle are the same word, because both are things coming undone.

# **surrogate** *(n. / adj.)* — **SUR-uh-gut**

*Noun and adjective end in a mumbled -gut; the rarer verb is SUR-uh-gayt.*

### Means
A **substitute** — a person or thing put in someone else's place to carry out their role.

The word is fussier than *replacement* or *substitute*. A surrogate takes over a **role that properly belongs to another**: a surrogate mother, a surrogate father figure, a surrogate decision-maker for an unconscious patient, a surrogate war fought by proxies for the great powers. There's usually a faint second-best flavour — the surrogate does the job, but everyone knows who was supposed to be there. In US political coverage it's a standard term of art: a *campaign surrogate* is the deputy sent out to speak in the candidate's voice.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A surrogate doesn't replace a *thing* — it replaces a *relationship*: surrogate mother, surrogate family, surrogate war.**

You'd never call a spare tyre a surrogate, because the word wants a role someone else was meant to fill.

### In sentences
1. The court appointed a **surrogate** to consent to treatment on the patient's behalf.
2. Her flatmate's dog has become a full **surrogate** child — she talks to it more than to actual people.

### In the wild
**Surrogate mother** is the phrase that keeps the word alive in ordinary speech — a woman who carries a pregnancy in another's place. Note it's exactly the *role*, not the person's body parts, that the word is naming.

### Where it comes from
Latin **subrogāre** = *"to ask in place of another"* — *sub-* here meaning "in place of," plus **rogāre**, *"to ask, to propose."* Originally the Roman act of proposing a substitute official.

**rogāre** is a high-yield GRE root: **interrogate** (ask between/thoroughly), **arrogant** (claiming for oneself), **prerogative** (asked for first — a privilege), **abrogate** (ask away — repeal), **derogatory** (asking down — belittling).

# **vilify** *(v.)* — **VIL-uh-fye**

*One l, not two — vilify, not "villify."*

### Means
To attack someone's reputation in public — to write or speak about them as wicked, worthless, contemptible.

Scale and intent are what mark it out. You *criticise* an argument, you *insult* a person to their face, but you **vilify** someone over weeks, in print, to a crowd — it's sustained character assassination aimed at turning opinion against them. And it leans heavily toward the unjust: the word arrives most often as **"unfairly vilified,"** with the writer signalling that the target didn't deserve it. Use it about your own side and you're claiming victimhood, not describing debate.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Vile + -ify** — you don't just call someone vile, you *manufacture* their vileness.

The **-ify** suffix means *make into* (purify, falsify), so vilifying is a production process, not a single insult.

### In sentences
1. For a decade the pamphleteers **vilified** her as a traitor, until the archives were opened and the charge collapsed.
2. Don't **vilify** the guy in the group chat — he missed one deadline, he didn't burn the office down.

### In the wild
**"Vilify"** is a single by Device (David Draiman's band, 2013) — a Mainstream Rock hit built entirely on the idea of being torn down in public.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vīlis** = *"cheap, of low value"* + **-ficāre** (*to make*). To vilify is literally to **cheapen** a person — to mark them down in the public estimate.

Same root: **vile**, **revile**, **vilification**.

🚫 **Not from *villain*.** That's Latin *villa* — a farmhand, a peasant, someone tied to the estate. Everyone assumes vilify = "make into a villain"; the real image is a price tag being slashed.

# **wanting** *(adj.)* — **WON-ting**

*o as in hot — not "won".*

### Means
**Lacking; absent altogether, or present but falling short of the required standard.** *The evidence was wanting.* / *His technique was found wanting.*

The everyday sense — *wanting a coffee* — is desire. The GRE sense flips the direction: the deficiency is in the **thing**, not a craving in the person. Two shades sit inside it: total absence (*rainfall was wanting that year*) and, more commonly, **failure to measure up** when something is tested against a standard. That second shade is quietly damning — "found wanting" is the verdict of an examiner, an auditor, a judge, and it stings more than *inadequate* because it implies the thing was actually weighed and came up short. Formal, faintly biblical, and almost always negative.

⚠️ **wanting ≠ wanton.** *Wanting* = lacking, deficient. **Wanton** (WON-tuhn) = unrestrained, malicious, or lewd — *wanton cruelty*. One is short of something; the other has no limits at all.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A wanting dish isn't hungry — it's missing the salt.**

The want belongs to the object, which is short of something, not to a person who desires it.

### In sentences
1. The committee reviewed the safety protocols and found them **wanting** in every respect that mattered.
2. I tried his so-called famous lasagne and, honestly, it was a bit **wanting**.

### In the wild
The phrase's home is the writing on the wall in **Daniel 5** — Belshazzar is told *"Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting."* That single line is why "found wanting" still carries the sound of a final verdict.

### Where it comes from
**Want** entered English from Old Norse **vanta**, *"to lack"* — and lacking was the *original* meaning. The desire sense came later and second-hand: "I want bread" began life as *bread is lacking to me*. So the GRE meaning isn't a strange outlier; it's the older, straighter sense, still visible in **wanting** and in *for want of a nail*.

# **willful** *(adj.)* — **WIL-fuhl**

*British spelling: wilful, one l.*

### Means
Two senses, both unflattering.

**1. Headstrong** — set on having your own way, deaf to advice or authority. Said of children, mules, and adults behaving like both.

**2. Deliberate** — done knowingly, on purpose, when you should have known better: *willful negligence, willful blindness, willful destruction of evidence*.

The link between the two is **blame**. *Intentional* and *voluntary* are neutral descriptions of a state of mind; **willful** is a charge. Sense 2 is the harsher one precisely because it rules out the excuse — you didn't slip, you *chose*, and choosing is worse. Even sense 1 is never praise: a determined person has resolve, a willful person has a problem with being told.

⚠️ **willful ≠ willing.** Same root, opposite temperament: **willing** bends to *your* wishes, **willful** insists on *its own*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Will + full** — so full of his own will there's no room for yours.

It isn't a lack of will but a surplus, aimed only at itself — which is why the word always sounds like a complaint.

### In sentences
1. The court found the breach **willful** rather than inadvertent, and trebled the damages accordingly.
2. She's a **willful** kid — tell her not to touch it and that's the only thing she wants to touch.

### In the wild
Read almost any contract or insurance policy and you'll hit the carve-out **"except in cases of gross negligence or willful misconduct"** — the clause that says a company can cap its liability for mistakes, but never for things it did on purpose.
