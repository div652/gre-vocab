# **acrimonious** *(adj.)* — **ak-ruh-MOH-nee-uhs**

*The noun pulls the stress to the front: acrimony = AK-ruh-muh-nee.*

### Means
Bitter, sharp-tongued and **hostile** — used of quarrels, debates, negotiations and breakups.

The word almost never describes a person; it describes the **atmosphere between two sides**, usually sides that were once allies. A stranger insulting you is rude, not acrimonious. A business partnership, a marriage, a band — those dissolve acrimoniously, because the bitterness is *earned*. It's a journalist's word: cool, formal, faintly disapproving of everyone involved.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ACRI-** like **acrid** smoke, **-MONIOUS** like **matri*mony*** — acrimonious is matrimony gone acrid.

The word's single most common companion is *divorce*, and both halves of it point there.

### In sentences
1. The merger talks collapsed after an **acrimonious** final session in which each delegation accused the other of bad faith.
2. They're still fighting over the dog — it got pretty **acrimonious**.

### In the wild
The 2009 **Oasis** split is the textbook example — every account of the Gallagher brothers' backstage fight in Paris reaches for the same adjective.

### Where it comes from
Latin **acer, acris** = *"sharp, keen, bitter."* Literally, bitterness of temper.

The root is worth a lot elsewhere: **acrid** (sharp smell), **acerbic** (sharp wit), **acute** (sharp angle), **exacerbate** (make sharper), even **eager** and **vinegar** (*vin aigre*, "sour wine").

# **belligerent** *(adj. / n.)* — **buh-LIJ-uh-ruhnt**

### Means
**Adj.:** aggressively hostile — actively looking for or carrying on a fight.
**Noun:** a nation or party **engaged in a war** ("the belligerents met in Geneva").

Angry is a feeling; **belligerent is a posture**. The belligerent person isn't sulking or seething — they're leaning in, raising their voice, daring you to answer. It's the standard word in two very different registers: diplomats use it neutrally for combatant states, and police reports and bar stories use it for the man who won't leave quietly. Both keep the same core: hostility that has already moved into action.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Bell**igerent — he's already rung the **bell** for round one.

The fight is underway, not merely brewing — that's what separates belligerent from plain irritable.

### In sentences
1. Both **belligerents** claimed the ceasefire had been broken by the other side.
2. Three drinks in, he got **belligerent** and started shouting at the bartender.

### In the wild
It's the fixed phrase in incident reports and news copy: *"the passenger became belligerent and was removed from the flight."* Nobody writes "became angry" — belligerent signals it escalated.

### Where it comes from
Latin **bellum** (*war*) + **gerere** (*to wage, to carry on*) — literally *war-waging*.

**bellum** unlocks a cluster: **antebellum** (before the war), **bellicose** (itching for a fight), **rebel** (*re-* + *bellum*, to wage war back), **casus belli** (the trigger for war).

🚫 **Not *bene-* (good).** *Benevolent, benefit, benign* are a different root entirely — one letter apart, opposite worlds.

# **beneficent** *(adj.)* — **buh-NEF-ih-suhnt**

*Stress falls on NEF, not on BEN — it is not "BEN-uh-fih-sent".*

### Means
Doing good; generous and kindly **in action** — actually conferring benefits on people.

The word points at deeds, not feelings. A beneficent donor has already funded the ward; a benevolent one is fond of the idea. It also carries a slight tilt of power: the beneficent party is usually the one with means — a patron, a monarch, a foundation — bestowing good downward. That makes it warm but a touch grand, and easy to turn ironic ("our beneficent landlord").

Keep it apart from **beneficial** too: *beneficial* describes things that do good (exercise, rainfall); *beneficent* describes agents who do good.

⚠️ **beneficent ≠ benevolent.**
- **beneficent** = *does* good — `bene` + **fic** (facere, to do)
- **benevolent** = *wishes* good — `bene` + **vol** (velle, to wish)

A benevolent billionaire smiles at the hospital appeal. A beneficent one pays for it.

### Trick to lock it in
> Benefi**cent** hides **benefit** — someone actually got one.

If no benefit has changed hands, the word you want is *benevolent*.

### In sentences
1. The college owes its library, its observatory and half its endowment to a single **beneficent** industrialist who never once visited.
2. My aunt had a **beneficent** phase where she'd turn up unannounced with groceries and pay off your phone bill.

### In the wild
Disney's **Maleficent** is this word's exact evil twin: *male-* (ill) + *-fic-* (doing) = **ill-doing**, built on the same frame as *bene-* + *-fic-* = **good-doing**. Swap the first syllable and you flip the fairy.

### Where it comes from
Latin **bene** (*well*) + **facere** (*to do, to make*).

**bene-** gives you *benefit, benefactor, benediction* (well-speaking), *benign*, *benevolent*. **-fic-/facere** gives you *factory, manufacture, efficient, artifice* — and *malefactor*, the beneficent man's opposite number.

# **canny** *(adj.)* — **KAN-ee**

### Means
Shrewd and careful — quick to spot an advantage and hard to cheat, especially in money, bargaining, or judging people.

*Cunning* and *crafty* hint that someone is working an angle on you; **canny** hands out credit. It's the praise you give a person who never overpays, never signs the bad contract, and knows exactly when to walk away. Note the scale, too: canny is quiet, practical competence, not dazzling brilliance — a canny investor is careful, not a genius.

**canny ≠ the opposite of uncanny.** *Uncanny* means eerie, unsettlingly strange (*an uncanny resemblance*) — it is not "unshrewd." The pair split centuries ago; treat them as separate words.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Canny** is just **can** + **-y** — he *can* see what you're up to.

The hidden *can* is literal: the word grew out of the old sense of *can* meaning "to know how," so a canny person is the one who knows how.

### In sentences
1. The negotiators were **canny** enough to let the other side name a figure first.
2. Buying that flat before the line opened was a **canny** move — it's worth double now.

### In the wild
Sports and business journalism run on it: a transfer window bargain is always "a **canny** bit of business," a veteran manager "a **canny** operator."

### Where it comes from
Scots and northern English, from **can** in its old sense *"to know, to know how"* — the same root as **ken** (Scots for "know"), **cunning** (originally just "knowledgeable"), and **know** itself.

That root also explains **uncouth**: *un- + couth*, "not known" → unfamiliar → boorish. And **uncanny** started as "not safely known" — beyond ordinary knowledge — which is how it drifted to *eerie* while *canny* stayed with worldly know-how.

# **cavalier** *(adj. (also n.))* — **kav-uh-LEER**

### Means
**Adjective:** offhand and dismissive about something that deserves care or seriousness.
**Noun (historical):** a horseman or gallant — specifically a Royalist supporter of Charles I.

The insult isn't laziness or cruelty; it's **airy unconcern from above**. A *careless* doctor made a mistake. A *cavalier* doctor waved the risk away as beneath discussing — the fault is the attitude, not the error. It implies the speaker thinks you're too grand, or too breezy, to take the stakes seriously, and it's a word you use about people with some power to be casual with.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cavalier** almost always comes with **about** — and whatever follows *about* is something that deserved better: safety, facts, other people's money.

Nobody is ever cavalier about something trivial, so the word convicts the person the moment it's used.

### In sentences
1. The report condemned the firm's **cavalier** disregard for basic safety procedure.
2. Don't be so **cavalier** about the deadline — that thing is due Friday and you've written nothing.

### In the wild
The **Cleveland Cavaliers** — sword-and-horseman logo, named for exactly the swaggering mounted gentlemen the word came from.

### Where it comes from
Latin **caballus** = *"horse"* → Italian *cavaliere*, "horseman, knight." A cavalier was a gentleman on horseback, looking down — literally — on everyone on foot. That lofty ease curdled into the modern insult.

Same horse gives you **cavalry**, **cavalcade**, and (via French *cheval*) **chivalry** and **chevalier**.

Note the split: **chivalrous** kept the horseman's honour, **cavalier** kept his arrogance.

# **distressed** *(adj. (also v., past tense of distress))* — **dih-STREST**

### Means
Suffering — upset, anxious, in pain or in trouble.

It sits below *distraught*: a distressed person is visibly struggling but still coping; a distraught one has come apart. It's also the calm, official word for someone else's crisis — *a distressed passenger*, *a vessel in distress*, *fetal distress*. You rarely say "I'm distressed" about yourself; it's what the report says about you.

Two specialist senses the GRE likes: in finance, **distressed** assets or debt belong to a company in trouble, so they sell cheap. In design, **distressed** leather, wood or denim has been deliberately battered to look worn — the only sense where the word is a selling point.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Stress** is sitting right inside it — and *dis-* stretches that stress until something gives.

Whatever has been stretched past comfort is distressed: a person, a company's balance sheet, or a pair of jeans.

### In sentences
1. The lifeboat crew reached the **distressed** vessel an hour before the storm closed in.
2. She turned up **distressed** jeans and a distressed face, having paid extra for one of them.

### In the wild
**Distressed denim** — the rips and fading you pay a premium for. Same word, same idea: fabric put under strain until it looks like it's been through something.

### Where it comes from
Latin **distringere** = *"to stretch apart, draw tight"* (*dis-* apart + *stringere* to pull tight).

That *stringere* root is a GRE goldmine: **stringent** (tightly binding), **astringent** (makes tissue contract), **strict**, **constrict**, **restrict** — and **district**, originally the territory a lord could *draw tight* under his control.

# **dwindling** *(adj. / v. (pres. part. of dwindle))* — **DWIN-dling**

### Means
Steadily shrinking, bit by bit, in a way that points toward nothing left.

*Decreasing* is neutral arithmetic and can stop anywhere; *plummeting* is fast and dramatic. **Dwindling** is slow, continuous, and already sad — it carries the implication that the thing will eventually run out. It also has no villain: supplies dwindle, crowds dwindle, hope dwindles, all by themselves, with nobody doing it. That's why it attaches to things you're counting anxiously — funds, numbers, reserves, time.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dwindle** is **swindle** with one letter changed — and both take your money slowly, a little at a time.

The rhyme fixes the gradualness: nothing is seized in one grab, it just quietly gets less and less.

### In sentences
1. Attendance at the village church has **dwindled** from three hundred to a scattered dozen in the front pews.
2. We started with a full tank and a big plan, and by Tuesday both were **dwindling** fast.

### In the wild
It's the default word of business and climate reporting: *dwindling cash reserves*, *dwindling glaciers*, *dwindling subscriber numbers*. If you learn one collocation, learn **dwindling reserves** — it's practically a fixed phrase.

### Where it comes from
Old English **dwīnan** = *"to waste away, languish."* The **-le** on the end is the English "keep doing it, little by little" suffix — the same one in *spark-le, crumb-le, trick-le, nib-ble*. So *dwindle* is literally **to go on wasting away, in small instalments** — the suffix, not the root, is what encodes the slowness.

# **eclipse** *(v. / n.)* — **ih-KLIPS**

### Means
**Noun:** one body passing in front of another and blotting out its light — a solar or lunar eclipse.
**Verb (the GRE sense):** to **outshine so completely that the other thing is no longer noticed.**

The eclipsed thing isn't beaten or destroyed — it's still there, just invisible while something brighter stands in front of it. That's what separates *eclipse* from *defeat* or *surpass*: it's about **attention**, not victory. It also implies a before-and-after — a predecessor, an earlier record, an older sibling — so it very often shows up in the passive: *his early novels were eclipsed by the trilogy.* Neutral in itself, but it usually carries a faint sadness for whatever got left in the shade.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Eclipse** has **clips** in it — you get clipped out of the picture, not knocked out of it.

Being eclipsed means still being there and simply not being seen.

### In sentences
1. Her later diplomatic career so thoroughly **eclipsed** her time on the bench that few recall she was ever a judge.
2. Great party, but the free bar completely **eclipsed** the speeches — nobody remembers a word of them.

### In the wild
Bonnie Tyler's **"Total Eclipse of the Heart"** — the karaoke standard. The heart isn't gone; it's blotted out.

### Where it comes from
Greek **ekleipein** = *"to leave out, to fail to appear"* — **ek-** ("out") + **leipein** ("to leave"). The sun doesn't die during an eclipse; it *fails to show up.*

Same **leip-** root gives you **ellipsis** (the three dots marking words left out) and **ellipse** (a circle with something left out of it).

# **encyclopedic** *(adj.)* — **en-seye-kluh-PEE-dik**

*British spelling: encyclopaedic. Same sound.*

### Means
Covering a huge range of a subject, in exhaustive detail — the way an encyclopedia does.

Nearly always it lands on **someone's knowledge or memory**, and it's a compliment with a whiff of hyperbole: *encyclopedic knowledge of 1970s soul*, *an encyclopedic memory for faces*. It praises **breadth plus retrievable detail** — not depth of insight. A scholar can be encyclopedic and still say nothing original.

Turn it on a book, a film or a writing style and the warmth drains out: **encyclopedic** there means comprehensive to the point of being flat, catalogue-like, no argument, just everything.

⚠️ **encyclopedic ≠ encyclical.** An **encyclical** is a letter from the Pope circulated to the whole Church — same *circle* root, completely different word. Nothing encyclopedic about it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **En-CYCL-o-pedic** — the *cycle* is the giveaway: it goes all the way **round** a subject, leaving no arc uncovered.

The praise is for full coverage of the circle, not for how deep any one point is drilled.

### In sentences
1. His notes on the campaign were **encyclopedic** — every rally, every donor, every misquote — yet he never ventured a single conclusion.
2. She's got **encyclopedic** knowledge of the Marvel films; don't start an argument with her about timelines.

### In the wild
Wikipedia editors slap articles with a maintenance tag for not being written in an **"encyclopedic tone"** — meaning: neutral, comprehensive, no opinions, no jokes. That tag *is* the second sense of the word.

### Where it comes from
Greek **enkyklios paideia** = *"education in the circle"* — the full round of general learning every free citizen was supposed to have.

- **kyklos** = circle → *cycle, bicycle, cyclone, encyclical*
- **paideia** = the rearing of children, from **pais/paidos** = child → *pediatric, pedagogue, pedant*

🚫 The **ped-** here is **child**, not **foot**. *Pedal, pedestrian, pedestal* come from Latin *pes/pedis* — unrelated.

# **exacerbate** *(v.)* — **ig-ZAS-er-bayt**

*The c is soft — never "eks-uh-KER-bate."*

### Means
To make an already bad thing **worse** — more severe, more painful, more inflamed.

The word carries a precondition: there must already be a problem. You can worsen anything, but you can only *exacerbate* a deficit, a drought, a shortage, tensions, an injury, an illness. And it takes an impersonal subject — a policy, a comment, a heatwave exacerbates; people generally don't. Register is formal: it's the verb of medical charts, editorials, and policy reports, where **worsen** would sound too plain and **aggravate** too casual.

⚠️ **exacerbate ≠ exasperate.**
- **exacerbate** = make a bad *situation* worse
- **exasperate** = make a *person* furious

The strike exacerbated the shortage; the shortage exasperated the shoppers.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside is **acerb** — bitter. To exacerbate is to add bitterness to something already sour.

Nothing sweet can be exacerbated — the sourness has to be there first.

### In sentences
1. Rather than calming the crowd, the mayor's remarks **exacerbated** a resentment that had been building for years.
2. Don't text him at 2 a.m. — you'll just **exacerbate** things.

### In the wild
Standard hospital language: an **"acute exacerbation of COPD"** is a flare-up of a disease the patient already has — exactly the word's precondition, printed on charts every day.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** (thoroughly) + **acerbus** = *"bitter, harsh"*, from **acer** = *"sharp."*

That root cuts across the vocabulary list: **acerbic** (sharp-tongued), **acrid** (sharp smell), **acrimonious** (sharp-tempered dispute), **acute** (sharp angle, sharp pain) — and even **eager**, which once meant "keen, sharp," and **vinegar**, French *vin aigre*, "sour wine."

# **exasperated** *(adj. (past tense of *exasperate*, v.))* — **ig-ZAS-puh-ray-tid**

### Means
Pushed past the end of your patience — intensely irritated and worn down, usually by something that has happened *again*.

The word carries repetition and fatigue. You get **angry** in one moment; you become **exasperated** after the fifth time you've explained it, the third failed attempt, the child who has asked "why?" eleven times. It's also fairly powerless: the exasperated person has tried the reasonable options and run out. That's why it collocates almost automatically with **sigh**, **groan**, **eye-roll** — never with *punch*.

⚠️ **exasperate ≠ exacerbate.**
- **exasperate** = irritate a *person* past their patience
- **exacerbate** = make a *problem* worse

Your excuses exasperate me; they exacerbate the situation.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Exasperated ends in a sigh, not a shout.**

It's the deflated, worn-thin end of patience after one repetition too many — not a sudden explosion of rage.

### In sentences
1. After three hours of circular debate, the chairwoman gave an **exasperated** wave of the hand and adjourned the meeting.
2. I'm getting **exasperated** — that's the fourth time I've sent you the same link.

### Where it comes from
Latin **asper** = *"rough"*, with **ex-** as an intensifier: to *thoroughly roughen* someone — rub them raw.

Same root gives you **asperity** (harshness of tone or manner) — a GRE word in its own right. Keep the pair: *asperity* is the rough edge; *exasperated* is what being scraped by it repeatedly leaves you.

# **fungible** *(adj.)* — **FUN-juh-buhl**

*Soft g, as in *gem* — not the hard g of *fungus*.*

### Means
Interchangeable: each unit is **perfectly substitutable** for another of the same kind, so which particular one you get makes no difference. A dollar, a barrel of crude, a share of stock, a bushel of wheat.

The word is stronger than *similar* or *replaceable* — fungible things aren't merely alike, they're **indistinguishable for the purpose at hand**. It comes out of law and finance and still carries that clipped, contractual tone. Applied to things it's neutral bookkeeping; applied to **people** it turns cold fast: calling workers fungible says they're units, not individuals.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nobody's wedding ring is fungible. Their cash is.

The test is whether swapping in an identical unit would bother you at all — if yes, it isn't fungible.

### In sentences
1. Under the terms of the warehouse receipt the grain was **fungible**: any ton in the silo discharged the obligation.
2. Management talks like engineers are **fungible** — swap one out, ship the same code.

### In the wild
**NFT** = *non-fungible token*. The whole point of the name: every other crypto coin is fungible (your Bitcoin = my Bitcoin), but an NFT is one specific, non-swappable item.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fungī** = *"to perform, discharge a duty"* — from the phrase *fungī vice*, "to serve in place of." That root also gives you **function**, **perfunctory** (going through the motions), and **defunct** (done performing).

🚫 **Nothing to do with *fungus*.** Different Latin word, and the g is soft.

# **hackneyed** *(adj.)* — **HAK-need**

*Three letters, two syllables — not "hack-nee-yed".*

### Means
Worn out and drained of force **by overuse** — stale from being repeated too often.

It is aimed almost exclusively at **language and ideas**: phrases, plots, metaphors, opening lines, plot twists. You don't call a chair hackneyed. And the charge is *laziness*, not falsity — a hackneyed line is usually one that was good, which is precisely why it got used to death. Where **cliché** names the offending thing, *hackneyed* is the verdict passed on it, and it carries a faint sniff of the literary critic: mildly contemptuous, never neutral.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **hack** writer writes **hackneyed** prose.

The word wears its insult on its sleeve — *hack* is the writer-for-hire churning out the same tired phrases, and *hackneyed* is what comes out.

### In sentences
1. The screenplay's dialogue was so **hackneyed** that reviewers could finish each line before the actors did.
2. Please don't end the toast with "last but not least" — it's so **hackneyed** everyone's already cringing.

### In the wild
London's black cabs are still licensed, in law, as **hackney carriages** — the same word, one step back: a horse for hire.

### Where it comes from
From **Hackney**, the London district where horses were bred and **hired out**. A *hackney* was a rented nag — ridden by everyone, worn down by everyone, and never anyone's prized mount.

That gives you the whole family in one image: **hack** (a writer for hire, grinding out routine copy), **hackney carriage** (a cab for hire), and **hackneyed** (an idea so hired-out it's exhausted).

# **incongruous** *(adj.)* — **in-KONG-groo-uhs**

*Stress the KONG — not "in-kon-GROO-us".*

### Means
Out of place; clashing with what surrounds it. A thing is **incongruous** when it doesn't match its setting, its company, or the rest of itself.

The word describes a **mismatch, not a fault**. *Inappropriate* accuses; *incongruous* just registers the clash — a tuxedo at a beach barbecue, a nursery rhyme sung at a funeral. Very often the effect is faintly comic, and writers use it exactly for that: the detail that refuses to blend in. Note it needs a context to clash *with* — nothing is incongruous on its own.

**incongruous vs. incongruent.** *Incongruous* = doesn't fit the surroundings (the everyday, literary word). *Incongruent* = doesn't correspond or align, used technically — congruent triangles, incongruent data sets. If you're describing a scene, you want **incongruous**.

### Trick to lock it in
> in-**GROUP**-ous — it isn't in the group.

The middle of the word sounds like *group*, and that's precisely the complaint: the thing sits there without belonging to the set around it.

### In sentences
1. A glass tower rose behind the abbey, **incongruous** among four centuries of grey stone.
2. He turned up to the funeral in neon trainers — a bit **incongruous**, but nobody said anything.

### Where it comes from
Latin **congruere** = *"to come together, to agree."* Add *in-* ("not") and you get things that refuse to come together. Same root gives **congruent** (shapes that agree exactly) and **congruity** — so *incongruous* is literally *not-agreeing-with*.

# **interchangeable** *(adj.)* — **in-ter-CHAYN-juh-buhl**

### Means
Able to be **swapped one for the other with no loss** — either one does the job equally well.

The claim is about *function*, not identity: two things can differ in look, brand, or wording and still be interchangeable, so long as substituting one changes nothing that matters. Two words are interchangeable when the sentence means the same either way; two bolts are interchangeable when either holds.

Point it at people or creative work and it turns cold: *interchangeable candidates*, *interchangeable pop songs*. There it means nothing distinguishes them — and therefore nothing is lost when one replaces another.

### Trick to lock it in
> The stock phrase is **interchangeable parts** — so call a *person* interchangeable and you've just called them a part.

The word's home turf is machinery, which is exactly why aiming it at people carries the sting of "mass-produced, replaceable, no one would notice."

### In sentences
1. In careful usage the two terms are not **interchangeable**: every forgery is a fake, but not every fake is a forgery.
2. Honestly, half these streaming thrillers are **interchangeable** — same plot, different actors.

### In the wild
**Interchangeable parts** is the phrase every history class hangs on the birth of mass production — Eli Whitney's muskets, then Ford's assembly line. The whole industrial revolution rests on parts you don't have to hand-fit.

# **laconic** *(adj.)* — **luh-KON-ik**

### Means
Using **very few words** — terse to the point of seeming cool, dry, or unbothered.

Brevity alone isn't the point. *Concise* praises efficiency; *curt* accuses you of rudeness. **Laconic** sits between them and adds temperature: the speaker could have said more and chose not to, and the silence around the words does the rest of the work. It's the one-word answer that lands harder than a paragraph — often admiring (the unflappable hero), occasionally chilly (the boss who replies "Noted.").

⚠️ **laconic ≠ taciturn.**
- **laconic** = says *little when he speaks* — style
- **taciturn** = *rarely speaks at all* — temperament

A taciturn man may go a whole dinner in silence; a laconic man answers every question, in four words.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Laconic = LACKing words — deliberately.**

The *lac-* sound gives you "lack," and the deliberateness is what separates it from someone who simply has nothing to say.

### In sentences
1. His **laconic** dispatches from the front — "Held. Losses heavy." — told the ministry less than it wanted and more than it could bear.
2. I asked how the interview went and got a **laconic** "fine," so I assume it was a disaster.

### In the wild
Film critics reach for this word constantly for the strong-silent type — Clint Eastwood's Man with No Name, Ryan Gosling in *Drive*. "Laconic hero" is practically a stock phrase in reviews.

### Where it comes from
Greek **Lakōnikos** = *"of Laconia,"* the region around **Sparta** — and the Spartans were famous for cutting speech to the bone.

The founding anecdote: Philip II of Macedon warned them, *"If I enter Laconia, I will raze Sparta to the ground."* The Spartans wrote back one word — **"If."** He didn't invade.

# **lucrative** *(adj.)* — **LOO-kruh-tiv**

### Means
Producing a lot of money; highly profitable.

It attaches to the **activity, deal, or venture** — never to the person. A contract, a sideline, a franchise, a career is lucrative; the man who owns it is *wealthy*. And unlike *profitable* (a flat accounting fact — even 2% is profitable), *lucrative* implies the money is **notably good, better than you'd expect**. Tone is admiring and slightly worldly: it says "the money is real" without moralising, which is exactly where its cousin *lucre* differs — that one drips with contempt.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Lucrative** hides **LUCRE** — cold cash. But lucre is dirty and lucrative isn't: the *deal* is lucrative, the *greed* is lucre.

Same root, split tone — keep *lucrative* on the venture and neutral, and leave the sneer to *lucre*.

### In sentences
1. What began as a hobby restoring bicycles turned out to be startlingly **lucrative**, and within two years she had left the firm.
2. Teaching pays the rent, but the weekend wedding gigs are the **lucrative** bit.

### In the wild
Sports and business journalism runs on it — every transfer window brings a "**lucrative** endorsement deal" or a "**lucrative** five-year contract." The word is practically welded to the phrase.

### Where it comes from
Latin **lucrum** = *"profit, gain."* Straight into English as **lucre**, money — best known from the Bible's **"filthy lucre"** (1 Timothy), which is why *lucre* alone sounds greedy and grubby while *lucrative* stayed clean.

# **magisterial** *(adj.)* — **muh-jis-TEER-ee-uhl**

### Means
Two related senses, and the GRE tests both.

**1. Of a work or performance:** showing complete mastery — comprehensive, commanding, the definitive treatment. *A magisterial history of the Ottoman Empire.*
**2. Of a person or manner:** speaking with the unquestionable authority of a master — lofty, imposing, brooking no argument.

This is far above *authoritative*. *Authoritative* means reliable; **magisterial** means so thoroughly in command that disagreement feels presumptuous. Sense 1 is high praise, usually reserved for decades-long scholarly work. Sense 2 tilts cool or faintly barbed: the magisterial speaker isn't arrogant exactly, but he addresses the room as a professor addresses freshmen.

### Trick to lock it in
> **magister** = *master*. A magisterial book has mastered the subject; a magisterial manner acts like it has mastered **you**.

One root, two directions — praise when it points at the work, a raised eyebrow when it points at the person.

### In sentences
1. Gibbon's **magisterial** account of Rome's decline went unchallenged for a century.
2. He waved off the question with a **magisterial** little gesture, as if we'd all wandered in from the wrong meeting.

### In the wild
It lives on book jackets. Flip over any doorstop biography or history and the blurb almost certainly says **"magisterial"** — it's the reviewer's highest word for *big, definitive, and never to be redone*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **magister** = *"master, teacher"*, from **magis** = *"more, greater."*

That root is everywhere: **master**, **maestro**, **magistrate**, **mister**, and — via *magis* — **major**, **majesty**, **magnitude**, **magnify**. All of them mean *bigger, higher, in charge*.

# **onerous** *(adj.)* — **OH-nuh-ruhs**

*Also ON-uh-ruhs; both are standard. Stress the first syllable — never "oh-NER-us."*

### Means
Burdensome — imposing a heavy, troublesome load of work, duty or expense, usually more than is fair.

The weight is **obligation**, not difficulty. *Arduous* describes effort (an arduous climb); *onerous* describes being bound to something you can't put down — paperwork, upkeep, a clause in a contract. A crossword can be hard but never onerous; filing quarterly returns is onerous even though a child could do it. The word also carries a quiet complaint: calling terms onerous is one step from calling them unjust.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Onerous** ≈ *owner-ous* — what you own, owns you: the mortgage, the upkeep, the duty you can't hand back.

That's why *onerous* attaches to terms, duties and contracts rather than to puzzles — it's heavy because you're bound to it, not because it's clever.

### In sentences
1. The bank's revised terms were so **onerous** that the smaller borrowers walked away rather than sign.
2. Watering his neighbour's plants sounded easy until he saw the list — twelve pots, twice a day, an unexpectedly **onerous** favour.

### In the wild
Standard contract-desk vocabulary: negotiators strike out "**onerous** terms," and accounting has a formal category for the **onerous contract** — one where the cost of honouring it now exceeds anything you'll get back. The word survives precisely where obligations are priced.

### Where it comes from
Latin **onus, oneris** = *"burden, load"* — the same *onus* English borrowed whole: *the **onus** is on you.*

Best payoff is the opposite: **ex-** (off) + **onerare** (to load) = **exonerate**, literally *to take the burden off someone* — which is exactly what clearing a person of blame does.

# **opprobrium** *(n.)* — **uh-PROH-bree-uhm**

*Second syllable is a long "PROH", not "prob" as in probable.*

### Means
**Harsh public criticism and scorn** directed at someone — and, in its second sense, **the disgrace that results** from it.

Scale and audience are what set it apart. Criticism can be one editor's note; **opprobrium is a crowd**. It carries a moral charge — the target hasn't merely been wrong, they've done something the public finds *shameful* — and the word is heavy, formal, the register of leaders, editorials and sentencing remarks. Note the double life: you can *heap* opprobrium on a man, and he can *live under* it afterwards.

⚠️ **opprobrium ≠ approbation.** One letter apart, near-opposites: **approbation** = *approval, praise*; **opprobrium** = *condemnation, disgrace*. **A**pprobation **a**pplauds.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Opprobrium is always *heaped*** — never a pinch of it, always a pile, and always in public.

The collocation gives you the two things the definition alone won't: the sheer quantity, and the fact that a crowd is doing the pouring.

### In sentences
1. The verdict drew such public **opprobrium** that the judge's name was known in every household within a week.
2. He deleted the tweet in ten minutes, but by then the **opprobrium** was already unstoppable.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob-/op-** = *"against"* + **probrum** = *"a shameful act, a reproach."* Literally: the shame thrown **at** you.

That **op-** is the prefix of hostility across English — *oppose, opponent, obstruct, object*. Whenever you see it fused onto a root, something is being aimed **against** something else.

# **parsimonious** *(adj.)* — **par-suh-MOH-nee-uhs**

### Means
**Extremely unwilling to spend or give** — stingy, tight-fisted, sparing to the point of meanness.

A *frugal* person spends carefully and you admire them; a **parsimonious** one hoards and you judge them. It's a cold, formal word — the kind used in an obituary or an audit, not a shout across a table. And it stretches past money: you can be parsimonious with praise, with detail, with sympathy.

One flip side worth knowing, because the GRE loves it: applied to a theory or explanation, **parsimonious is praise** — it means economical, using the fewest assumptions necessary.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Parsimonious** ≈ *"parses my money"* — he parses every penny before he parts with it.

The word isn't about spending little, it's about the obsessive counting that precedes it.

### In sentences
1. The estate revealed a man **parsimonious** in life and lavish only in death, having left a fortune he never once enjoyed.
2. My landlord is so **parsimonious** he times the hallway lights.

### In the wild
Occam's razor is formally called **the law of parsimony** — of two explanations, take the one that spends fewer assumptions. That's the same word doing its complimentary job, and it's how scientists and statisticians actually use it ("the more parsimonious model").

### Where it comes from
Latin **parcere** = *"to spare, to refrain from using"* → *parsimonia*, thrift. The whole word is just "sparingness," which is why it fits both the miser and the lean theory.

🚫 Not from **parse** (Latin *pars*, "part") — handy as a sound hook, unrelated by blood.

# **peripheral** *(adj. / n.)* — **puh-RIF-uh-ruhl**

### Means
**Literally:** lying at the outer edge or boundary. **Figuratively:** secondary, marginal — off to one side of the main business. **As a noun:** a device attached around a computer (mouse, printer).

Calling something *peripheral* is a soft demotion. It doesn't claim the thing is wrong or worthless — only that it isn't the point. That is exactly what makes it a debating move: *"a peripheral concern"* concedes your issue exists while quietly sliding it off the table. **Trivial** is openly contemptuous, **irrelevant** flatly denies any connection; *peripheral* is the polite one, and usually the one worth pushing back on.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Peripheral vision** — you can still see it. You're just not *looking* at it.

Peripheral things are present and real, merely outside the centre of attention — which is why the word demotes without quite dismissing.

### In sentences
1. Questions of consent were treated as **peripheral** to the study's design, and the ethics board later said so in writing.
2. Don't argue about the font — that's **peripheral**. Get the numbers right first.

### In the wild
Your mouse, keyboard and headset are sold as **peripherals** — the kit arranged *around* the computer, not one piece of which is the computer.

### Where it comes from
Greek **peri-** = *"around"* + **pherein** = *"to carry."* The **periphery** is the line carried around a shape — its outer boundary.

**peri-** unlocks a run of GRE words: **perimeter** (measure around), **periscope** (look around), **peripatetic** (walking around), **periodontal** (around the tooth).

**pherein** gives **metaphor** (carry across), **euphoria** (bearing up well), **paraphernalia** (the stuff carried alongside).

# **provocative** *(adj.)* — **pruh-VOK-uh-tiv**

*Short o in the middle — pruh-VOK-, even though *provoke* is pruh-VOHK.*

### Means
Deliberately designed to **cause a strong reaction** — argument, anger, or desire.

The word always implies **intent**. A provocative statement isn't accidentally offensive; it was aimed. Which reaction it aims for decides the tone: in a review or a seminar, **provocative** is praise — the book unsettles you productively, it starts the argument. Applied to clothing or a look, it means sexually suggestive. Applied to a remark at a family dinner, it means you were asking for a fight. Same intent, three different targets, so the connotation swings entirely on context.

**provocative ≠ evocative.**
- **provocative** = *calls out* a reaction (you argue back)
- **evocative** = *calls up* a memory or mood (you drift off)

A provocative essay picks a fight; an evocative one smells like your grandmother's kitchen.

### Trick to lock it in
> **pro-VOKE** ≈ **poke** — and nobody pokes you by accident.

The sound hook carries the crucial part: provocative isn't merely offensive, it's *aimed*, a jab meant to make you jump.

### In sentences
1. His **provocative** thesis — that the famine was engineered, not endured — drew furious rebuttals from three continents.
2. She only said it to be **provocative**, and it worked; the whole table stopped eating.

### In the wild
Book jackets and film reviews lean on it constantly — *"a provocative new work"* is critic-speak for *this will start arguments, and that's the point*. Note it's a compliment there.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pro-** (*forth*) + **vocāre** (*to call*) — literally *to call forth* a reaction out of you.

That *vocāre* root is a GRE goldmine: **evoke** (call up), **revoke** (call back), **invoke** (call upon), **convoke** (call together), **advocate** (one who's called to your side), **vocation** (a calling).

# **renounce** *(v.)* — **ri-NOWNSS**

### Means
To **formally and publicly give up** a claim, right, title, belief, or allegiance — and to say so out loud.

The word is a *declaration*, not a drift. You can quietly stop believing something; you **renounce** it only when you announce the break. That's what separates it from *abandon* (you just leave it behind) and *give up* (neutral, everyday). **Renounce** carries weight and witnesses: a monk renounces wealth, a prince renounces the throne, a militant renounces violence. There's usually a cost being paid on purpose.

⚠️ **renounce ≠ denounce.**
- **renounce** = give up something **of your own** (a right, a faith, a claim)
- **denounce** = publicly condemn **someone else**

He renounced his citizenship; the papers denounced him for it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Renounce** = **re-** + **announce**: you *announce* that you're giving it up.

The hidden "announce" is the whole nuance — renouncing is public and spoken, never a quiet fade.

### In sentences
1. Upon taking the oath of citizenship, she **renounced** all allegiance to any foreign sovereign.
2. He didn't just quit the band — he went on air and **renounced** the whole thing.

### In the wild
The baptism scene in **The Godfather** — the priest asking Michael Corleone, *"Do you renounce Satan?"*, intercut with the murders he's ordering. The word is doing all the irony.

### Where it comes from
Latin **re-** ("back") + **nuntiāre** ("to report, announce"), from **nuntius**, *a messenger* — so literally *to announce back*, to send word that you're done.

That root runs deep: **announce**, **denounce**, **pronounce**, **enunciate**, **annunciation**, and the **nuncio** (the Pope's ambassador — his messenger).

# **tempestuous** *(adj.)* — **tem-PES-choo-uhs**

### Means
Stormy — literally *of the weather*, but in practice almost always **figurative**: violently turbulent, driven by high emotion.

The word carries heat, not just trouble. A **turbulent** decade is unstable; a **tempestuous** marriage has shouting, slammed doors and passionate reconciliations. It implies people with big feelings, and it borrows the glamour of a storm — which is why journalists reach for it about affairs, divas and volatile geniuses. Applied to a person or relationship it's disapproving, but never *dull*.

⚠️ **tempestuous ≠ temperamental.**
- **tempestuous** = *stormy, violently passionate* (the storm has broken)
- **temperamental** = *moody, unpredictable* (it might, at any moment)

A temperamental soprano sulks; a tempestuous one throws the score at the conductor. Unrelated roots, too.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's a **tempest** sitting inside it — and the storm is never the weather, it's the marriage.

Nine times out of ten the thing being called tempestuous is a relationship, a temper or a career, so hear "tempest" and then move it indoors.

### In sentences
1. Their correspondence records a **tempestuous** decade: three separations, two reconciliations, and one duel.
2. They had a **tempestuous** thing going for about six months — screaming matches, then flowers, then screaming matches.

### In the wild
Shakespeare's **The Tempest** — the storm in Act I is conjured by Prospero out of pure rage and grievance, which is exactly the word's figurative sense: weather made of feeling.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tempus** = *"time"* → **tempestas** = *"season, weather"* → then, because bad weather is the weather people talk about, *"storm."*

That single root pays out widely: **tempo, temporal, temporary, contemporary, extempore** (out of the moment, unrehearsed).

🚫 **Not from *temper* / *temperature*.** Those are Latin *temperāre*, "to mix in proportion" — hence *temperate, temperament, distemper*. Similar sound, different family.

# **tenable** *(adj.)* — **TEN-uh-buhl**

### Means
Able to be **defended, held, or maintained** — of a position, theory, claim, or job.

It began as a military word (a fort you can hold against attack) and now lives mostly in argument: a tenable theory is one that survives cross-examination. Note how modest it is — tenable doesn't mean *true* or *convincing*, only that it hasn't yet been demolished. "Her reading of the poem is tenable" is a long way from praise; it means you can't quite disprove it. Applied to a job or a situation, it shifts to *survivable*: a position that can still be occupied without collapse.

⚠️ **tenable ≠ tenuous.**
- **tenable** = holds up
- **tenuous** = thin, flimsy, barely holding on

They look like cousins and are near-opposites: a tenuous argument is exactly the one that isn't tenable.

### Trick to lock it in
> You'll almost always meet it wearing the **un-**: *his position became untenable, so he resigned.*

Define it backwards from the far commoner *untenable* — whatever can't be held is untenable, so tenable is the fort still standing.

### In sentences
1. No **tenable** account of the manuscript's provenance has yet been offered, though three have been attempted.
2. Blaming the intern for a decision he made himself wasn't a **tenable** line and he knew it.

### In the wild
Political journalism runs on the negative: when a minister's "position becomes **untenable**", that's the standard press signal that a resignation lands within the week.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tenēre** = *"to hold"* — one of the great GRE root-mines: **tenacious** (holding on), **tenant** (one who holds property), **tenure** (the holding of a post), **tenet** (a belief you hold), plus **retain, contain, sustain, detain, abstain**.

# **transgression** *(n.)* — **tranz-GRESH-uhn**

### Means
A **breach of a law, rule, or moral code** — an act of crossing a line you were meant to stay behind.

Weight and register are what set it apart. A *violation* is administrative, a *crime* is legal, an *offence* is procedural — a **transgression** is moral. It belongs to conscience, scripture and sermon, and it implies there was a boundary with authority behind it, not just a regulation. That gravity is also why it flips so easily into dry humour: "forgive my transgressions" over the last slice of cake works precisely because the word is far too grand for the crime.

### Trick to lock it in
> You **confess** a transgression; you **report** a violation.

The verbs it keeps company with tell you the line crossed was a moral one, not a clause in a rulebook.

### In sentences
1. The committee judged his falsification of the data a **transgression** grave enough to end a thirty-year career.
2. She snuck out at 2am, which in that house counted as a serious **transgression**.

### In the wild
Isaiah 53:5 — *"But he was wounded for our **transgressions**"* — a line most people have heard sung, since Handel set it in *Messiah*. That biblical home is exactly why the word still smells of sin rather than paperwork.

### Where it comes from
Latin **trans** (*across*) + **gradī, gressus** (*to step, to walk*) — literally **to step across**. The line is right there in the word.

**-gress = step** unlocks a whole GRE shelf: **progress** (step forward), **regress** (step back), **digress** (step aside, off topic), **egress** (step out, an exit), **congress** (step together), **aggression** (step *toward* — i.e. at you). Same root gives **gradual**, **grade**, **degree** — steps by another name.

# **urbane** *(adj.)* — **er-BAYN**

*Stress on the second syllable — not UR-ban with an -e tacked on.*

### Means
Smooth, polished and socially at ease — the poise of someone who has been everywhere, met everyone, and can keep a difficult dinner table talking.

The word is about **surface, and that's a compliment** — an urbane person is unruffled, lightly witty, never earnest or awkward. It's more than polite: politeness is following rules, urbaneness is making the rules look unnecessary. It skews male, older, and worldly, and it carries a faint suggestion that the charm may be all there is — call a man urbane and you praise his manner while saying nothing at all about his depth.

⚠️ **urban ≠ urbane.**
- **urban** = *of the city* (urban planning, urban decay)
- **urbane** = *of the city's manners* (an urbane host)

Same root, one letter apart, completely different exam answer.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Urban** is where you're from; **urbane** is what the city did to your manners.

The extra **-e** adds the polish and pulls the stress to the fancy end — er-BAYN — turning a place into a personality.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador was **urbane** to the last, deflecting a hostile question with a joke about his own tailoring.
2. He's the most **urbane** guy in the office — somehow he can tell you your work is rubbish and you leave feeling flattered.

### In the wild
**Urbane** is the standing epithet film critics reach for with Cary Grant — dinner jacket, raised eyebrow, never once flustered. If you can picture Grant, you have the definition.

### Where it comes from
Latin **urbs** = *"city"* → **urbānus**, "of the city," hence *refined*. Rome's insult pair was **urbānus** vs **rūsticus** — city-smooth against country-clumsy — which is why *rustic* still means rough-edged today.

The same **urbs** gives you **urban**, **suburb** (*sub-* = under/near the city) and **urbanise**.

# **verisimilitude** *(n.)* — **ver-uh-si-MIL-i-tood**

*Five syllables; British speakers often say -tyood at the end.*

### Means
The quality of **seeming true** — of being lifelike, plausible, convincingly real.

It is a word about *appearance*, not fact. A novel, a film set, an alibi, a forged passport can all have verisimilitude; none of them is thereby true. Critics reach for it as praise for craft — the dialogue sounds like people actually talk, the 1840s kitchen has the right grease on it — and it stays cool and technical rather than admiring. Notice too that it's almost always used of something *made*: you praise the verisimilitude of a performance, not of a mountain.

⚠️ **verisimilitude ≠ veracity.**
- **verisimilitude** = *looks* true
- **veracity** = *is* true, habitually truthful

A con man's story can have great verisimilitude and zero veracity.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Veri-similitude** — read it as *"very similar"*: similar to the truth, which is exactly not the same as being it.

The word's own halves confess the limit — resemblance is all it ever claims.

### In sentences
1. The jury was swayed less by evidence than by the **verisimilitude** of the witness's account — the small, useless details no liar would bother to invent.
2. The costumes are gorgeous, but there's no **verisimilitude** — nobody in 1912 had teeth like that.

### In the wild
This is a film-and-TV critic's workhorse word — the standard praise for something like *The Wire* or a meticulous period drama. It always means "it feels real," never "it happened."

### Where it comes from
Latin **vērus** (*true*) + **similis** (*like*) — "truth-like."

Both halves pay off across the exam. **vērus** gives *verify, verdict* (vere dictum, "truly said"), *veritable, aver* — and **very** itself, which once meant "true" (*the very man*). **similis** gives *similar, simulate, assimilate, resemble, simultaneous*.

# **vitiate** *(v.)* — **VISH-ee-ayt**

*The -ti- is "sh", as in *initiate* — never "vitty-ate".*

### Means
To **spoil, impair, or corrupt** something — and, in legal use, to **render it invalid or void**.

The damage comes from *within* and from *one flaw*: a single bad ingredient, a single false premise, a single act of fraud, and the whole thing is no longer sound. You don't vitiate a wall by knocking it down; you vitiate an argument by smuggling one bad assumption into it. Compare **ruin** (total, physical) and **taint** (contaminated but still standing): *vitiate* sits between them — the thing survives in form but no longer works, or no longer counts. Formal and slightly bookish; it lives in law, philosophy, and criticism.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Vit**iate has a **vice** buried in it — one vice, and the whole thing is void.

*Vice* and *vitiate* are the same Latin root, and the sense is the same: one hidden fault is enough to cancel the whole.

### In sentences
1. The study's conclusions were **vitiated** by a sampling error nobody caught until publication.
2. One dodgy signature and the whole deal's **vitiated** — we're back to square one.

### In the wild
The old legal maxim **"fraud vitiates everything"** (*fraus omnia vitiat*) — still quoted in contract cases: prove fraud anywhere in the agreement and the entire agreement collapses.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vitium** = *"fault, defect, blemish."* Same root as **vice** (a moral defect), **vicious**, and **vituperate** (to abuse someone verbally — literally "to bring up their faults").
