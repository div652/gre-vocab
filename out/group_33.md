# **affluent** *(adj.)* — **AF-loo-uhnt**

*Stress the first syllable — not uh-FLOO-ent.*

### Means
Wealthy, well-off, prosperous.

But *affluent* isn't *rich* and it certainly isn't *loaded*. It describes **settled, comfortable, ongoing** money — the kind you were probably born into or have had for years. Nobody becomes affluent overnight. It's also the sociologist's and marketer's word: it attaches naturally to **groups and places** — an affluent suburb, affluent households, affluent shoppers — far more readily than to one person. Neutral on the surface, but in journalism it often carries a faint sniff: *affluent* is how you describe privilege you're not entirely sympathetic to.

⚠️ **affluent ≠ effluent.** Same root, opposite plumbing: **affluent** = wealth flowing *in*; **effluent** *(EF-loo-uhnt)* = liquid waste flowing *out* of a factory or sewer. One is a nice postcode, the other is what the water company gets fined for.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Affluent** = money that **flows** — like *fluent* speech, it's steady and effortless, never a sudden jackpot.

The *-flu-* in the middle is the same *flow* as in *fluent*, which is exactly the nuance: affluence is a continuous stream, not a windfall.

### In sentences
1. The council's report noted that the new library had been sited, predictably, in the most **affluent** ward in the borough.
2. They're not just doing okay — that's a seriously **affluent** part of town, have you seen the driveways?

### In the wild
**"Affluenza"** — the coinage that went global during the 2013 Texas case where a teenager's defence argued his wealthy upbringing left him unable to grasp consequences. Affluence treated as a disease.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*toward*) + **fluere** (*to flow*) — wealth flowing toward you.

*Fluere* is a workhorse root: **fluent** (words flow), **influence** (originally a flowing-in from the stars), **confluence** (rivers meeting), **flux**, **fluid**, **superfluous** (over-flowing, hence excess), and **effluent** (flowing out).

# **allegorical** *(adj.)* — **al-uh-GOR-i-kuhl**

*The noun shifts the stress to the front: allegory = AL-uh-gor-ee.*

### Means
Describing a story, painting or film whose surface content **systematically stands for something else** — a moral, a political argument, a religious scheme.

The word implies *machinery*, not decoration. A symbol can be a single glinting detail; an allegory is a full parallel structure, where each character and event has an assigned counterpart in the real subject. Because of that, "allegorical" often carries a faint chill in criticism — it hints that the story is a delivery vehicle for a message, and that once you've decoded it there's nothing left to discover.

### Trick to lock it in
> **If you can swap every character for an idea and the story still runs, it's allegorical.**

Symbolism only decorates a story, but allegory *is* the story, so the substitution has to hold all the way through.

### In sentences
1. Bunyan's *The Pilgrim's Progress* is relentlessly **allegorical**: the hero is Christian, the swamp is Despond, and the road is salvation itself.
2. Everyone said the film was just a heist movie, but it's obviously **allegorical** — the bank is the government and the crew is us.

### In the wild
Orwell's **Animal Farm** is the standard example: every pig, horse and dog maps onto a figure in the Russian Revolution, which is why it gets taught as allegory rather than as a story about farming.

### Where it comes from
Greek **allos** = *"other"* + **agoreuein** = *"to speak in the agora,"* the public marketplace. So: **to speak in public but mean something other than what you say.**

Both halves pay off elsewhere: *allos* gives **allergy** (the body reacting *otherly*), **allonym**, **allele**; the *agora* gives **agoraphobia** (fear of the open square) and even **category** (*kata-* + *agoreuein*, originally "to accuse publicly").

# **allude** *(v.)* — **uh-LOOD**

### Means
To refer to something **indirectly** — to hint at it, gesture toward it, mention it without naming it.

The indirectness is the whole point. You *mention* a thing; you *allude* to it. Alluding means the listener has to do a little work: you name the affair as "recent difficulties," you name the rival's book as "a certain bestseller." So it's the verb of tact, of insinuation, and of literature — a poet alludes to Homer, a diplomat alludes to a scandal, a colleague alludes to your unread emails. Note it takes **to**: you allude *to* something, never allude something.

⚠️ **allude ≠ elude.**
- **allude to** = hint at it
- **elude** = escape or slip away from it (the thief eluded capture; the word eludes me)

Same Latin root, opposite directions: *toward* vs *away*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Never said *aloud* — only *alluded* to.**

The near-homophone flips the meaning: what's alluded to is precisely what nobody says out loud.

### In sentences
1. In his resignation letter he **alluded** to "differences of judgement" with the board, and named no one.
2. She kept **alluding** to some big announcement and then refused to tell us what it was.

### In the wild
Every English class you've sat in: a **literary allusion** is a line that nods at another text without quoting it — Eliot glancing at Dante, a film title lifted from Shakespeare.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** ("toward") + **ludere** ("to play") — literally *to play toward*, to touch on a thing in passing.

That root pays off across a whole family: **elude** (play *away* — escape), **delude** (play someone *false*), **collude** (play *together*, secretly), **prelude** / **interlude** (play before / between), **ludicrous** (fit only for play).

# **amplify** *(v.)* — **AM-pluh-fye**

### Means
To **make something bigger, louder, or stronger** — sound, a signal, an effect, a message.

The key is that amplify never *creates*; it takes something already there and turns the dial up. That's what separates it from **exaggerate** (which distorts) and **embellish** (which adds invented detail) — *amplify* stays neutral about whether the thing is true. Second sense, common on the GRE: to **expand on** a statement — "could you amplify that point?" means *say more*, not *say it louder*. Modern professional usage ("amplify underrepresented voices") is warmly positive, but the word itself carries no judgement — panic, tension, and rumours get amplified too.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Amp**lify hides an **amp** and **ample** — turn up what's already playing, until there's plenty of it.

The guitar amp boosts a string that's already vibrating, which is exactly why you amplify a rumour but fabricate a lie.

### In sentences
1. Social media did not create the outrage so much as **amplify** it, feeding a local grievance to a global audience within hours.
2. "You said the schedule was 'unrealistic' — care to **amplify** that a bit before we sign the contract?"

### In the wild
Every electric guitar runs through an **amp** — short for *amplifier*. The pickup produces a signal far too faint to hear; the amp does nothing but make that existing signal enormous. That's the whole word in one box.

### Where it comes from
Latin **amplus** = *"large, spacious"* + **-ficāre** (*facere*, "to make"). Literally *make it large*.

Same root gives you **ample** (plenty), **amplitude** (the size of a wave's swing), and **amplification**. And **-fy / -ficāre** is a free GRE bonus: *clarify, ratify, mollify, vilify* — all "make it X."

# **catastrophic** *(adj.)* — **kat-uh-STROF-ik**

### Means
Disastrous in the fullest sense — sudden, total, and beyond repair.

*Disastrous* stretches to cover a ruined dinner party; **catastrophic** does not. It implies the thing didn't merely go badly, it **stopped existing in working form**: a bank, a bridge, a harvest, an ecosystem. Engineers use it precisely — *catastrophic failure* means a part gave way all at once rather than wearing down gracefully — and that technical sense colours the ordinary one. Casual speech does inflate it ("my hair looks catastrophic"), but the joke only works *because* the real word is so absolute.

### Trick to lock it in
> There is no such thing as **slightly** catastrophic.

The word sits pinned at the top of the damage scale, so unlike *bad*, *costly* or *damaging* it refuses to be dialled down — if it can be softened, it isn't catastrophic.

### In sentences
1. The 2008 collapse was **catastrophic** not because losses were large but because the mechanism of lending itself ceased to function.
2. One wrong keystroke and the whole database was gone — genuinely **catastrophic**, and I'd skipped the backup.

### In the wild
Therapists talk about **catastrophizing** — spiralling from "he hasn't texted back" to "my life is over." The habit is named after the word precisely because the leap is always to *total* ruin, never to mild inconvenience.

### Where it comes from
Greek **kata-** (*down*) + **strephein** (*to turn*) = **katastrophē**, "the overturning." In Greek drama it was a neutral technical term: the sudden final turn of the plot — the scene after which nothing can be undone.

That **strephein** shows up again in **apostrophe** (a turning away — to address someone absent) and **strophe** (the turn a chorus made across the stage). The **cata-** "down" half runs through **catapult**, **cataract**, **catalyst**.

- **Intensity scale** · [Scale of disaster](groups.md#intensity__scale-of-disaster) — with *calamitous, cataclysmic*

# **contemporary** *(adj. / n.)* — **kuhn-TEM-puh-rer-ee**

### Means
**Adj.:** (1) belonging to the **same time** as something else; (2) belonging to **the present** — modern, current.
**Noun:** a person living at the same time as another — *Leibniz was a contemporary of Newton*.

Sense (1) is the one the GRE tests, and it's relational: contemporary *with* or *of* something. So "**contemporary accounts** of the battle" means written by people who were there — not written by modern historians. When the word stands alone with no comparison point ("contemporary art", "contemporary dance"), the era it matches is *ours*. Same word, two clocks: it always means "of that time," and context decides which time.

⚠️ **contemporary ≠ contemporaneous.** *Contemporaneous* only ever means "happening at the same time," usually of events or documents (*contemporaneous notes*) — it can never mean "modern." Anything described as contemporaneous is being dated, not praised as current.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Contemporary** hides **tempo** — two things keeping the *same tempo* are running on the same clock.

The buried *tempo* pulls you to the "same era as" sense rather than the lazy "modern" reading.

### In sentences
1. Scholars prize the diary because it is **contemporary** with the siege itself, not a reconstruction written forty years later.
2. My gran and Elvis were **contemporaries**, which she brings up roughly once a week.

### In the wild
A **Museum of Contemporary Art** shows work by artists who are alive now — the label is a date range, not a style.

### Where it comes from
Latin **con-** (*together*) + **tempus, temporis** (*time*) — literally "sharing a time."

That root pays off everywhere: **temporary** (lasting a short time), **temporal** (of time, or of the worldly world as opposed to the eternal), **tempo**, **extempore** (out of the moment, unprepared), **temporize** (stall for time).

### Also sits in
- **Root family** · [Root: tempus, temporis (time)](groups.md#root__tempus) — with *temporal, extemporize, tempestuous*

# **conundrum** *(n.)* — **kuh-NUN-drum**

### Means
A **problem that baffles you** — a hard question with no clean answer. Older, narrower sense: a **riddle whose answer is a pun**.

Tone is the giveaway: a conundrum is *puzzling* rather than agonising. A **dilemma** forces you to pick between two bad options; a conundrum leaves you stuck one step earlier, unable to work out what's even going on. It carries a faint intellectual relish — the speaker is half-enjoying the difficulty — so it fits "the pricing conundrum" but sits badly on genuine tragedy.

### Trick to lock it in
> A conundrum **cons** you, then leaves your head **drumming**.

Both halves are hiding in plain sight: it tricks you rather than wounds you, which is why the word stays playful even when the problem is serious.

### In sentences
1. The committee faced the familiar **conundrum** of a policy that was popular, affordable, and unworkable — but never all three at once.
2. So I've got a **conundrum**: two job offers, same salary, and I can't stand either boss.

### In the wild
Headline writers live on it — "the productivity **conundrum**", "the China **conundrum**". Spot the pattern and you've got the register: a problem serious enough to write about, puzzling enough that nobody has the answer.

### Also sits in
- **Meaning cluster** · [A Situation With No Clean Exit](groups.md#meaning__a-situation-with-no-clean-exit) — with *quandary, predicament, imbroglio, contretemps*

# **defame** *(v.)* — **dih-FAYM**

### Means
To damage someone's reputation by saying or publishing **false** things about them.

The falsity is built in — you cannot defame someone with the truth, however brutal. That's what separates it from *insult* or *criticise*: an insult wounds the person, defamation rewrites how everyone else sees them. It's also public by nature (a private sneer defames nobody) and carries a courtroom hum — *defamation* is an actual tort, split into **libel** (written) and **slander** (spoken).

**defame ≠ defile.** *Defame* blackens a reputation with lies; *defile* dirties something sacred or pure — a temple, a river, a memory.

### Trick to lock it in
> **De-fame** — you are *removing the fame*, i.e. stripping off the good name, and you're doing it with a lie.

The word wears its meaning on the outside: *fame* here means "reputation," and the *de-* pulls it down.

### In sentences
1. The pamphlet **defamed** the magistrate as a taker of bribes, a charge the court found to be wholly invented.
2. Say that about me in public and I'll sue you for **defaming** me.

### In the wild
The Depp v. Heard trial (2022) — streamed and memed for six weeks — was a **defamation** suit: the argument was never "she was mean," it was "she published something false that destroyed his name."

### Where it comes from
Latin **fāma** = *"report, rumour, what people say about you"* — so *fame* originally just meant reputation, good or bad.

That root pays off across the list: **famous**, **infamous** (famous *for* something bad), **infamy**, **defamation**.

# **detract** *(v.)* — **dih-TRAKT**

### Means
To **take away from** the value, quality, or good impression of something — usually a small subtraction, not a demolition.

To *ruin* is total; to **detract** is a shaving-off. A cracked frame detracts from a painting — the painting is still good, just worth a little less. Note the grammar, because the GRE tests it: you detract **from** something, never detract something. The second, rarer sense is human — to **disparage**, to talk a reputation down, which is why a critic is a **detractor**.

**detract ≠ distract.** *Distract* pulls your **attention** away; *detract* pulls the **value** down. A ringing phone distracts you from the film; bad acting detracts from it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Nothing detracts a thing — things detract *from* it, one notch down, never to zero.**

Lock in the preposition and you've locked in the size of the damage: partial, not fatal.

### In sentences
1. The clumsy final chapter does not seriously **detract** from a novel of otherwise remarkable control.
2. Sure, the seats are ugly, but that hardly **detracts** from a car this cheap.

### In the wild
**Detractors** is the word sports and political coverage reaches for constantly — "his detractors say he peaked three seasons ago." Same root, and it tells you exactly what a detractor does: chips away at your standing.

### Where it comes from
Latin **de-** (*down, away*) + **trahere** (*to pull, drag*) — to drag value downward.

**trahere** is one of the highest-yield GRE roots: **tractor** (pulls), **attract** (pulls toward), **extract** (pulls out), **retract** (pulls back), **subtract** (pulls out from under), **protract** (drags out lengthwise), **contract** (pulls together), **abstract** (pulled away from the concrete).

- **Root family** · [Root: trahere, tractus — to drag, pull (abs-, de-, -able)](groups.md#root__trahere) — with *abstract, tractable*

# **detrimental** *(adj.)* — **det-ruh-MEN-tuhl**

### Means
Causing harm, loss, or damage — especially harm that shows up as a measurable effect on something.

It's the **cool, official** word for bad. *Devastating* shouts, *pernicious* implies something creeping and sinister, but **detrimental** is what a doctor, an auditor, or an HR report says: harm stated as a finding, with no anger in it. It also barely stands alone — you're almost always **detrimental *to*** something (health, morale, the environment, your case). And the harm is usually cumulative rather than a single blow: late nights are detrimental to your health; a bullet is not.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Detri-mental** hides **TRIM** — it trims a bit off you at a time, and the tone stays clinical, never furious.

The hidden "trim" gives you both halves of the word: steady wearing-away, described in a calm official voice.

### In sentences
1. The committee concluded that the proposed tariffs would be **detrimental** to long-term growth in the region.
2. Look, three hours of doomscrolling before bed is **detrimental** to literally everything about you.

### In the wild
This is the house style of public-health and workplace copy — "**detrimental** to your health," "**detrimental** to team morale." When an institution wants to say *this is hurting you* without sounding alarmed or accusing anyone, this is the word it reaches for.

### Where it comes from
Latin **terere** = *"to rub, wear away"*; **de-** + *terere* → **dēterere**, "to rub off," giving **dētrīmentum**, *damage caused by wearing down*.

That root pays out across the GRE: **attrition** (wearing away by friction), **trite** (an idea rubbed smooth by overuse), **detritus** (the debris left after rubbing away), **contrite** (crushed, worn down by guilt).

- **Intensity scale** · [Degrees of harm: from working against you to poisoning you](groups.md#intensity__degrees-of-harm) — with *adverse, deleterious, pernicious, noxious*

# **devious** *(adj.)* — **DEE-vee-uhs**

*Not DEV-ee-uhs — the first syllable is long, as in deviate.*

### Means
Getting what you want by **sneaky, roundabout, underhanded means**; scheming. (Literal, older sense: *winding, indirect* — "a devious mountain track.")

The word's eye is on the **route**, not the lie. *Dishonest* judges your morals; *devious* describes your method — the loops, the cover stories, the third party who "happened" to mention it. Because the method takes brains, *devious* carries a flicker of grudging respect that *deceitful* never has, and it's the one you can aim at a sibling half-fondly. Aimed at a politician or a contract, it's not fond at all.

⚠️ **devious ≠ dubious.** *Devious* = **sneaky**, a quality of the person or plan. *Dubious* = **doubtful**, either uncertain yourself or not to be trusted. A dubious scheme might just be badly thought out; a devious one was thought out very well.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A devious *plan* can earn a grudging smile — a dishonest *person* never does.**

*Devious* points at the ingenuity of the crooked route, which is why it's the one accusation that can double as a compliment.

### In sentences
1. The report exposed a **devious** arrangement of shell companies, each one routing the money a further step from its owner.
2. You told Mum I was at the library so you could take the car? That's **devious**.

### In the wild
The 2021 TikTok craze of filming yourself stealing school property was called the **"devious lick"** — sneaky, planned, and pleased with itself, which is the word exactly.

### Where it comes from
Latin **de-** (*off*) + **via** (*road*) = **"off the main road."** The devious person won't travel in a straight line.

That *via* is a GRE workhorse: **deviate** (turn off the road), **obvious** (*ob + via* — lying right in your way, so you can't miss it), **trivial** (*tri + via* — where three roads meet, the street corner where the cheap gossip is), plus **viaduct**, **convey**, **envoy**.

# **dormant** *(adj.)* — **DOR-muhnt**

### Means
Temporarily inactive — asleep rather than finished. A **dormant** volcano, seed, disease, account, or rivalry is doing nothing *right now*, but the capacity to act is intact and could return.

The whole force of the word is the **implied comeback**. *Extinct* and *defunct* close the door; *dormant* leaves it ajar. That's why it so often appears in warnings and reassurances alike — "a dormant infection" is not a cured one, and "dormant talent" is a compliment about what hasn't happened yet. It's a calm, slightly clinical word: no drama, just paused.

### Trick to lock it in
> **dorm**ant — a **dorm** at 3pm: nobody's moving, everybody's alive.

The students in there aren't gone, they're sleeping, and they'll be up and loud by evening — which is exactly the promise *dormant* makes about a volcano, a seed, or a feud.

### In sentences
1. The bacterium can lie **dormant** in the lungs for decades before any symptom appears.
2. My gym membership's been **dormant** since January, but I keep paying for it.

### In the wild
Volcanoes are officially sorted into **active / dormant / extinct** — and the middle category exists precisely because "quiet for 300 years" is not the same as "over." Vesuvius is classed dormant, not extinct.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dormīre** = *"to sleep."* The family is unusually easy: **dormitory** (a sleeping room), **dormer** window (it sits in the roof over the bedrooms), and the **dormouse** — named for how much it hibernates, which is why Lewis Carroll's is permanently nodding off at the tea party.

# **draconian** *(adj.)* — **druh-KAY-nee-uhn**

*Also heard as dray-KAY-nee-uhn; often capitalised, Draconian.*

### Means
Excessively harsh and severe — used of laws, rules, penalties, and policies.

The word is an accusation of **disproportion**. Calling a punishment draconian doesn't say the offence was innocent; it says the response was wildly out of scale with it — a life sentence for a small theft, a school expelling a child over a dress code. It attacks rules and the people who write them, not personalities: a *cruel* teacher, but a *draconian* attendance policy. Journalistic and political in flavour, and never a compliment — nobody describes their own regime as draconian.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Draconian = dragon-ian** — the rulebook breathes fire on a parking ticket.

The absurd mismatch is the point: the word condemns the size of the punishment, not the crime.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal struck down the statute as **draconian**, noting that a first-time offender faced the same term as a repeat arsonist.
2. Two weeks' pay docked for being five minutes late? That's a bit **draconian**.

### In the wild
A newsroom staple: every COVID lockdown, immigration bill, and social-media ban gets called **"draconian measures"** by whoever opposes it — the word is essentially a headline's way of saying *too far*.

### Where it comes from
A real man. **Draco** wrote Athens' first written law code around 621 BC, and it prescribed **death for almost everything** — including stealing a cabbage. Plutarch reports the crack that Draco's laws were written **not in ink but in blood**.

So the word carries his reputation, not a general idea of strictness: one legislator so over-the-top that his name became the adjective for punishment out of all proportion.

# **egalitarian** *(adj. / n.)* — **ih-gal-uh-TAIR-ee-uhn**

### Means
Holding — or built on — the principle that **all people are equal** and deserve equal rights, status, and rewards. As a noun, a person who believes that.

The word points at **structures, not sentiments**: pay bands, voting rights, who speaks first in a meeting, who inherits. A generous boss isn't egalitarian; a boss who abolishes the private office and flattens the pay scale is. Note also that *democratic* describes how decisions get **made**, while *egalitarian* describes how status and resources get **distributed** — a democracy can be deeply unequal.

Tone is usually approving, and slightly lofty — it belongs to political writing, sociology and manifestos rather than casual speech. Critics use it drily, for levelling they think has gone too far.

⚠️ **egalitarian ≠ humanitarian.** *Egalitarian* is about **equal standing** — no one above anyone else. *Humanitarian* is about **relieving suffering** — feeding, healing, rescuing. An aid convoy is humanitarian; abolishing the caste system is egalitarian.

### Trick to lock it in
> **e-GAL-itarian — every gal, every guy, the same share.**

The stressed middle syllable hides the point: equal shares by arrangement, not kindness handed down.

### In sentences
1. The founders imagined an **egalitarian** republic, though they wrote its charter while owning the people who served them.
2. It's a pretty **egalitarian** office — the CEO queues for coffee like everyone else.

### In the wild
The French Revolution's motto — *Liberté, **Égalité**, Fraternité* — is this word's root standing in the middle of the most famous slogan in political history, still stamped on French coins.

### Where it comes from
French **égal** ← Latin **aequus** / **aequalis** = *"level, even."*

That root pays off across the GRE: **equity** (fairness), **equanimity** (a *level* mind under pressure), **equivocate** (giving two claims *equal* weight, so you say nothing), **adequate** (levelling up to the requirement), and **iniquity** — *in-* + *aequus*, literally "un-level," hence gross injustice or wickedness.

- **Opposites** · [Impartial vs. slanted](groups.md#antonym__impartial-vs-slanted) — with *disinterested, equitable, partial, tendentious, polemical, invidious, chauvinistic*
- **Opposites** · [Levelling vs. Ranking: Equal, Fair, and Above](groups.md#antonym__everyone-equal-vs-a-chosen-few-above) — with *equitable, elitist, echelon, chauvinistic, patronize*

# **elusive** *(adj.)* — **ih-LOO-siv**

### Means
Hard to catch, find, or pin down — **it escapes you as you reach for it.**

The word carries motion. Something *rare* is simply scarce; something **elusive** is actively slipping away — a fugitive who's always one town ahead, a memory that dissolves the moment you reach for it, a word on the tip of your tongue. It works on the physical (an elusive suspect), the abstract (an elusive concept, an elusive scent), and the desired (that elusive promotion).

Tone shifts with the target: applied to a thing you want, it's faintly **frustrating**; applied to a person — a boxer, a winger, a witness — it edges into **admiring**, because escaping is a skill.

⚠️ **elusive ≠ illusive.**
- **elusive** = it *escapes* you (from *elude*)
- **illusive** = it *deceives* you; it isn't really there (from *illusion*)

An elusive fortune is real but always just out of reach. An illusive one was never there.

### Trick to lock it in
> **e-LOOSE-ive** — it stays **loose**, never in your grip.

The stressed syllable literally sounds like *loose*, which is the whole nuance: not absent, just never held.

### In sentences
1. A unified theory of the four forces has remained **elusive** for a century, retreating each time physicists think they have closed on it.
2. I've been chasing him for three weeks — the man is **elusive**, he never picks up.

### In the wild
Wildlife documentaries have worn this word smooth: *"the **elusive** snow leopard"* is practically a fixed phrase — the animal you film for six months and glimpse for four seconds.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ēlūdere** = *"to escape by play"* — *ex-* (out) + *lūdere* (to play). The sense of dodging is baked in: you get away by playing with your pursuer.

That *lūdere* root is a GRE goldmine: **allude** (play toward → hint), **delude** and **illusion** (play someone false), **collude** (play together, secretly), **prelude** and **interlude** (play before / between), **ludicrous** (fit only for play).

- **Meaning cluster** · [Slips out of your grasp](groups.md#meaning__slips-out-of-your-grasp) — with *evasive, subtle, inscrutable*
- **Lookalike** · [allusive / elusive / evasive / illusory](groups.md#lookalike__allusive-elusive) — with *allusive, evasive, illusory*
- **Root family** · [Root: ludere (to play) — indirection, evasion, and things that aren't there](groups.md#root__ludere) — with *allude, allusive, collude, delusion, illusory*

# **erstwhile** *(adj. (also adv., archaic))* — **ERST-wyle**

### Means
**Former, one-time, previous** — the thing or person someone *used to* be. Archaically also an adverb meaning *formerly*.

*Former* is flat reporting; **erstwhile** comes with a faint backward glance, and often a raised eyebrow. It's the word for relationships that ended and changed status: an **erstwhile ally**, an **erstwhile rival**, an **erstwhile heartthrob**. The implication is *that was then*, sometimes with quiet amusement at how far things have moved.

Grammar point worth banking: it sits **in front of the noun**. "Her erstwhile business partner" — never "her partner is erstwhile."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Erst = *first* with the f knocked off.** Your erstwhile partner came first — and is now firmly in the past.

*Erst* really is the old superlative "earliest," so an erstwhile anything belongs to the first chapter, not this one.

### In sentences
1. The treaty was signed by two erstwhile enemies who had spent a decade shelling each other's capitals.
2. He's now working for his erstwhile boss's biggest competitor, which must make Christmas awkward.

### In the wild
Newspaper and obituary English runs on it — "his **erstwhile** ally," "the band's **erstwhile** frontman." If you read business or football coverage, you've absorbed this word without ever looking it up.

### Where it comes from
Old English **ǣrest** = *"earliest,"* the superlative of **ǣr** = *"before"* — which also gives you **ere** (*"ere long"*) and **early**. German kept it plain: *erst* still means "first."

So *erstwhile* is literally **"at the earliest while"** — back in the day.

# **gloat** *(v.)* — **GLOHT**

### Means
To dwell on your own triumph — or someone else's misfortune — with obvious, self-satisfied pleasure, usually **in front of them**.

Gloating is pride with a victim. You can be *proud* alone in a room; you can only really gloat at someone. Note also the asymmetry of who uses the word: it is an accusation levelled at other people, never a neutral description of yourself. And it needn't involve winning at all — watching a rival fail is enough (**gloat over** is the standard collocation: *gloat over her downfall*).

### Trick to lock it in
> **Glo**at = **glow**: satisfaction you can see on the face — and they can see it too.

Private pleasure isn't gloating; it only counts once it's on display for the loser.

### In sentences
1. Rather than **gloat** over his opponent's collapse, the champion praised him at length in the press conference.
2. He didn't just win the bet — he had to **gloat** about it for a solid week.

### In the wild
Nobody ever admits to it in the present tense. The word's natural habitat is the phrase **\"I don't mean to gloat, but…\"** — said, always, by someone about to gloat.

# **harrowing** *(adj.)* — **HA-roh-ing**

### Means
Acutely distressing — an experience or account so painful it leaves you shaken afterwards.

Scale matters: *unpleasant* is a bad afternoon, *harrowing* is the thing you still can't talk about. It attaches to the **ordeal or the telling of it** — a harrowing escape, a harrowing testimony, harrowing footage — almost never to a person. And the pain is mental: a harrowing story doesn't hurt your body, it wrings you out.

⚠️ **harrowing ≠ harried.** *Harried* = hassled, pestered, rushed off your feet (a harried waiter). *Harrowing* = soul-wrenching. A harried morning is annoying; a harrowing one is traumatic.

### Trick to lock it in
> **h-ARROW-ing** — an arrow *through* you, not a scratch on you.

The hidden word marks the intensity: harrowing things pierce and stay lodged, which is why the word belongs to ordeals rather than mere inconveniences.

### In sentences
1. The commission's report contained **harrowing** testimony from survivors of the siege, much of it read aloud in silence.
2. I finally watched that documentary about the cave rescue — absolutely **harrowing**, I couldn't sleep after.

### In the wild
Hamlet's Ghost, on what he could tell his son: *"I could a tale unfold whose lightest word / Would **harrow** up thy soul."* Even the *lightest word* of it would tear him open — that's the register the adjective still carries.

### Where it comes from
A **harrow** is a farm implement: a heavy spiked frame dragged across ploughed ground to rip the clods apart. To *harrow* someone is to drag that over their mind.

The literal tool is still in the language — and the metaphor is why the word means *lacerating*, not merely *sad*.

# **indefinite** *(adj.)* — **in-DEF-uh-nit**

### Means
Not fixed or limited — with **no stated end, boundary, or precise form**. Two flavours: of *time* ("an indefinite postponement" — no return date given), and of *outline* ("an indefinite shape on the horizon" — blurry, unspecified).

The word doesn't claim endlessness; it claims **the limit was never set**. A contract suspended indefinitely may resume next week — nobody has said. That's why it carries a faint chill in official language: "detained indefinitely," "on indefinite leave." The speaker isn't promising forever, they're refusing to promise anything.

⚠️ **indefinite ≠ infinite.**
- **indefinite** = the limit is *undeclared*
- **infinite** = there *is* no limit

An indefinite delay might end on Tuesday. An infinite one never ends.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in + DEFINE + it** — nobody has defined it.

Hearing "define" inside the word keeps you on *unspecified* rather than *unending* — the boundary exists, it just hasn't been drawn.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal adjourned for an **indefinite** period, offering the families no date to hold on to.
2. They said the shop's shut **indefinitely**, which probably means it's shut for good.

### In the wild
Grammar class: *a* and *an* are the **indefinite articles** — "a dog" doesn't specify *which* dog, while *the* (definite) pins it down. Same logic, every sentence you speak.

### Where it comes from
Latin **finis** = *"end, boundary."* To **define** something is to *set bounds around it*; add **in-** ("not") and you get the unbounded, unpinned thing.

That root pays out across the exam: **finite, infinite, finish, final, confine, definitive**.

# **ingenious** *(adj.)* — **in-JEEN-yuhs**

*Three syllables. *Ingenuous* has four and a different vowel: in-JEN-yoo-uhs.*

### Means
Strikingly clever and inventive — said of a solution, device, plan, or of the mind that devises them.

The praise is for **contrivance**, not wisdom or depth. *Brilliant* admires raw intellectual power; *creative* admires originality; **ingenious** admires the neat trick — the unexpected workaround, the elegant bit of engineering that makes you say *how did you think of that?* It attaches happily to things as well as people (an ingenious hinge, an ingenious loophole), and it survives moral doubt: an excuse, a scam, or a forgery can all be ingenious, and calling it so is still a compliment to the craft.

**ingenious ≠ ingenuous.**
- **ingenious** = cleverly inventive
- **ingenuous** = innocent, frank, unguarded

An ingenious child builds a trap out of coat hangers; an ingenuous child tells you exactly where it is.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ingenious** has an **ENGINE** in it — same word, same root; an ingenious mind is an idea engine.

Engines are contraptions, and *ingenious* is precisely the praise you give a clever contraption or the person who rigged it.

### In sentences
1. The dam's fish ladder is an **ingenious** piece of design, coaxing salmon upstream by exploiting the very current that blocks them.
2. She found an **ingenious** way to fit a month's clothes into a carry-on — vacuum bags and a lot of nerve.

### In the wild
Cervantes' novel is *El ingenioso hidalgo Don Quijote* — **"The Ingenious Gentleman Don Quixote"**. The title is doing sly work: his ingenuity is real, and it is aimed entirely at windmills.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ingenium** = *"inborn talent, natural capacity"* (*in-* "in" + *gignere* "to beget") — the same root behind **genius**, **ingenuity**, **congenital**.

It also gives **engine**: a medieval *engin* was any ingenious contrivance, usually a siege machine. Engine and ingenious are the same word wearing different clothes.

🚫 **Not the root of *ingenuous*.** That's Latin *ingenuus*, "free-born" — hence frank, unspoilt, the manner of someone with nothing to hide.

# **keen** *(adj. / v.)* — **KEEN**

### Means
**Adj. 1 — sharp.** Of a blade, an edge, a wind, a mind: cutting. *A keen knife, a keen frost, a keen intellect.*
**Adj. 2 — acutely perceptive.** *A keen eye for forgeries*, keen hearing — senses tuned finer than other people's.
**Adj. 3 — eager, avid.** *Keen to help; a keen gardener.* This sense is everyday British English and mildly informal.
**Verb — to keen:** to wail loudly in grief, especially over the dead. A completely different word wearing the same spelling.

The thread through the adjective is *fineness of edge* — metal, wit, senses, or appetite, all honed rather than blunt. Note the intensity gradient: *interested* is polite, *keen* is leaning forward, *avid* is close to hungry. And unlike *shrewd* or *astute*, keen carries no whiff of calculation — a keen observer simply sees more, without any implication that they're working an angle.

⚠️ **keen ≠ ken.** *Keen* = sharp/eager. *Ken* = the range of what you know — *"quantum field theory is beyond my ken."* Same ancient root (knowing), opposite exam answers.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Keen rhymes with *clean* — a keen edge cuts clean, whether it's a blade, an eye, or an appetite.**

Every adjective sense is the same idea sharpened onto a different object: honed metal, honed senses, honed enthusiasm.

### In sentences
1. The critic brought a **keen** eye to the manuscript, catching an inconsistency three editors had walked past.
2. She's dead **keen** on the job — she's already emailed twice about the start date.

### In the wild
In *Hamlet*'s play scene, Ophelia tells him **"You are keen, my lord, you are keen"** — meaning *your wit is cutting* — and Hamlet's reply, *"It would cost you a groaning to take off mine edge,"* spells the metaphor out: keen = edged.

### Where it comes from
Old English **cēne** = *"bold, brave, wise"* — from the Germanic root for **knowing** (*cunnan*). That family gives you **can**, **cunning**, **uncanny**, and **ken**: all about knowledge, which is why *keen* drifted from "clever" to "sharp."

🚫 **The verb is a different word.** *To keen* over a corpse is Irish **caoinim**, "I weep" — borrowed into English in the 1800s. No connection to sharpness at all.

# **multifaceted** *(adj.)* — **muhl-tee-FAS-uh-tid**

*The middle is FAS (as in facet), not FAYS — never "multi-FACE-ted."*

### Means
Having **many distinct sides, aspects, or talents** — a problem with several dimensions, a person good at several things.

The word flatters. **Complicated** and **convoluted** treat many-sidedness as a burden; *multifaceted* treats it as richness — depth, range, something rewarding to turn over. It's the word a citation or a profile piece uses ("a multifaceted career", "a multifaceted approach"), which also makes it faintly corporate when overused. Applied to an issue it means *don't expect one cause*; applied to a person it means *don't put them in one box*.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **facet** is a little **face** — and a multifaceted thing has many, every one of them flattering.

The hidden word *face* gives you the meaning, and the gem-cutting sense reminds you the term is praise, not complaint.

### In sentences
1. The commission's report treats homelessness as a **multifaceted** crisis, resisting the temptation to name a single villain.
2. She's the most **multifaceted** person on the team — codes, negotiates, and somehow runs the offsite.

### In the wild
Job ads and performance reviews run on it: *"a multifaceted role"* is HR for *you'll be doing five jobs*, and *"a multifaceted contributor"* is the compliment you get instead of a raise.

### Where it comes from
From French **facette**, "little face" — the flat cut surfaces a jeweller grinds onto a gemstone, so a diamond literally has facets. Behind it, Latin **facies** = *face, form*.

Same root: **facade** (the face of a building), **surface**, **efface** and **deface** (rub out / spoil the face).

- **Meaning cluster** · [Good at many things](groups.md#meaning__good-at-many-things) — with *versatile, resourceful*

# **omniscient** *(adj.)* — **om-NISH-uhnt**

*The -sci- is "sh", not "sky". British speakers often say om-NIS-ee-uhnt.*

### Means
Knowing everything — every fact, every motive, every hidden thing, all at once.

It's a word built for gods and narrators. Applied to an actual person it almost always tilts: either awe ("the omniscient Ms. Patel, who knew about the party before we planned it") or a sneer at someone who behaves as if nothing can be told to them. *Knowledgeable* is a compliment about effort; **omniscient** is an absolute, so a human can only ever approximate it — which is why it lands as either worship or mockery, rarely plain description.

⚠️ **omniscient ≠ prescient.**
- **omniscient** = knows *everything*, across all time
- **prescient** = knows what's coming *beforehand* — foresight only

A prescient investor called the crash. An omniscient one wouldn't have needed to call it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Omni** + **sci** — *all* + *science*. Nobody in the room qualifies, so if you're calling a colleague omniscient, you're being sarcastic.

The word is an absolute, and absolutes applied to humans read as irony.

### In sentences
1. The novel is told by an **omniscient** narrator who moves freely between the minds of all seven passengers.
2. Ask Dev — he's **omniscient** about the office gossip, somehow knows things before they happen.

### In the wild
Straight out of English class: **third-person omniscient** — the narrator who can see inside every character's head, as opposed to *limited*, who's stuck with one. If you've ever labelled a point of view, you've used this word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **omnis** (*all*) + **scīre** (*to know*).

Both halves pay rent across the GRE: *omnis* gives **omnipotent** (all-powerful), **omnipresent**, **omnivore**, **omnibus**; *scīre* gives **science**, **conscience** (knowing-with-yourself), **prescient** (knowing beforehand), and **nescient** (*not* knowing — ignorant).

# **remarkable** *(adj.)* — **ri-MAR-kuh-buhl**

### Means
Striking or unusual enough to be worth noticing and commenting on; extraordinary.

The praise is deliberately cool. *Amazing* and *incredible* report the speaker's feelings; **remarkable** reports a fact about the thing — it compels comment. That restraint is why it sounds credible in obituaries, citations and reviews, and why an examiner or biographer will use it where a fan would gush. It also runs in reverse: a **remarkable lack of judgement**, a **remarkable failure** — anything conspicuous enough to draw remark, good or bad.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Remarkable** doesn't say *amazing* — it says people will **remark**.

The word is literally "able to be remarked on," so it praises by pointing at the reaction rather than by shouting.

### In sentences
1. Her recovery was **remarkable**: three months after the accident she was running again.
2. That's a **remarkable** amount of cheese for one sandwich.

### In the wild
Radiologists close reports with **"unremarkable"** — the scan gave them nothing worth commenting on. In medicine, being unremarkable is the best possible news, which pins the meaning exactly: remarkable = *stands out enough to mention*.

# **sober** *(adj. / v.)* — **SOH-ber**

### Means
**1.** Not drunk. **2.** Serious, restrained, and clear-headed — free of excitement, exaggeration, or excess. **3.** Of colours or clothes: plain, quiet, undecorated. **Verb:** *sober (up)* — to make or become serious or clear-headed.

On the GRE it's almost always sense 2, and it's usually **approving**: a *sober assessment* is one you can trust because nobody is hyping it. Sober isn't sad and it isn't dull — it's unintoxicated in the broad sense, judgment unclouded by drink, hope, or panic. When something is described as **sobering**, it has jolted you out of comfortable optimism.

⚠️ **sober ≠ somber.** *Sober* = serious and clear-eyed; *somber* (sombre) = gloomy, dark, funereal. A sober analysis is useful; a somber mood is miserable.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sober strips something away — the drink, the decoration, or the drama.**

A sober man has lost the drink, a sober grey suit has lost the decoration, and a sober forecast has lost the drama.

### In sentences
1. After a decade of triumphalist forecasts, the committee delivered a **sober** account of what the project would actually cost.
2. Give it a night — you'll feel differently about quitting once you're **sober** and it's Monday.

### In the wild
News writing runs on the figurative sense: *"a **sobering** reminder,"* *"**sobering** new figures"* — the standard way a headline says *stop feeling optimistic*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sōbrius** = *sē-* ("without") + ***ēbrius*** ("drunk"). That second half is the payoff: *ēbrius* gives you **inebriated** — so *sober* is literally *un-inebriated*, and **sobriety** is the state of it.

# **stem from** *(phrasal v.)* — **STEM fruhm**

### Means
To **originate in**; to arise as a result of some earlier cause.

What it points at is almost always abstract — problems, beliefs, conflicts, resentments, policies — not physical objects. A river doesn't stem from a lake; a dispute stems from a misunderstanding. And it looks *backwards*: "stems from" is the move you make when you've got an effect in hand and are naming its source. Compare *results in*, which runs the other way, and *derives from*, which is drier and more technical. Neutral in tone, but it drifts toward diagnosing trouble — you far more often read that a failure stemmed from something than that a triumph did. The *from* is obligatory; the verb never stands alone in this sense.

⚠️ **stem from ≠ stem.**
- **stem from** = *come out of* a source
- **stem** (alone) = *stop the flow* — *stem the tide*, *stem the bleeding*

Two unrelated verbs. One traces a flow back; the other dams it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A stem always runs back to a root — so *stem from* names the root, never the branch.**

The phrase is a backwards-pointing verb: you say it about the cause, not the consequence.

### In sentences
1. The commission concluded that the outage **stemmed from** a single misconfigured server, not from sabotage.
2. Half his stress **stems from** the fact that he refuses to say no to anyone.

### In the wild
**Stem cells** — the same metaphor, made literal: the cell every other cell type grows out of.

# **subdued** *(adj.)* — **suhb-DYOOD**

*American speakers usually say suhb-DOOD; stress never falls on "sub."*

### Means
**Of a person:** quiet, low-spirited, noticeably less lively than usual. **Of light, colour, or sound:** softened, muted, toned down.

The word only works against a former higher level — it names a *drop*. A naturally shy person isn't subdued; a loud friend who has gone quiet after bad news is. Something pressed them down, and the word leaves that cause unnamed but implied, which is why "the mood was subdued" hangs so heavy in a news report. Applied to light or colour it loses the sadness entirely and becomes tasteful: **subdued** décor is deliberately restrained, never drab.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sub-dued = turned down, not turned off** — the dimmer switch, not the off switch.

Whatever is subdued is still running, just held below its normal level — that residue of the original energy is the whole word.

### In sentences
1. The delegation returned in a **subdued** mood, having conceded far more than its mandate allowed.
2. He's usually the loudest one in the pub, so his **subdued** hello told me something had happened.

### In the wild
"**Subdued lighting**" is estate-agent and restaurant-menu boilerplate — the dimmed, flattering half-light you sit in on a nice evening. Same word, no sadness at all.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** (*under*) + **dūcere** (*to lead*) — literally *led under*, brought beneath someone's control. To **subdue** an enemy is to conquer them; to be **subdued** is to have that pressure applied to your mood.

**dūcere** is a GRE workhorse: *induce, deduce, conduct, produce, seduce, ductile, aqueduct* — all leading something somewhere.

# **token** *(adj. / n.)* — **TOH-kuhn**

### Means
**Adj.:** done as a **minimal gesture** — just enough to look like compliance, never enough to matter.
**Noun:** a sign, mark, or keepsake that *stands for* something bigger (a token of gratitude; a subway token).

Both senses share one idea — a **stand-in for the real thing** — but the tone splits hard. The noun is warm or plain: a token of affection is a genuine emblem. The adjective is dismissive, almost an accusation: a *token* raise, a *token* apology, a *token* seat on the board. It concedes that the thing happened while denying it any weight. Watch the collocations — **token gesture, token effort, token resistance, token amount** — the word almost always sits in front of a noun that should have been substantial and wasn't. Related **tokenism** is flatly pejorative: including someone as a symbol so you can point at the symbol.

### Trick to lock it in
> A token is a coin that isn't money — a token effort is work that isn't work.

It's shaped like the real thing, it passes at a glance, and it's worth nothing outside the machine it was minted for.

### In sentences
1. The ministry's inquiry proved a **token** exercise: three afternoons of testimony, no findings, no consequences.
2. They gave us a **token** pay rise and expected us to shut up about the extra hours.

### In the wild
The arcade token — you hand over real money and get back a brass disc that looks like a coin, spends like a coin, and buys nothing anywhere else. That gap between *looks like currency* and *is currency* is the adjective in one object.

### Where it comes from
Old English **tācen** = *"sign, mark, portent"* — still visible in **betoken** (to be a sign of). It traces to a root meaning *to show*, the same one behind Latin *dīcere* and English **indicate, index, digit, dictate**.

So the oldest sense is simply **a sign** — and the modern insult follows straight from it: a token effort is a *sign* of effort, offered instead of the effort.

# **unravel** *(v.)* — **un-RAV-uhl**

### Means
Literally, to undo something woven or knitted by pulling its threads apart. Figuratively it runs in **two opposite directions**, and the GRE tests both:

- **Transitive — you unravel it:** to untangle and solve. *Unravel the mystery, the plot, the accounting fraud.* Patient, thread-by-thread work; the answer was always in there, knotted up.
- **Intransitive — it unravels:** to fall apart progressively. *The ceasefire unravelled. His marriage unravelled.* Not a sudden break but a **slow, self-accelerating disintegration** — one thing gives, then everything attached to it.

What links them is the sweater: the same pull that reveals the pattern also destroys the garment. Note the tone difference — the first is a compliment to someone's intelligence, the second is an obituary for something that used to hold together.

⚠️ **ravel is not the opposite of unravel.** *Ravel* means to fray or tangle — and, confusingly, also to unravel. The `un-` here doesn't reverse anything, so if a passage uses **ravel**, do not read it as "weave back together."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Same loose thread, two endings: the detective *unravels* the case, the suspect *unravels* on the stand.**

Pulled deliberately it yields the truth; pulled by circumstance it just comes apart — one verb, both outcomes.

### In sentences
1. Three years of forensic accounting were needed to **unravel** the shell companies the money had been threaded through.
2. She held it together through the funeral and then **unravelled** completely in the car park.

### In the wild
**"Unravel"** — the *Tokyo Ghoul* opening theme, a song entirely about a mind coming apart at the seams. The intransitive sense, four minutes long.

# **withstand** *(v.)* — **widh-STAND**

### Means
To **hold out against** a force, attack, or pressure — and not give way.

The word is about **structural survival**, not suffering. *Endure* and *bear* look inward at pain patiently absorbed; *withstand* looks outward at the thing pushing on you and reports that it failed. It takes a force as its object — a siege, a storm, 200 mph winds, scrutiny, temptation — and it quietly implies you were built well enough for the job.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Withstand** has **stand** sitting inside it — the test isn't whether it hurt, it's whether you're still standing.

Something hit you and the structure held, which is why the word implies strength rather than patience.

### In sentences
1. The garrison **withstood** three months of bombardment before the walls finally gave.
2. My phone case is fine, but I don't think it can **withstand** another drop like that.

### In the wild
It's the standard verb on spec sheets: a watch **rated to withstand 100 m**, a roof **built to withstand hurricane-force winds**. Whenever a product boasts about toughness, this is the word it reaches for.

### Where it comes from
Old English **wiþ** = *"against"* (not "alongside") + **standan** = *"to stand."* To withstand is literally to **stand against**.

That old adversarial *with-* unlocks a set: **withhold** (hold back), **withdraw** (draw back), and best of all **notwithstanding** = *"not standing against it"* → **despite**. The fossil survives in ordinary speech too: to *fight with* someone is to fight against them.
