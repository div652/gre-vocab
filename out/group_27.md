# **antagonize** *(v.)* — **an-TAG-uh-nyze**

*British spelling: antagonise.*

### Means
To provoke someone into active hostility — to behave so that a person turns *against* you. Also, in science: to act against, counteract, or block (one drug antagonizes another; one muscle antagonizes its partner).

The difference from *annoy* or *irritate* is what gets damaged. Annoying someone changes their mood; antagonizing them changes the **relationship** — you have converted a neutral party into an opponent. That's why it usually arrives as strategic advice about someone who can hurt you: *don't antagonize the landlord, the regulator, the judge*. It's often deliberate and always costly.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Antagonize** = hand someone the role of **antagonist** in your story.

You're not merely irritating them — you're casting them as the villain who now works against you for the rest of the plot.

### In sentences
1. The proposed tariffs **antagonized** trading partners whose cooperation the treaty still required.
2. Just apologise — there's no point **antagonizing** the one guy who can approve your leave.

### In the wild
In pharmacology the word is literal and everyday: naloxone (Narcan) is an opioid **antagonist** — it blocks the receptor, working directly against the drug. Same idea as antagonizing a person: not annoying it, *opposing* it.

### Where it comes from
Greek **agōn** = *"a contest, a struggle"* (the games, the arena) + **anti-** = *"against"*. An *antagonist* is the one struggling against you.

That root pays out across the list: **agony** (the struggle itself), **agonize** (to writhe in it), **protagonist** (*prōtos*, first — the one who struggles *first*, i.e. the lead), **antagonism**.

# **barren** *(adj.)* — **BA-ruhn**

*Identical in sound to baron.*

### Means
Producing nothing: **land** too poor to grow crops, a **woman or animal** unable to bear young, or an **effort/period** that yields no result — *barren negotiations*, *a barren decade for the club*.

The word isn't a synonym for *empty*. A cupboard is empty; a field is **barren** — the failure is one of *yield*, of something that ought to produce and can't. That's why it stings when aimed at a person: *barren* is the old, cold, blaming word for infertility, and modern writing prefers *infertile* for exactly that reason. Keep it for landscapes and fruitless efforts.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **barren baron** leaves no heir — the bloodline, like the soil, yields nothing.

Same sound, and the image locks in the key idea: not merely bare, but *incapable of producing*.

### In sentences
1. Nothing grew on that **barren** hillside but gorse and wind-bent thorn.
2. Three meetings in and we've got nothing — completely **barren**.

### In the wild
In *The Handmaid's Tale*, Gilead's law holds that sterile men don't exist — there are only women who are fruitful and women who are **barren**. The novel's whole horror runs on that one word.

# **bombastic** *(adj.)* — **bom-BAS-tik**

### Means
Of speech or writing: **inflated, pompous, high-sounding — and empty**. Grand words doing the work that substance should be doing.

The charge is against the *style*, not the argument. A bombastic speaker isn't lying and isn't stupid; he's **padding** — swelling small thoughts with big vocabulary and thunderous delivery. It's always an insult, and it carries a whiff of the ridiculous: the listener can hear the gap between the volume and the content. Compare **eloquent** (grand and earned), **verbose** (merely too many words), **grandiloquent** (the close cousin — same puffery, slightly more polished-sounding).

### Trick to lock it in
> **Bombastic** sounds like *bomb blast* — all bang, no shrapnel.

The sound hook gives you the loudness; "no shrapnel" gives you the emptiness that makes it an insult.

### In sentences
1. The general's **bombastic** address promised total victory in a war his own staff privately expected to lose.
2. Skip his TED talk — it's forty **bombastic** minutes to say "work hard."

### In the wild
Shaggy's 1995 hit **"Boombastic"** — a deliberate pun on *bombastic*, and the song is four minutes of the narrator boasting about himself in enormous language. The word means exactly what he's doing.

### Where it comes from
**Bombast** was literally **cotton stuffing** — the padding tailors packed into Elizabethan doublets to make a man's chest look bigger (Latin *bombax*, "cotton"). Bombastic speech is padded the same way: bulked out to look impressive.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *bomb*.** The explosive is unrelated (Greek *bombos*, "booming noise") — the resemblance is what makes the mnemonic work, not what makes the word mean this.

# **cajole** *(v.)* — **kuh-JOHL**

### Means
To get someone to do something by **sustained sweet-talk** — flattery, coaxing, small promises, wearing down resistance with charm rather than argument or force.

Two things are always implied: the target **didn't want to do it**, and the persuader **kept at it**. You can't cajole someone in one sentence, and you can't cajole a willing person. Next to *persuade* (neutral, reason-based) and *browbeat* (force), cajoling sits in between — soft pressure applied repeatedly. The word carries a faint smirk: it credits the cajoler's skill while hinting the tactics were slightly beneath honest argument. It's affectionate when a parent does it to a toddler, faintly cynical when a lobbyist does it to a senator.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ca-JOLE = ca-JOLLY** — you *jolly* them along until they say yes.

The stressed syllable is literally the sound of *jolly*, which is the tactic: keep things light and flattering until the reluctant person gives in.

### In sentences
1. Only after weeks of dinners and quiet promises did the ambassador manage to **cajole** the smaller states into signing.
2. I had to **cajole** him into coming out — three texts, a free drink, and a lift home.

### In the wild
It's a fixture of political reporting: a party whip spends the night before a vote **cajoling** holdouts — the standard pairing is "cajoling and arm-twisting," which tells you exactly where the word sits on the scale (the soft half).

# **chary** *(adj.)* — **CHAIR-ee**

*Rhymes with *wary* and *vary* — not with *Harry*.*

### Means
Cautious, hesitant, unwilling to risk it — and, crucially, **sparing**: a chary person doesn't just watch their step, they hold something back.

The word almost always takes *of* or *about*: **chary of praise**, **chary about commitments**, **chary of lending money**. That construction is the tell. Where *cautious* describes how you move, *chary* describes how little you hand over — approval, information, cash, trust. It's a quiet, bookish word, and it carries a faint suggestion of stinginess that plain caution doesn't: being chary with compliments makes the compliment worth more, but also makes you look tight-fisted.

**chary ≠ wary.** *Wary* is watching for danger — eyes up, threat outside. *Chary* is holding back from giving — hands closed, the thing withheld. You are **wary of** a stranger; you are **chary of** trusting him with your keys.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Chary** hides **care** — too much care to let go of it.

It's *care* worn as an adjective: the chary man cares so much about the praise, or the money, that he keeps it.

### In sentences
1. The committee was notably **chary** of endorsing any figure it could not independently verify.
2. He's **chary** with compliments — one "not bad" from him is worth a standing ovation from anyone else.

### Where it comes from
Old English **cearig** = *"full of care, anxious"* — the same **caru** that gives you **care** and **careworn**. Chary is simply *care* turned into an adjective and left to age for a thousand years, drifting from "sorrowful" to "cautious" to "sparing."

🚫 Nothing to do with **charity**, **charisma**, or **cherish** — those are Latin/Greek imports (*carus* "dear", *charis* "grace"). Chary is homegrown Germanic.

# **curmudgeon** *(n.)* — **ker-MUJ-uhn**

*Three syllables — not "cur-MUD-gee-on". The *dg* is a plain **j**.*

### Means
A **bad-tempered, grumbling person** — stereotypically old, male, and unimpressed by everything modern.

Unlike *misanthrope*, which is cold and serious (hating humanity on principle), a curmudgeon is loud, specific, and faintly comic: he complains about the music, the prices, the neighbours' bins. That's why the word is usually said with a grin — people cheerfully call themselves curmudgeons, which nobody does with *misanthrope*. The implication is gruff surface, not real malice.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **cur** is a snarling mongrel dog — and a curmudgeon is all bark.

The growl is real but the bite isn't, which is exactly the word's affectionate edge.

### In sentences
1. The old critic had settled into the role of literary **curmudgeon**, dismissing each new novel before he had finished the first chapter.
2. He's a bit of a **curmudgeon** about group chats, but he'll still drive you to the airport at 5am.

### In the wild
Carl Fredricksen in Pixar's ***Up*** — the growling old man who tells the kids to get off his lawn and then turns out to be the softest character in the film. That arc *is* the word.

# **dirge** *(n.)* — **DERJ**

*One syllable — rhymes with *urge*.*

### Means
A **slow, mournful song or chant for the dead** — the music of a funeral, a lament sung over a grave.

The literal sense is ceremonial and sombre, but the word does most of its modern work as an **insult about tempo and mood**: any performance so slow, flat and joyless that it feels like a funeral. A *lament* expresses grief; a *dirge* is the sound of it — heavy, dragging, sung aloud. Call a ballad a dirge and you're not praising its depth, you're saying it killed the room.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A dirge drags you down to the dirt.**

*Dirge* and *dirt* share the opening sound, and both belong at the graveside — which also gives you the sneering sense, since a dirge is anything that *drags*.

### In sentences
1. The procession moved to a **dirge** of low brass, each note held longer than seemed bearable.
2. Their new album is one **dirge** after another — I nearly fell asleep at the gig.

### In the wild
Claudius in *Hamlet*, listing the upside-down state of Denmark: **"With mirth in funeral and with dirge in marriage."** A dirge at a wedding is exactly how wrong the word feels out of place.

### Where it comes from
From Latin **dīrige** — *"direct!"* — the first word of the antiphon sung in the medieval Office of the Dead: *Dirige, Domine, Deus meus, in conspectu tuo viam meam* ("Direct my way in thy sight, O Lord"). The service got nicknamed after its opening word, and the nickname became the name for the music.

Same root as **direct**, **dirigible** (a steerable airship) and **dirigisme** (state direction of an economy) — all from *dirigere*, "to steer."

# **estimable** *(adj.)* — **ES-tim-uh-buhl**

*Stress lands on the first syllable, not on -TIM-.*

### Means
Worthy of respect; deserving to be held in high regard.

It's cool-blooded praise. *Admirable* has warmth, *beloved* has affection, but **estimable** is the respect you grant someone on their merits — solid, creditable, worth taking seriously. Because it's formal and a touch old-fashioned, it very often arrives with a raised eyebrow: "my estimable colleague" in a debate almost always means the speaker is about to disagree with him. Praise held at arm's length.

**estimable ≠ inestimable — they are not opposites.**
- **estimable** = worthy of respect
- **inestimable** = too great to be measured (*inestimable value*, *inestimable loss*)

The *in-* negates the "can be counted" sense, not the "worthy" one — so *inestimable* is the bigger compliment, not the insult.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Estim-able = esteem-able.** Able to be esteemed — but esteem is measured out, never gushed.

The *-able* suffix tells you it's a rating someone has earned, which is why the word sounds like a formal appraisal rather than genuine affection.

### In sentences
1. The committee was chaired by an **estimable** jurist whose opinions no one dared summarise carelessly.
2. He's an **estimable** guy — I'd trust him with the accounts, I just wouldn't want to be stuck at dinner with him.

### In the wild
Courtroom and parliamentary boilerplate: **"my estimable colleague"** — a phrase that has never once been followed by agreement.

### Where it comes from
Latin **aestimāre** = *"to value, to put a price on."* The whole family is one act of appraisal: **estimate** (put a number on it), **esteem** (put a value on a person), **estimable** (worth valuing), **inestimable** (off the scale).

# **euphemism** *(n.)* — **YOO-fuh-miz-uhm**

*The adjective moves the stress: euphemistic = yoo-fuh-MIS-tik.*

### Means
A **mild, vague or roundabout word used in place of a blunt, harsh or taboo one** — *passed away* for *died*, *let go* for *fired*, *between jobs* for *unemployed*.

Two flavours, and the GRE cares which one you hear. At the kind end it's tact: you say *passed away* to spare a grieving person. At the other end it's laundering — *collateral damage*, *enhanced interrogation*, *rightsizing* — words chosen so nobody pictures what actually happened. Because of that second flavour, calling something "a euphemism" is usually a mild accusation: you're pointing out that the speaker picked the soft word deliberately. The opposite move has its own name — a **dysphemism** (*croaked*, *bean-counter*).

### Trick to lock it in
> **Eu-** is the *good* prefix — **eu**phoria, **eu**logy, **eu**phemism. A eulogy says only nice things about the dead; a euphemism says only nice things about the ugly.

Both words work by leaving the unflattering part out.

### In sentences
1. The report never used the word *torture*, retreating instead into the **euphemism** "enhanced interrogation."
2. "Restructuring" is just a **euphemism** for firing half the floor and hoping nobody says so.

### In the wild
Every flight safety briefing: "in the unlikely event of a **water landing**." Nobody lands on water — that's a crash, wrapped in a soft word so 200 people stay calm.

### Where it comes from
Greek **eu-** (*good, well*) + **phēmē** (*speech, saying*) = "speaking well of."

Both halves pay off. **eu-** gives you *euphoria* (good feeling), *eulogy* (good words), *euphony* (good sound), *euthanasia* (good death). **phēmē** gives you **blasphemy** — *blas-* (evil) + *phēmē*, the exact mirror image: speaking ill of the sacred.

# **excoriate** *(v.)* — **ik-SKOR-ee-ayt**

### Means
**To denounce someone ferociously and publicly.** (Older, literal sense: to strip the skin off.)

This is criticism as flaying — sustained, itemised, and delivered by someone with standing to deliver it. Judges excoriate counsel; editorials excoriate ministers; inquiries excoriate agencies. Set it beside its neighbours: **berate** is loud and personal in the moment, **rebuke** is short and almost polite, **excoriate** is the long articulate demolition that leaves nothing of the target intact. It also quietly flatters the critic — you have to be somebody before your attack counts as an excoriation.

⚠️ **excoriate ≠ exonerate.** Same shape, opposite verdicts: *excoriate* = flay them in public; *exonerate* = clear them of blame. "The inquiry ___ the chief constable" reverses completely depending on which one you pick.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ex-CORE-iate** — peel the hide off until only the core is left.

The "core" you hear in the middle is the point: it isn't a jab, it's stripping someone down layer by layer in front of an audience.

### In sentences
1. The inquiry's final chapter **excoriated** the regulator for a decade of deliberate blindness.
2. Don't post that draft tonight — the replies are going to **excoriate** you.

### In the wild
Still literal in medicine: DSM-5 lists **excoriation disorder** (compulsive skin-picking), and dermatologists write "excoriations" for scratch marks on a chart — the skin sense never left the word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** ("off") + **corium** ("hide, skin") — to take the hide off. *Corium* also gives **cuirass**, the leather breastplate.

🚫 **Not *excruciate*.** That's Latin *crux*, "cross" — pain on a cross, not pain from a flaying. Different root, different torture.

# **exigent** *(adj.)* — **EK-si-juhnt**

*Stress the front — not "eg-ZIJ-uhnt." The related noun is exigency (EK-si-juhn-see or ig-ZIJ-uhn-see).*

### Means
**1.** (of a situation) urgent; **requiring immediate action**. **2.** (of a person or task) **demanding**, exacting, hard to satisfy.

*Urgent* just says the clock is running. **Exigent** says the situation is actively **making demands on you** — it presses, it exacts, it leaves you no slack. That's why the two senses live in one word: an exigent crisis and an exigent boss both want something from you *now*, and neither will take "later" for an answer. It's a formal word — you'll meet it in law, journalism and boardrooms, not in conversation.

⚠️ **exigent ≠ exiguous.**
- **exigent** = *demanding, pressing*
- **exiguous** = *scanty, meagre, too small*

An exigent deadline on an exiguous budget: huge pressure, tiny resources.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Exigent** = **"exact"** with a different ending — an exigent situation *exacts* something from you.

Same Latin verb, same idea of extracting payment under pressure, which is exactly why the word means demanding as well as urgent.

### In sentences
1. The board deferred the strategy review, judging the liquidity crisis the more **exigent** matter.
2. Sorry, can't chat — something **exigent** just landed in my inbox and it's on fire.

### In the wild
**"Exigent circumstances"** — the legal doctrine every cop show leans on: police may enter without a warrant when someone's in danger or evidence is about to be destroyed. If you've heard a TV detective justify kicking a door in, you've heard this word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **exigere** = *"to drive out, demand, require"* — from **ex-** (out) + **agere** (to drive, to do).

That root pays out widely: **exact** (both "precise" and "to exact a toll"), **exigency**, and from *agere* alone — **agent, agenda, act, agile, agitate**. Anything that *drives*.

# **haughty** *(adj.)* — **HAW-tee**

*The gh is silent — it rhymes with naughty.*

### Means
Arrogant in a **visible, chin-up way** — treating other people as beneath you and letting it show in your manner.

*Arrogant* is about the claims you make; *haughty* is about the **bearing you carry** — the cool stare, the clipped reply, the refusal to be impressed. It's the aristocrat's vice, and it's always an outsider's verdict: nobody describes themselves as haughty. Slightly literary, faintly old-world, and never a compliment.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Haughty** is *haute* with a **-y**: haute couture is high fashion, and a haughty person keeps her chin at exactly that altitude.

The word is literally "high-y," which is why it names a posture rather than an opinion.

### In sentences
1. The duchess received her guests with a haughty civility that made everyone feel dismissed and inspected at once.
2. He gave me one haughty look over his glasses and went back to his laptop.

### In the wild
Proverbs 16:18, King James Bible: *"Pride goeth before destruction, and an **haughty** spirit before a fall."* — the word's home turf, and a reminder that it always precedes a comeuppance.

### Where it comes from
Old French **haut** = *"high"* (from Latin **altus**), so haughty = *high-and-mighty*, literally.

Same root pays out across the exam: **altitude**, **alto**, **exalt**, **hauteur** (the noun for this exact manner), and **haute cuisine**.

# **heady** *(adj.)* — **HED-ee**

### Means
**1.** Intoxicating — strong enough to make you dizzy or giddy: *heady wine, a heady perfume, the heady thrill of a first win.*
**2.** (Older, GRE-friendly sense) **rash, impetuous** — acting on the rush rather than on judgment.

*Exciting* is about the event; **heady** is about what the event does to your head. It names the swimming, slightly unsteady feeling — and almost always smuggles in a warning: someone under a heady influence is not thinking straight. Hence *"the heady days of the revolution"*, which is never quite a compliment; it means everyone was drunk on the moment. Applied to smells and drinks it's sensuous and positive; applied to success or power, it's a raised eyebrow.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Heady = it goes to your HEAD** — and whatever's gone to your head has left your judgment.

The word wears its meaning on the outside: wine, perfume, praise and power are all "heady" precisely because they rise to the head and displace sense.

### In sentences
1. The heady optimism of the early months gave way, by winter, to a sober accounting of what the campaign had actually achieved.
2. Two promotions in a year — heady stuff for someone who was interning here in June.

### In the wild
Perfume and wine copy runs on this word — "a **heady** blend of jasmine and oud," "**heady** notes of blackcurrant." It's the marketing term for a scent so strong it makes you swoon.

# **imperturbable** *(adj.)* — **im-per-TER-buh-buhl**

### Means
Unshakeably calm; incapable of being agitated, flustered, or rattled.

The word only does its work when there's something to be rattled *by* — a crisis, an insult, a plane losing an engine. Plain **calm** describes a moment; **imperturbable** describes a temperament, a settled quality of the person. It leans admiring (the surgeon, the negotiator, the butler), but it can carry a faint chill too — someone so unmoved that you start to wonder whether anything registers at all.

**imperturbable ≠ impertinent.** Same *imper-* opening, opposite social effect: *imperturbable* = unflappably composed; *impertinent* = cheeky, out of line. The imperturbable host smiles through the impertinent question.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Turbulence** hits everyone on the plane; the **imperturbable** passenger doesn't spill her coffee.

The *turb* is literally *turbulence* — stirring, churning — and *im-* means it never gets through.

### In sentences
1. Under three hours of hostile cross-examination the witness remained **imperturbable**, correcting dates in the same flat voice she had used at the start.
2. Our nan is **imperturbable** — the oven caught fire and she just said "well, that's the pie done."

### Where it comes from
Latin **turbāre** = *"to stir up, throw into confusion,"* from **turba**, *"a crowd, an uproar."* Picture the disorder of a mob and the whole family opens up: **disturb**, **perturb**, **turbulent**, **turbid** (water stirred cloudy), **turmoil**, and even **trouble**. *Imperturbable* = the crowd cannot be made to churn.

# **implacable** *(adj.)* — **im-PLAK-uh-buhl**

*Short "a" as in cat — not im-PLAY-kuh-buhl, a common slip.*

### Means
Impossible to appease, soften, or talk round — hostility or opposition that no apology, concession, or plea will touch.

The force of the word is in what *doesn't* work on it. An angry person can be calmed; an implacable one cannot, and that's the point — it describes a permanent setting, not a mood. So it carries weight rather than pettiness: implacable enemies, implacable hatred, implacable opposition. It stretches easily to impersonal things that simply keep coming — the implacable advance of the tide, of age, of the deadline — and when applied to a person's *resolve* rather than their malice, it can even read as admiring.

⚠️ **implacable ≠ impeccable.** Near-identical on the page and in the ear, opposite in feel: **implacable** = cannot be appeased; **impeccable** = flawless (Latin *peccare*, "to sin" — literally *sinless*).

### Trick to lock it in
> You cannot **placate** the im-**plac**-able.

The word wears its own test: *placate* means to soothe with concessions, and this is the person on whom concessions are wasted.

### In sentences
1. Two apologies and a resignation did nothing; the committee remained **implacable**.
2. She's been **implacable** about that parking space since 2019 — don't even try.

### In the wild
Kyle Reese's warning in **The Terminator** is the finest definition of *implacable* ever filmed: *"It can't be bargained with. It can't be reasoned with. It doesn't feel pity, or remorse, or fear. And it absolutely will not stop, ever, until you are dead."*

### Where it comes from
Latin **placāre** = *"to soothe, calm down"* + **in-** (*not*). Literally: **un-soothe-able.**

Same root pays out across the exam: **placate** (soothe), **placid** (soothed, calm), **complacent** (smugly pleased and therefore idle), **placebo** (*"I shall please"*).

# **lambaste** *(v.)* — **lam-BAYST**

*Also spelled lambast, and often said lam-BAST — both are standard.*

### Means
To **attack someone with harsh, sustained criticism** — and, in the older physical sense, to beat or thrash.

Volume and audience are what set it apart. You *reprimand* an employee behind a closed door; you *lambaste* a minister on the front page. It implies a pounding rather than a point — the critic keeps swinging past the moment anyone stopped disagreeing. Slightly journalistic in flavour, and it always makes the attacker sound forceful, never fair.

⚠️ **lambaste ≠ bombast.** *Lambaste* is what you do **to** someone (savage them); **bombast** is inflated, pompous speech you produce **yourself**. A bombastic speaker lambastes his rivals in a great deal of hot air.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Lamb + baste** — the poor thing got publicly **roasted**.

The slang "roasted" is exactly the register: lambasting is loud, sustained, and served up for an audience, never a quiet word in private.

### In sentences
1. The editorial **lambasted** the committee for a decade of quiet inaction, naming every member in turn.
2. Mum **lambasted** me for twenty solid minutes over one unwashed pan.

### In the wild
This word lives almost entirely in news headlines — "Critics lambast sequel", "Senator lambasted over remarks." If you meet it anywhere else, someone is writing like a tabloid.

### Where it comes from
Probably **lam** ("to beat" — as in *on the lam*, running from a hiding) + **baste** ("to thrash"). Two words for hitting, welded together — the redundancy *is* the meaning: an excessive beating.

🚫 Nothing to do with basting a roast in the kitchen; that's a different *baste* entirely. The lamb is a pun, not a parent.

# **miscreant** *(n. / adj.)* — **MIS-kree-uhnt**

*Three syllables, stress up front — not "mis-CREE-ant."*

### Means
**Noun:** a wrongdoer, a villain, someone who behaves badly. **Adjective:** depraved, unprincipled.

Scale and tone are what you're buying. A *criminal* is a legal category; a *miscreant* is a moral judgement delivered from a height — the word is faintly archaic and very often dry or amused. Headmasters, magistrates and narrators use it. It fits the boys who egged the car far better than it fits a murderer, and when it *is* used of someone genuinely dangerous, the effect is deliberately arch, as though the speaker refuses to grant them the dignity of a bigger word.

### Trick to lock it in
> **miscre-ANT** — a pest, not a predator.

The word literally ends in *ant*, which is the right size for it: miscreants are scurrying nuisances you sweep up, not monsters you flee.

### In sentences
1. The magistrate surveyed the row of **miscreants** with the weary contempt of a man who had seen this exact crime forty times before.
2. Some **miscreant** has been nicking my lunch from the office fridge.

### In the wild
Indian newspaper English keeps this word alive daily: *\"unidentified **miscreants** set fire to two buses overnight.\"* It's the standard police-blotter term for troublemakers nobody has managed to name yet.

### Where it comes from
Old French **mescreant** = *"disbelieving"* — **mes-** (*wrongly*) + **creant**, from Latin **credere**, *"to believe."* A miscreant was originally a **heretic or infidel**; since medieval Europe assumed the wrong faith meant the wrong morals, "unbeliever" slid into "scoundrel."

*Credere* is a workhorse root: **credit, credible, creed, credential, credulous, incredulous** — and **recreant** (one who *re*-believes, i.e. surrenders: a coward, a traitor).

# **peccadillo** *(n.)* — **pek-uh-DIL-oh**

*English keeps the double-l hard: DIL-oh, not the Spanish "DEE-yoh". Plural: peccadilloes or peccadillos.*

### Means
A **small sin** — a minor offence, a trifling fault, the kind of wrongdoing nobody is going to prosecute.

The word does two things at once: it admits the fault *and* waves it away. You cannot call something a peccadillo without also excusing it, which is why it turns up in indulgent, faintly amused, worldly-wise sentences — often about appetites (drinking, gambling, flirting) rather than blunders. Note the difference from its neighbours: a **transgression** is grave, a **foible** is a harmless quirk of temperament and not an offence at all, and a peccadillo sits between them — a real wrong, deliberately shrunk.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pecc**adillo starts like **peck** — and it ends in the Spanish "little" suffix **-illo**. A peck of a sin, not a bite.

The sound tells you the scale: a peck is the smallest thing a bird can do to you, and *-illo* shrinks whatever it's attached to.

### In sentences
1. His biographers treat the affair as a **peccadillo**, tucked into a footnote between two chapters of genuine achievement.
2. Leaving the milk out isn't a crime, it's a **peccadillo** — stop apologising.

### In the wild
Standard political-scandal vocabulary: reporters reach for "youthful **peccadilloes**" precisely when a party wants the story filed under *minor* and forgotten.

### Where it comes from
Spanish **pecadillo**, little sin — diminutive of *pecado*, from Latin **peccāre**, *"to sin, to stumble."*

Same root gives you the high-value GRE word **impeccable**: literally *un-sin-able*, incapable of fault — hence flawless taste, an impeccable record. Also **peccant** (offending, faulty).

# **philistine** *(n. / adj.)* — **FIL-uh-steen**

*Also FIL-i-styne, chiefly British — same word.*

### Means
Someone **smugly indifferent or actively hostile to art, culture and ideas**, who values only what is practical, profitable or comfortable. As an adjective: crass, uncultured, materialistic.

It is a **snob's word**, and it cuts both ways. Calling someone a philistine says they can't see the value of a poem or a painting — but it also announces that *you* can, and that you're looking down from a height. *Ignorant* is a lack; a philistine isn't merely ignorant, he's **contentedly** ignorant, even proud of it — the man who says "I could have painted that" and thinks he's scored a point.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Starts with *phil-*, but loves nothing it can't put a price on.**

The spelling promises a lover of something — like *philosophy* or *philanthropy* — and delivers a man who looks at a Rothko and asks what it sold for.

### In sentences
1. The gallery's new director dismissed the trustees as **philistines** who measured every acquisition by its insurance value.
2. Sorry to be a **philistine**, but I fell asleep about ten minutes into the opera.

### In the wild
**Goliath was a Philistine** — 1 Samuel, the giant brute jeering at the Israelite line until a shepherd boy drops him with a stone. Same people, same reputation: all bulk and swagger, no finesse.

### Where it comes from
Hebrew **P'lishtim**, the Philistines — the Israelites' coastal enemies in the Old Testament, cast throughout as crude warlike outsiders.

The modern sense is student slang. German university men called the non-academic townsfolk **Philister** — outsiders, the enemy across the wall — and Matthew Arnold hauled it into English in *Culture and Anarchy* (1869) to mock the middle class that cared about money and not beauty. That sneer is still audible in the word.

🚫 **Nothing to do with Greek *phil-*, "love."** *Philosophy, philanthropy, philology, bibliophile* — all that family, and the philistine belongs to none of it.

# **relegate** *(v.)* — **REL-uh-gayt**

### Means
To **consign something or someone to a lower rank, worse position, or lesser importance** — to demote, sideline, or banish it out of the way.

The motion is always **downward and dismissive**. *Demote* is a neutral administrative fact; *relegate* carries the shrug of whoever did it — the thing wasn't worth the better spot. Note also that it usually drags a destination behind it: relegated **to** the bench, **to** the basement, **to** a footnote, **to** the second division. And it works on things as easily as people: an idea, a language, a whole tradition can be relegated to irrelevance.

⚠️ **relegate ≠ delegate.**
- **delegate** = hand a task *out* to someone you trust with it
- **relegate** = push a person or thing *down* to somewhere lesser

You delegate the report to your deputy. You relegate the report to the shredder.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Relegate always names the worse place it sent you: to the bench, to the back page, to footnote 12.**

If you can't finish the sentence with "…to somewhere beneath," the word you want is *demote* or *delegate*, not *relegate*.

### In sentences
1. For a century her paintings were **relegated** to the storerooms, catalogued under her husband's name.
2. They **relegated** me to the kids' table again, at thirty-one years old.

### In the wild
Football: finish bottom three in the Premier League and your club is **relegated** to the Championship — the entire dread of the word, played out every May.

### Where it comes from
Latin **re-** (*back*) + **legāre** (*to send, to depute*) — literally *to send away*; in Rome, to banish a citizen from the city.

That *legāre* is worth banking: **delegate** (send someone in your place), **legate** (a papal envoy), **legacy** and **bequeath** (what you send on after you), **colleague** (one sent along with you).

# **repugnant** *(adj.)* — **ri-PUG-nuhnt**

### Means
Deeply offensive — disgusting in a way that offends your **moral sense**, not just your stomach or your taste.

The word carries a judgement. *Unpleasant* is preference, *disgusting* is often physical, but calling something **repugnant** says it violates a principle you hold: an idea, a proposal, a piece of behaviour that you refuse on ethical grounds. It's formal, it's what people say in editorials and courtrooms, and it always implies the speaker is pushing back.

There's also a drier legal sense you'll meet on the GRE: **incompatible, contradictory** — a clause *repugnant to* the rest of the contract is one that fights it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **PUG** sits in the middle — as in **pug**ilist, a fist-fighter. Repugnant things don't just bore you; something in you fights back.

That built-in shove is why the word is reserved for moral revulsion rather than mild dislike — and why the legal sense means "in conflict with."

### In sentences
1. The committee found the proposal morally **repugnant** and refused to put it to a vote.
2. Cheating people out of their pensions? That's just **repugnant**.

### In the wild
Chief Justice Marshall in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803): **"a law repugnant to the Constitution is void."** — the sentence that founded judicial review, and a clean use of the "in conflict with" sense.

### Where it comes from
Latin **re-** ("back") + **pugnāre** ("to fight"), from **pugnus**, "fist." Literally *to fight back against*.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **pugnacious** (spoiling for a fight), **pugilist** (boxer), **impugn** (attack someone's claim), **inexpugnable** (unassailable).

# **sentimental** *(adj.)* — **sen-tuh-MEN-tuhl**

### Means
Governed by tender feeling — nostalgia, affection, pity — rather than by reason; or **indulging** that feeling beyond what the occasion warrants.

Two temperatures. Said of a person keeping their grandmother's chipped teacup, it's warm and harmless. Said of a novel, a film, or an argument, it's a **put-down**: the emotion has been laid on thicker than the subject can bear, and the writer is enjoying the feeling rather than earning it. That critical edge is why the GRE likes it — *sentimental* prose is cheap prose. Note also that a sentimental person is not simply an emotional one: rage and terror are emotions, but nobody calls them sentimental. The word covers only the soft, sweet, backward-looking end of the range.

⚠️ **sentimental ≠ sententious.**
- **sentimental** = soppy, awash in feeling
- **sententious** = preachy, dishing out pompous moral maxims

A sentimental eulogy makes you cry; a sententious one lectures you.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside it: **senti-MENTAL**. Sentimental value is value that exists only in your *mental* picture of the thing — never in the thing.

That gap between the object and the feeling piled onto it is exactly what makes the word a criticism when it's aimed at writing.

### In sentences
1. Critics dismissed the memoir as **sentimental** — every death arrived on cue, scored for strings.
2. I know it's a cracked mug, but I'm too **sentimental** to bin it.

### In the wild
The everyday phrase **"it has sentimental value"** — the standard excuse for keeping something objectively worthless. That's the whole word in three syllables: the worth is in you, not in the object.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sentīre** = *"to feel."* One of the highest-yield roots on the test: **sentient** (able to feel), **sentiment**, **consent** (feel with), **dissent** (feel apart), **resent** (feel back at), **sensitive**, **sense**.

# **squander** *(v.)* — **SKWON-der**

To **waste something valuable** — money, time, talent, a lead, a chance — through carelessness or folly.

# **swindle** *(v. / n.)* — **SWIN-duhl**

### Means
**Verb:** to cheat someone out of money or property by deception. **Noun:** the scheme itself.

The mechanism is *trust*, not force — a swindler talks you into handing it over. A mugger robs you; a swindler is invited in. The word also implies scale and a story: a plausible investment, a fake charity, a sure thing. Compared with **defraud** (legal, clinical) or **con** (street-level, small), *swindle* is the tabloid word — vivid, contemptuous, and always with a gullible victim somewhere in the sentence.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Swindle** rhymes with **dwindle** — your savings dwindle while he's still talking.

Nothing is grabbed; the money quietly drains away under the cover of a good story.

### In sentences
1. The prospectus was elegant, the returns imaginary; he had **swindled** two hundred pensioners before anyone thought to check the registry.
2. Forty quid for a "guided tour" that lasted ten minutes — we got **swindled**.

### In the wild
The Sex Pistols' 1980 film **The Great Rock 'n' Roll Swindle**, in which manager Malcolm McLaren gleefully claims he invented the band to fleece the record industry — the word used exactly right: money extracted by performance.

### Where it comes from
From German **schwindeln** = *"to be dizzy"* — and, by extension, *to tell tall tales*. A **Schwindler** was a giddy schemer; London slang borrowed it in the 1760s. The swindler makes your head spin, and you sign.

# **tangible** *(adj.)* — **TAN-juh-buhl**

### Means
Literally: **able to be touched** — having physical substance. In practice, far more often: **real enough to be shown, measured, or pointed at** rather than merely claimed.

The word does its real work as the opposite of *vague*. When someone demands **tangible results**, they aren't asking for something touchable — they're saying that promises, intentions and good feelings don't count as evidence. So *tangible* carries a faint edge of scepticism about whatever preceded it: a manager asking for tangible progress has already decided the last report was fluff.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tangible** starts with **tang** — a tang is a taste you can actually *detect*, not one you're told about.

Both words demand that something register on the senses before you'll believe it exists.

### In sentences
1. The treaty produced no **tangible** reduction in troop numbers, only a shared vocabulary of restraint.
2. Six months of meetings and not one **tangible** thing to show for it.

### In the wild
Every company balance sheet splits **tangible assets** (buildings, machines, stock) from **intangible** ones (brand, goodwill, patents) — the accountant's line between what you could trip over and what you can only argue for.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tangere** = *"to touch."* One root, a whole GRE cluster: **tangent** (a line just touching a curve), **tangential** (barely touching the topic), **contact**, **contagious** (caught by touch), **tactile**, and **intact** — literally *un-touched*.

# **turpitude** *(n.)* — **TER-pi-tood**

*British: TER-pi-tyood.*

### Means
Depravity — wickedness of a **base, shameful, disgraceful** kind.

This isn't a word for ordinary bad behaviour. Where *wickedness* is moral and *misconduct* is procedural, **turpitude** is the vocabulary of tribunals: conduct so foul it *disqualifies* you — from the job, the office, the country. It survives almost entirely inside the fixed legal phrase **moral turpitude**, so it carries a whiff of the statute book. Used of everyday failings, it's deliberately grandiose, and usually a joke.

⚠️ **turpitude ≠ torpitude.** There's no such word — you're reaching for **torpor** (Latin *torpēre*, "to be numb"): sluggishness, inertia, the family of *torpid* and *torpedo* (the ray that numbs you). **Turpitude** is Latin *turpis*, "vile" — filth, not laziness. A sinner has turpitude; a sloth has torpor.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Turp-** ≈ **turd**: turpitude is moral filth — and it almost never appears without **moral** bolted in front of it.

The gross-out sound gives you the meaning and the collocation gives you the register: this is the word of disciplinary boards and visa forms, not of everyday scolding.

### In sentences
1. The bar association struck him off not for incompetence but for **moral turpitude** — four years of quietly forged client signatures.
2. Mate, you were twenty minutes late, it's not exactly **turpitude** — nobody's revoking your badge.

### In the wild
Every US visa application asks whether you have ever been arrested for **"a crime involving moral turpitude."** That form is where most people meet this word for the first and only time — and it tells you everything about its register.

# **unalloyed** *(adj.)* — **un-uh-LOYD**

### Means
**Pure, unmixed, complete** — with nothing lesser stirred in to dilute it.

It lives almost entirely with **positive abstractions**: *unalloyed joy, unalloyed delight, unalloyed admiration, an unalloyed success*. You don't get unalloyed misery. And note how often it arrives **negated** — "the deal was not an unalloyed good" is the reviewer's civilised way of saying *there's a catch*. Compared with *pure* (plain, wide-ranging) or *utter* (blunt, loud), *unalloyed* is formal and faintly wry: it concedes that a bit of alloy is the normal state of things.

### Trick to lock it in
> The word is just **un- + alloy**: gold with no cheap metal stirred in.

An alloy dilutes precious metal with base metal, so *unalloyed* joy is joy with no cheaper feeling mixed into it.

### In sentences
1. Her return to the stage after twelve years was met with unalloyed delight.
2. Free pizza and no meeting? Mate, that's unalloyed good news.

### In the wild
Op-ed and boardroom boilerplate: **"not an unalloyed good."** Whenever a columnist says that about AI, globalisation, or a merger, they mean *good, but there's a catch* — which is exactly why the word is worth owning.

### Where it comes from
From **alloy** ← Old French *aloier* ← Latin **alligare** = *"to bind to"* (*ad-* + *ligare*, "to tie"). An alloy is a metal **bound to** another, usually a cheaper one — so *unalloyed* is metal bound to nothing.

The *lig-* root ties a lot together: **ally, alliance, ligament, oblige, liable**.

# **undercut** *(v. / n.)* — **UN-der-kut**

### Means
**Verb:** to weaken something by taking away what holds it up — *undercut an argument, a policy, your own authority*. Also commercial: to sell cheaper than a rival, taking their customers out from beneath them.

Nothing is attacked head-on. What's undercut is still standing, just hollow: the claim remains on the page, but the support under it is gone. Set it against **undermine** — undermining is patient, usually deliberate, a slow tunnelling under someone's position over months. Undercutting takes one gesture, and it is very often self-inflicted: the trembling voice that undercuts the confident speech, the single exception that undercuts the whole rule.

### Trick to lock it in
> You **undermine** a rival over months; you **undercut** him in a sentence.

Undermining is slow deliberate sabotage, while undercutting is a single stroke that pulls the floor away — and it's usually the speaker's own slip that does it.

### In sentences
1. The witness's cheerful contradiction on cross-examination **undercut** the entire theory of the prosecution's case.
2. They **undercut** us by two quid a unit, and just like that we lost the contract.

### In the wild
The haircut called an **undercut** — shaved underneath, long on top — is the word doing its literal job: the whole mass is left in place, the support beneath it is cut away.

### Where it comes from
Plain *under* + *cut*, and the source is mining and logging: you **undercut** a coal seam or a tree by chopping a notch out of its base, so the mass above comes down under its own weight. That's the figurative sense exactly — you never touch the top, you remove what it rests on.

# **wheedle** *(v.)* — **WEE-duhl**

### Means
To get something out of someone by **soft, flattering, persistent charm** — sweet-talking them into it.

*Coax* can be kind and even tender; *cajole* is insistent; **wheedle** is the sly one. It always implies the flatterer is working an angle, and that the flattery is a tool rather than a feeling. Faintly comic too — the classic wheedlers are children after sweets and salesmen after signatures, not villains.

Watch the two grammars: you **wheedle someone into** doing something, and you **wheedle something out of** someone.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Wheedle rhymes with needle** — both work on you steadily, but the needle jabs and the wheedle flatters.

Same nagging persistence, opposite method: needling is pressure by irritation, wheedling is pressure by honey.

### In sentences
1. Over three dinners he **wheedled** the old woman into changing her will, praising her judgement at every course.
2. She **wheedled** an extra day off out of her manager by telling him he was the only reasonable person in the building.

# **xenophobic** *(adj.)* — **zen-uh-FOH-bik**

*The x is pronounced z — never "ex-". Noun: xenophobia, zen-uh-FOH-bee-uh.*

### Means
Hostile to or fearful of foreigners, immigrants, and anything foreign.

Despite the *-phobic* ending, this is not a clinical fear — it's a charge. Nobody describes themselves as xenophobic; it is what an opponent calls your immigration policy, your tabloid headline, your remark about "these people coming over here." It's also broader than **racist**: xenophobia targets the *outsider* — nationality, accent, origin, foreign food, foreign customs — so a country can be xenophobic toward people of its own race. The "fear" half is largely a polite fiction; what the word actually names is exclusion and contempt.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ends in *-phobic*, but it isn't a phobia.** Arachnophobia is a diagnosis; xenophobia is an accusation.

The suffix promises a private fear, but the word is always used as a public verdict on someone's politics.

### In sentences
1. The election was fought on **xenophobic** rhetoric: closed borders, foreign hands, a nation supposedly under siege.
2. He insists he's "just being realistic" about immigration, but half the pub can hear how **xenophobic** it sounds.

### In the wild
The alien in *Alien* is a **Xenomorph** — literally "strange form," the outsider whose foreignness is the whole horror.

### Where it comes from
Greek **xenos** = *"stranger, foreigner"* + **phobos** = *"fear."*

*Xenos* is worth banking: **xenon** (the noble gas, named "the strange one" because it wouldn't react with anything), **xenophile** (a lover of the foreign — the exact opposite), **xenograft** (tissue transplanted from another species).

And *phobos* gives you every *-phobia* you'll ever meet — plus **Phobos**, the moon of Mars named for the god of Fear.
