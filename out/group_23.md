# **allusive** *(adj.)* — **uh-LOO-siv**

*The s hisses — "-siv", never "-ziv".*

### Means
Full of **indirect references** — to books, myths, events, people — made in passing rather than spelled out.

The key is deliberateness. Allusive writing isn't unclear because the writer fumbled; it's indirect **on purpose**, and it assumes you already know the thing being pointed at. That makes it a compliment among literary types (dense, layered, sophisticated) and a complaint everywhere else (show-offy, impenetrable to outsiders). It describes **style**, not people: a poem, an essay, a remark can be allusive — a person is usually *evasive* instead.

⚠️ **allusive ≠ elusive.**
- **allusive** = *refers* indirectly (from **allude**)
- **elusive** = *escapes* you — hard to catch, pin down, or define (from **elude**)

**E** for **E**scape. An allusive clue points at something; an elusive answer runs away from you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **All**usive drops the reference and leaves you to catch it — all hint, no footnote.

The hint only works if you already know the source, which is exactly why allusive style flatters some readers and locks out the rest.

### In sentences
1. Her prose is so **allusive** that a single paragraph nods at Ovid, the Book of Job, and a Motown B-side.
2. He made some **allusive** comment about "what happened in Lisbon" and then just changed the subject.

### In the wild
T. S. Eliot's **The Waste Land** is the textbook case of allusive writing — it packs in so many buried quotations that Eliot bolted footnotes onto the end of the poem to explain his own references.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** ("toward") + **lūdere** ("to play") → *allūdere*, "to play at, touch on lightly, joke toward."

That **lūdere** root is a GRE workhorse: **elude** (play *away* → escape), **delude** (play someone false), **collude** (play together, in secret), **illusion**, **prelude**, **interlude**, **ludicrous**. Spot *-lud-/-lus-* and you're looking at play.

# **astute** *(adj.)* — **uh-STOOT**

*British speakers often say uh-STYOOT.*

### Means
Sharp in judgement — quick to see what's really going on and how to turn it to advantage.

This is praise, and it's practical praise. *Intelligent* is about horsepower; **astute** is about reading a situation — people, markets, motives — and acting on it before anyone else does. It lives in business and politics ("an astute negotiator", "politically astute"). There's a faint whiff of self-interest, of the person who noticed the angle, but the word stays complimentary: call someone **cunning** and you've insulted them, call them **astute** and you've praised them for the same skill.

**astute ≠ acute.** *Acute* is sharpness of sense or severity — *acute hearing, acute pain*. *Astute* is sharpness of judgement, and only ever describes a person or their decisions. An acute observer hears everything; an astute one knows what it means.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Astute** minds are sharp; **obtuse** ones are blunt — geometry already taught you which angle is the dull one.

The two near-rhymes are opposites, so the obtuse angle you drew in school anchors astute at the sharp end of the scale.

### In sentences
1. The committee's most **astute** member said nothing for an hour, then asked the one question that collapsed the proposal.
2. She made one **astute** move — bought the flat before the new station was announced — and never had to work again.

### In the wild
It's the standard compliment in financial journalism: every profile of a successful investor calls them an **astute** reader of the market — never a *clever* or *smart* one.

### Where it comes from
Latin **astūtus**, from **astus** = *"craft, cunning."* The word started out meaning *sly* and was gradually promoted into a compliment — which is exactly why a trace of calculation still clings to it.

# **commence** *(v.)* — **kuh-MENS**

### Means
To **begin** — but with an air of formality, procedure, or ceremony.

*Begin* and *start* are neutral workhorses; **commence** wears a suit. It belongs to proceedings, hostilities, trials, terms, and rituals — things with an official starting point that someone declares. Use it of a hearing or a countdown and it sounds right; use it of eating your sandwich and you sound pompous (which is exactly why it's often used as a joke). It also implies a *first* beginning, not a resumption.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Commence** is *begin* in a gown — which is why graduation is called **commencement**.

The word only ever shows up where there's a podium, a robe, or a rulebook.

### In sentences
1. Proceedings will **commence** at nine sharp, and latecomers will not be admitted to the chamber.
2. Right, everyone's here — let the chaos **commence**.

### In the wild
American universities call graduation **commencement** — not the end of your degree, but the official *beginning* of everything after it. The whole ceremony is the word's definition acted out.

### Where it comes from
Latin **com-** (intensive) + **initiare** = *"to initiate, begin"* — the same *initiare* behind **initial**, **initiate**, **initiative**. So *commence* is literally "to initiate," which is why it never quite loses its ceremonial edge.

# **convalescent** *(adj. / n.)* — **kon-vuh-LES-uhnt**

### Means
**Adjective:** recovering from illness or injury — gradually regaining strength. **Noun:** the person doing the recovering.

The word marks a *middle state*, and that's its whole point: a convalescent is no longer sick and not yet well. *Recovering* is the plain everyday word; **convalescent** is clinical and slightly genteel — it carries the smell of a nursing home, a sanatorium, a long slow armchair-and-blanket stretch rather than a Tuesday off work with the flu. It implies time. Nobody is convalescent for an afternoon.

It also travels figuratively: a **convalescent economy** is one that has stopped collapsing but is still weak.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-escent = becoming.** An adol**escent** is becoming an adult; a conval**escent** is becoming well.

That suffix always means mid-process, never arrived — which is exactly why a convalescent is still in bed.

### In sentences
1. After the surgery he spent six weeks **convalescent** at his sister's house on the coast, walking a little further each morning.
2. She's out of hospital but still **convalescing** — don't invite her to a five-a-side game just yet.

### In the wild
**Convalescent plasma** — blood plasma taken from recovered patients — was headline news through 2020 as an experimental COVID treatment. Recovered donors, literally the convalescent.

### Where it comes from
Latin **valēre** = *"to be strong, to be well"* + **con-** (intensifier) + **-escere** (*begin to*). To convalesce is to *start growing strong again*.

That root is a goldmine: **valid** (strong enough to hold), **invalid** (not strong — both the argument and the wheelchair sense), **prevail**, **valiant**, **valour**, **value**, and the Roman farewell **vale** ("be well").

# **curb** *(v. / n.)* — **KERB**

*In British English the stone edge of a pavement is spelled kerb; the verb is curb everywhere.*

### Means
**Verb:** to hold something in check — restrain, limit, keep within bounds.
**Noun:** the raised edge of a pavement; originally the chain under a horse's jaw that checks it.

Curb is **moderation, not abolition**. You *ban* a thing to end it; you *curb* it to keep it running at an acceptable level — and it implies the thing was already in motion and pulling hard. Governments curb inflation, spending, emissions; people curb their temper, their appetite, their tongue. It's the standard, faintly bureaucratic verb of policy prose, and it always carries the sense of a hand on the reins of something with its own momentum.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A curb doesn't stop the traffic — it keeps it in its lane.**

The kerb you drive beside is the whole meaning: it constrains without halting, which is exactly what curbing inflation or curbing your temper does.

### In sentences
1. The central bank raised rates sharply in an attempt to **curb** runaway inflation.
2. Could you **curb** the sarcasm for ten minutes? My parents are right there.

### In the wild
**Curb Your Enthusiasm** — the title is an order to dial it down, not switch it off, which is the verb in one phrase.

### Where it comes from
Latin **curvāre** *("to bend")* → Old French *courbe*. A **curb** was the chain under a horse's jaw: pull it and the head bends, and the horse checks itself.

Same root as **curve, curvature, curvaceous** — bending is the through-line: you bend a thing's course rather than break it.

# **decry** *(v.)* — **di-KRYE**

### Means
To **condemn something openly and loudly** — to denounce it as wrong, harmful, or worthless.

Volume and audience are built in. You can *criticise* a policy quietly over coffee, but you **decry** it from a podium, in print, in public. It also aims at things rather than people: you decry a trend, a ruling, a shortage, a fashion — where *denounce* points a finger at a person. And it carries a faint moral posture; the person decrying is taking a stand, sometimes more than they're making an argument.

⚠️ **decry ≠ descry.** *Descry* (also **di-SKRYE**) means to **catch sight of** something far off — "he descried a sail on the horizon." One condemns, one merely spots.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Decry** has **cry** in it — nobody decries anything quietly.

The word is literally "to cry down": public noise aimed at dragging something's value to zero.

### In sentences
1. Economists **decried** the tariffs as a tax on the poor dressed up as patriotism.
2. Everyone **decries** the group chat until they need something from it.

### In the wild
A headline workhorse — *\"Critics decry the ruling,\" \"Residents decry cuts to bus routes.\"* Journalists reach for it precisely because it means *complained loudly and publicly* without conceding the complaint was right.

### Where it comes from
Old French **descrier** = *"to cry down"* — **de-** (down) + **crier** (to cry, to proclaim). It was originally a money word: a king could *decry* a coin, publicly proclaiming it worthless so nobody would take it. That's still the shape of the modern sense — shouting a thing's value down to nothing.

# **duress** *(n.)* — **duu-RES**

*Stress the second syllable — not DUR-ess. British speakers often add a y: dyuu-RES.*

### Means
Compulsion by threat, violence, or unlawful pressure — force applied to make someone act against their will.

It lives almost entirely in one phrase: **under duress**. And it carries a built-in excuse. Saying you acted under duress is saying the act wasn't really yours: your consent was manufactured, so it shouldn't count. That's why it's a legal term first — a contract or confession obtained under duress is voidable. *Pressure* is neutral and can be fair; *duress* means the choice was taken away. It also gets used with dry humour for trivial coercion ("I watched the sequel under duress"), and the joke works precisely because the word is so heavy.

⚠️ **duress ≠ distress.** *Duress* is pressure someone puts **on** you; *distress* is the suffering you feel. A hostage signs under duress and is in distress.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Duress** is *dur*- as in **endure** and **obdurate** — someone gets *hard* with you until you say yes.

The hardness is coming from outside, which is why whatever you agreed to doesn't stick.

### In sentences
1. The court set aside the confession, finding it had been extracted under **duress** after eleven hours without counsel.
2. "I only agreed to the family photo under **duress** — Mum threatened to cancel Christmas."

### In the wild
Alarm panels and bank systems have a **duress code** — a second PIN you enter when someone is forcing you, which opens the door normally but silently calls the police. The word survives there in its purest sense: doing the thing, but not freely.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dūrus** = *"hard"* → **dūrāre**, *"to harden, to last."*

One root, a lot of GRE words: **durable** and **duration** (lasting), **endure** (stay hard under strain), **obdurate** (hardened against persuasion), **dour** (hard-faced), **during**. *Duress* is the hardness turned on a person until they give way.

### Also sits in
- **Meaning cluster** · [Force rather than persuade](groups.md#meaning__force-rather-than-persuade) — with *coercion, dictate, suppress*

# **evoke** *(v.)* — **ih-VOHK**

### Means
To **call up** a feeling, memory, image or atmosphere — usually **indirectly, by suggestion** rather than by force.

What's evoked was already latent in the person; the trigger only stirs it. A smell evokes a childhood kitchen, a chord evokes dread, a photograph evokes a decade. Compare its siblings: **provoke** pushes a reaction *out* of someone (anger, a fight), **elicit** extracts a specific response in a transaction (an answer, a confession), and **evoke** simply summons something that was sleeping. It is also the standard verb of criticism — reviewers say a novel or a painting *evokes* something, meaning it succeeds at conjuring a mood without stating it.

⚠️ **evoke ≠ invoke.**
- **evoke** = call a feeling *up*, often unintentionally — *the tune evoked her grandmother*
- **invoke** = deliberately call *on* an authority, rule or power — *he invoked the Fifth Amendment*

You evoke nostalgia; you invoke a clause.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Evoke** rhymes with **awoke** — it wakes something that was already asleep in you.

That rhyme carries the nuance: the feeling was yours all along, and the smell, song or line merely woke it.

### In sentences
1. The memorial's bare stone **evokes** absence more powerfully than any statue could.
2. One bar of that song and boom — instantly **evokes** every school bus ride I ever took.

### In the wild
Land Rover named a car the **Evoque** — a deliberate respelling of *evoke*, because the whole pitch was that the thing should stir a feeling rather than list its specs.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ē-** (*out*) + **vocāre** (*to call*), from **vōx**, *voice* — literally "to call out."

That root is a GRE goldmine: **invoke** (call on), **revoke** (call back), **provoke** (call forth, aggressively), **convoke** (call together), plus **vocation** (a calling), **advocate**, **vocal**, **equivocate**.

# **fawn** *(v. (usu. *fawn over / on*))* — **FAWN**

*One syllable, rhymes with *dawn*.*

### Means
To flatter and fuss over someone in a **cringing, servile way** — praise, attention and physical eagerness all aimed at a person with power over you.

The word carries the observer's contempt built in. **Flatter** describes what you say; **fawn** describes the whole grovelling posture — hovering, agreeing, laughing too hard. Nobody ever says it of themselves: you fawn *over* a boss, a celebrity, a rich uncle, and the person watching finds it slightly disgusting. Praise is neutral, flattery is calculated, fawning is undignified.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fawn** is **fond** with the volume broken.

Same warm sound, but the affection is pitched loud enough that everyone can see who outranks whom.

### In sentences
1. The courtiers **fawned** on the new favourite, discovering in him virtues they had failed to notice a week earlier.
2. Everyone at the table went quiet and started **fawning** the second the investor walked in.

### In the wild
The trauma-response list that's everywhere online — **fight, flight, freeze, fawn**. "Fawn" is the fourth one: you survive the threat by appeasing it. That's the word exactly.

### Where it comes from
Old English **fagnian** = *"to rejoice, welcome gladly"* — used above all of a **dog wagging its tail at its master**. That image is the whole word: delight performed at the feet of someone above you. Same root as **fain** (*"gladly"* — *I would fain go*).

🚫 **Not the baby deer.** That *fawn* came a different way entirely, from Latin **fetus** ("offspring") via French *faon*. Two unrelated words that collided in spelling.

# **fret** *(v. / n.)* — **FRET**

### Means
**Verb:** to worry in a small, continuous, unproductive way — and, in its physical sense, to **wear something away by constant rubbing** (a rope frets against rock).
**Noun:** a state of low-grade agitation ("in a fret about the exam").

Scale matters: you don't *fret* over a car crash, you fret over whether you locked the car. It's chronic, petty, and visibly fidgety rather than sudden or profound — and because it's mild, telling someone "don't fret" is half comfort, half dismissal. The two senses are the same idea: something small, repeated, quietly eroding what it touches.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fret rhymes with sweat** — not a scream, just a small, constant damp anxiety that wears you down.

Both senses are in the rhyme: low-level and repetitive, and the wearing-down is literal in the "rubbed away" meaning.

### In sentences
1. He spent the week **fretting** over a single line in the contract, long after the lawyers had stopped caring.
2. Mum, stop **fretting** — the flight lands at eight, it's fine.

### In the wild
Macbeth's most quoted speech: life is "a poor player, that **struts and frets** his hour upon the stage" — all that fussing and agitation, signifying nothing.

### Where it comes from
Old English **fretan** = *"to devour, eat up"* — the same root as German **fressen**, the verb used for animals gorging rather than people dining.

That's the whole word in one image: worry **eats at** you, and rust or rope **eats away** at what it rubs.

# **glib** *(adj.)* — **GLIB**

### Means
Speaking (or spoken) with easy, ready fluency — but a fluency that betrays **shallowness or insincerity**. A glib answer comes out too fast, too polished, and clearly cost the speaker no thought.

The word is never a compliment. Fluency itself is admired; *glib* is what you call fluency when it has replaced substance — the salesman, the politician with a soundbite for every hard question, the friend who says "everything happens for a reason" at a funeral. Calling someone glib accuses them of trading depth for smoothness.

### Trick to lock it in
> **glib** rhymes with **fib** and with **ad-lib** — talk that's made up on the spot and slides out too easily.

Both rhymes carry the accusation: no preparation behind it, and probably not true.

### In sentences
1. His **glib** assurances about the safety record collapsed the moment anyone asked for figures.
2. Don't give me the **glib** version — I actually want to know what went wrong.

### In the wild
Tom Cruise on the *Today* show in 2005, jabbing a finger at the host over psychiatry: **"Matt, Matt, Matt — you're glib."** The clip has been replayed and parodied for twenty years, and it's exactly the right use: *you're answering too easily about something you haven't thought about.*

### Where it comes from
From Low German/Dutch **glibberig** = *"slippery."* The original English sense was literally smooth and slick; it slid straight onto speech. Keep the physical image and the word defines itself — glib words are **slippery**: they go down easily and you can't get a grip on them.

# **headstrong** *(adj.)* — **HED-strong**

### Means
Determined to have your own way; bent on your own course and deaf to advice, warning, or authority.

Compare the neighbours: **obstinate** and **stubborn** are about *refusing to move* — you dig in. **Headstrong** is the opposite kind of failure: plenty of movement, no steering. The headstrong person charges ahead on their own judgement while everyone shouts from behind. It's most often aimed at the young and spirited — a headstrong daughter, a headstrong young officer — and it carries a flicker of grudging admiration that *pigheaded* never does.

⚠️ **headstrong ≠ headlong.**
- **headstrong** = *self-willed* — you won't be told
- **headlong** = *reckless haste* — you rush in without looking

A headstrong man ignores the advice; a headlong one never waits to hear it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Not a wall — a runaway horse.** A stubborn horse won't move; a **headstrong** one won't be *steered*.

The word is about ungovernable forward motion, not immovability — which is why the natural verbs around it are *rein in*, *curb*, and *break*.

### In sentences
1. Against the counsel of every adviser in the room, the headstrong young king marched his army north before the thaw.
2. She's headstrong — tell her not to do something and that's basically a dare.

### In the wild
Shakespeare, *The Taming of the Shrew* — Petruchio on Katherina: *"thus I'll curb her mad and headstrong humour."* Note the verb he reaches for: **curb**, which is literally a strap on a horse's bridle.

# **intermittent** *(adj.)* — **in-ter-MIT-uhnt**

### Means
Stopping and starting again, over and over, with gaps in between — never sustained for long.

The gaps are what the word is really about. **Continuous** rain falls all day; **intermittent** rain stops, teases you outside, then starts again. It also carries no promise of regularity: *periodic* means you can set your watch by it, *intermittent* just means it keeps coming back at its own convenience. Faintly exasperating in practice — intermittent power, intermittent signal, intermittent effort — because the thing is neither reliably there nor decently gone.

### Trick to lock it in
> There's an **intermission** sitting inside it — same root, same idea: the show stops, then resumes.

An intermission is a gap with more to come, which is exactly what makes something intermittent rather than simply over.

### In sentences
1. The patient reported **intermittent** chest pain over several months, present for an hour or two and then absent for days.
2. The wifi's been **intermittent** all morning — two minutes of video call, then nothing.

### In the wild
**Intermittent fasting** — eat, stop, eat again. The whole diet is named after the gaps.

### Where it comes from
Latin **intermittere** = *inter-* ("between") + *mittere* ("to send, to let go") — literally *to let go in between*, i.e. to leave off for a while.

*Mittere* is one of the great GRE roots: **transmit** (send across), **emit** (send out), **submit** (send under), **remit**, **omit**, **mission**, **missile**, **intermission**.

### Also sits in
- **Meaning cluster** · [Stopping and starting](groups.md#meaning__stopping-and-starting) — with *sporadic, fitful, erratic, desultory*

# **ire** *(n.)* — **EYE-er**

*One syllable, rhyming with fire — not "EER", not "EE-ray".*

### Means
Anger — specifically **anger provoked in someone by an offence**.

*Rage* is loud and out of control; *wrath* is grand and punitive, the anger of gods and kings; **ire** is cooler and more formal, the word a newspaper uses. It is almost never anger you're currently feeling — it's anger *someone else* directs at the person who caused it. Notice it lives in a handful of set phrases: *drew the ire of*, *raised the ire of*, *sparked the ire of*. Short and headline-friendly, which is exactly why journalists reach for it.

### Trick to lock it in
> You don't *have* ire — you **draw** it, the way a bad decision draws a crowd.

The verbs that cling to this word (*drew, raised, sparked*) tell you ire is always anger you provoked in somebody else, never a mood you're sitting in.

### In sentences
1. The decision to fell the old plane trees drew the **ire** of a parish that had otherwise agreed on nothing for a decade.
2. One badly worded email and he'd earned the **ire** of the entire group chat.

### In the wild
**Dies Irae** — "Day of *Wrath*", the medieval funeral chant. Its four-note melody is the standard shorthand for doom in film scores: the opening of *The Shining*, *It's a Wonderful Life*, *Star Wars*, *The Lion King*. If you've heard dread in a soundtrack, you've heard *ire*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ira** = *anger*. Same root gives **irate** (angry right now) and **irascible** (quick to get angry, a permanent temperament) — a useful trio, since the GRE likes the distinction between a state and a disposition.

# **languid** *(adj.)* — **LANG-gwid**

### Means
Slow, drooping, without energy — either from real weakness (illness, heat, exhaustion) or from sheer unhurried ease.

The word is an aesthetic one. *Lethargic* is a symptom, *sluggish* is a complaint, *lazy* is an accusation — **languid** is the only one of the four that can be a compliment. A languid gesture, a languid summer afternoon, a languid gaze: slow enough to be stylish. Even in its weaker sense it stays poetic rather than clinical — you'd write "languid after the fever," not put it in a chart. The exception is business prose, where **languid trading** just means limp and disappointing.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Lang**uid moves at the pace of a **lang**uage spoken in a drawl — long, slow, and rather attractive.

The buried "lang" gives you the *long, drawn-out* pace, and the drawl gives you the tone: unhurried, not diseased.

### In sentences
1. The heat pressed down until the whole household turned **languid**, moving between shuttered rooms with the slowness of sleepwalkers.
2. Three days off and I've gone completely **languid** — I got up at noon and considered that an achievement.

### Where it comes from
Latin **languēre** = *"to be faint, to droop."* It hands you a tight family worth owning: **languid** (slow and drooping), **languor** (the pleasant heavy state itself), and **languish** (to droop *over time* — to waste away, or sit neglected: *the bill languished in committee*).

# **lull** *(n. / v.)* — **LUL**

### Means
**Noun:** a temporary pause in noise, activity, or violence — a quiet stretch inside something that hasn't actually stopped.
**Verb:** to soothe or quieten, especially into sleep or trust.

Both senses smuggle in the same idea: *this won't last, and you shouldn't trust it.* A lull isn't peace, it's an interval — the fighting resumes, the storm arrives, the shop gets busy again. And the verb is rarely innocent at GRE level: things get **lulled into complacency**, **lulled into a false sense of security**. Where *calm* and *soothe* are simply pleasant, *lull* lowers your guard — often to your cost.

⚠️ **lull ≠ loll.** *Lull* (LUL) = a quiet spell, or to soothe. *Loll* (LOL) = to lounge, droop, or hang slackly — *he lolled on the sofa; the dog's tongue lolled out.*

### Trick to lock it in
> **A lull is a lullaby — it works by putting you to sleep.**

That's the catch built into the word: the quiet is doing something to you, and you wake up to find the noise never actually ended.

### In sentences
1. Three quiet weeks **lulled** the garrison into complacency, and the assault came on the fourth.
2. There's usually a **lull** around three o'clock — grab your coffee then.

### In the wild
Standard newsroom language: *\"a **lull** in the fighting\"* — reporters reach for it precisely because it means the shooting has paused, not stopped.

# **mettlesome** *(adj.)* — **MET-uhl-suhm**

### Means
Full of **mettle** — spirited, plucky, brave and eager, not easily daunted.

It's courage plus *liveliness*. A stoic is brave; a mettlesome person is brave and straining at the leash. The word is admiring but faintly amused — the classic subject is a mettlesome horse, all fire and hard to hold, and applied to a person it carries that same flavour of spirit that might be a little too much for the bridle. Slightly old-fashioned and literary; you'd meet it in a novel or a toast, rarely in an email.

⚠️ **mettlesome ≠ meddlesome.**
- **mettlesome** = full of fighting spirit (a compliment)
- **meddlesome** = always interfering in other people's business (an insult)

One letter apart, opposite verdicts.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Mettle**some is made of **metal** — steel in the spine; **meddle**some just gets in the **middle**.

The *tt* spelling is literally the metal one, so hear "tempered steel" for spirit and "middle" for the busybody.

### In sentences
1. The regiment's mettlesome young lieutenants were forever volunteering for the reconnaissance no one else wanted.
2. She's a mettlesome kid — told the coach to his face she should be starting, and she wasn't wrong.

### In the wild
You already know the noun: **"test your mettle"**, **"prove his mettle"** — sports commentary and job interviews run on it. *Mettlesome* is just the adjective for someone who has plenty to test.

### Where it comes from
**Mettle *is* metal** — the same word. English used *metal* figuratively for the stuff a person was made of (like tempered steel), and by around 1700 the two spellings split: *metal* for the substance, *mettle* for the temperament.

So "what is he made of?" is not a metaphor bolted on afterwards — it's the literal history of the word.

# **mollify** *(v.)* — **MOL-uh-fye**

### Means
To **soften someone's anger** — to calm, appease, pacify a person who is upset.

The object is almost always a person or their feelings (*mollify the critics, mollify her fears*), never a physical thing. It implies a grievance already exists and you are making a gesture — an apology, a concession, a soothing word — to take the heat out of it. Unlike *placate*, which can hint at buying someone off, *mollify* is neutral about the tactic and focused on the result: temperature down. It also carries a whiff of *partial* success; things are written as "failed to mollify" about as often as they're written the other way.

⚠️ **mollify ≠ mortify.** Both are Latin *-ify* verbs about someone's feelings, and they pull opposite ways: **mollify** = *soothe* them; **mortify** = *humiliate* them.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Mollify = "mellow-fy"** — you make the angry person mellow again.

The sound is nearly *mellow*, and that's exactly the job: not fixing the problem, just lowering the temperature of the person.

### In sentences
1. The chairman's carefully worded statement did little to **mollify** shareholders, who had come for a resignation, not a paragraph.
2. I brought her coffee to **mollify** her after showing up forty minutes late.

### In the wild
It's a newsroom staple: whenever a company apologises and the market shrugs, the report reads *"the statement failed to mollify investors."* Watch for it in any coverage of a corporate scandal.

### Where it comes from
Latin **mollis** = *"soft"* + *-ficare* (to make). To mollify is literally to **make soft**.

Same root: **emollient** — the moisturiser that softens skin — and **mollusc**, the soft-bodied creature in the hard shell.

# **neutralize** *(v.)* — **NOO-truh-lyze**

*British spelling: neutralise.*

### Means
To make something **ineffective or harmless** — to cancel out its force. In chemistry, to bring an acid or base to neutral; in politics or sport, to blunt an opponent's advantage; in military and intelligence-speak, to kill.

The word is cold and clinical. *Destroy* names the violence; *neutralize* hides it behind a chemistry-lab tone — the threat is simply **balanced out of existence**. That flatness is why bureaucracies love it: a general "neutralizes" a target, a defender "neutralizes" a striker, an antacid "neutralizes" acid, and the same calm verb covers all three. Note also that it often means *cancelled*, not *removed* — a neutralized force is still there, just made to count for nothing.

### Trick to lock it in
> Put it in **neutral** — the engine still runs, the car goes nowhere.

Neutralizing doesn't smash the thing, it takes it out of gear so its power stops translating into effect.

### In sentences
1. A single defensive midfielder **neutralized** their playmaker for ninety minutes; he touched the ball often and changed nothing.
2. Take one of these before dinner — it'll **neutralize** the acid and you'll sleep fine.

### In the wild
Every antacid ad ever made: "**neutralizes** excess stomach acid." That's the literal chemistry sense doing all the metaphorical work — acid meets base, and the burn is cancelled.

### Where it comes from
Latin **neuter** = *"neither"* (*ne* + *uter*, "not one or the other"). A neutral party is on **neither** side; to neutralize is to force something into that position of counting for neither side. Same root: **neuter, neutral, neutrality, neutron** (the particle with neither charge).

# **nonplussed** *(adj.)* — **non-PLUST**

### Means
So surprised, puzzled or bewildered that you have **no idea how to react** — speechless, blank, stalled.

It isn't ordinary confusion (not understanding something) and it isn't shock (an emotional blow). It's the specific *stall*: the situation is so unexpected that your response system produces nothing. Someone insults you at a funeral; someone answers a routine question with a personal confession — you stand there with nothing to say. Neutral about the person: being nonplussed says the world was strange, not that you were slow.

⚠️ **nonplussed ≠ unfazed.** The *non-* fools everyone into reading it as "not bothered, cool as ice" — the exact opposite. (American speech has drifted toward that misuse; the GRE tests the real sense.) **Nonplussed = at a total loss.**

### Trick to lock it in
> **Non plus** — nothing more. Your brain has **nothing further to add**.

The word is literally *no more* — the point where you've run out of responses, which is blankness rather than calm.

### In sentences
1. Asked to justify the missing funds in front of the entire board, the treasurer was **nonplussed**, and the silence went on far too long.
2. She proposed on the second date — I was completely **nonplussed**, just standing there holding my coffee.

### Where it comes from
Latin **non plus** = *"not more."* It named the state where nothing further can be said or done — you've hit the wall of your own responses.

Same *plus* runs through **ne plus ultra** (*"nothing beyond"* — the absolute peak), **plural**, **surplus**.

# **precipitous** *(adj.)* — **pri-SIP-uh-tuhs**

### Means
**1.** Dangerously **steep** — a cliff, a staircase, a mountain road.
**2.** Of a fall or decline: **sudden, sharp and alarming** — a *precipitous* drop in sales.

The word always carries a whiff of danger. A hill can be *steep* and pleasant; call it **precipitous** and you've said it could kill you. Applied to numbers, it's the graph line falling off a cliff — not a dip, not a slide, a plunge. Journalists and analysts reach for it precisely because it sounds alarming, so it does editorial work that "sharp" doesn't.

⚠️ **precipitous ≠ precipitate** *(adj., pri-SIP-uh-tuht)*.
- **precipitous** = *steep* — describes a **drop** or a **thing**
- **precipitate** = *rash, headlong, done too soon* — describes an **act** or a **decision**

A precipitous cliff; a precipitate withdrawal.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Precipit-ous** hides **precipice** — the edge you fall off.

If you can see the precipice in the word, you'll always land on *steep drop* rather than *hasty*.

### In sentences
1. The path narrowed and turned **precipitous**, falling away on the left into three hundred feet of nothing.
2. One bad quarter and the stock took a **precipitous** dive — down forty percent before lunch.

### In the wild
It's the default word of financial news: every "shares fell **precipitously**" headline is the same picture — the chart going over the edge.

### Where it comes from
Latin **praeceps, praecipit-** = *"headlong,"* literally **prae** (*forward*) + **caput** (*head*) — going head-first off something.

That gives you **precipice** (the drop), **precipitate** (to hurl headlong → to rush), and **precipitation** (water falling headlong out of the sky).

And **caput** = *head* is a GRE workhorse: **capital**, **captain**, **decapitate**, **per capita**.

# **pretentious** *(adj.)* — **pri-TEN-shuhs**

### Means
Claiming — through manner, vocabulary, or taste — an importance, refinement, or depth you don't actually possess.

The word attacks the **performance**, not the person's actual status. A genuinely learned professor can't be pretentious for using long words; the undergraduate imitating him can. Compare the neighbours: **pompous** is inflated self-importance (a manner), **ostentatious** is vulgar display of money (a yacht), **pretentious** is a bid for *cultural* credit — the unread Proust on the shelf, the wine notes, the film that thinks it's saying something. It is never a compliment and never neutral; calling something pretentious is a verdict that the substance doesn't back the show.

**pretentious ≠ portentous.** *Portentous* primarily means **ominous, foreboding** — a portent of disaster. (It has a secondary "pompously solemn" sense, which is exactly why the pair is a trap.) Ominous sky = portentous. Affected art student = pretentious.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Preten**tious = **preten**ding, with an extra syllable for show.

The word literally contains *pretend*, and the padding on the end is the word doing the very thing it describes.

### In sentences
1. The novel's ambitions collapse into **pretentious** obscurity — three hundred pages of portent about nothing in particular.
2. Twelve quid for a coffee and they call the guy a "barista artisan"? Bit **pretentious**.

### In the wild
It's the standard one-word kill in reviews — of films, restaurants, and wine lists. A critic who writes *pretentious* has said the thing is trying to be profound and isn't, and no further argument is offered.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae-** (*before*) + **tendere** (*to stretch*) — to stretch something out in front of you as a claim. Hence **pretend**, **pretext**, **pretension**.

*Tendere* is a workhorse root: **extend** (stretch out), **distend** (stretch apart), **contend** (stretch against), **intend** (stretch toward), **portend** (stretch forward as a sign), **tendon** (the stretchy bit).

# **profound** *(adj.)* — **pruh-FOWND**

*The noun is profundity — pruh-FUN-di-tee, not "pro-FOUND-ity".*

### Means
Two senses, one idea. **(1)** Showing or requiring great depth of thought or feeling — *a profound remark*. **(2)** Complete, thoroughgoing, reaching all the way down — *profound silence, profound ignorance, profound change*.

Depth is the whole point, and it is what separates *profound* from its neighbours. *Intense* is about force, *extreme* is about distance from the middle, *profound* is about how far down something goes. So praise that calls an idea profound says it reached bedrock, not that it was clever or loud — which is why the word turns sour so easily in the sceptic's mouth (*pseudo-profound*, *profound-sounding*). And in sense (2) it carries no admiration at all: **profound ignorance** is ignorance that goes all the way down.

### Trick to lock it in
> **pro-FOUND** — it goes down as far as the ***found**ation*.

Same buried root as *foundation*: profound things sit at the bottom, whether that's a bottomless insight or bottomless silence.

### In sentences
1. Her final essay makes a **profound** claim about grief: that it is not an emotion we pass through but a room we move into.
2. He nodded like it was **profound**, but honestly it was just a fortune cookie.

### In the wild
Medicine uses it as a grading term, not a compliment: hearing loss runs mild → moderate → severe → **profound**, where *profound* means the bottom of the scale — essentially total.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pro-** (*forward, toward*) + **fundus** (*bottom*) — literally *"toward the bottom."*

**fundus** pays off elsewhere: **foundation** and **fundamental** (what sits at the bottom), **fund** (the bottom stock of money), and **founder** — when a ship founders, it goes to the bottom. Keep that separate from **flounder**, to thrash about clumsily.

# **propagate** *(v.)* — **PROP-uh-gayt**

*The noun shifts the stress: propagation = prop-uh-GAY-shuhn.*

### Means
To **multiply and pass onward** — to breed new plants from cuttings, to transmit a wave or signal through a medium, or to spread a belief, rumour, or error so that it keeps reproducing.

The difference from *spread* or *disseminate* is the chain. **Disseminate** scatters from one source outward, one-to-many, and stops. **Propagate** means each copy becomes a new source: the plant makes plants, the error in row 3 corrupts every row after it. In botany, physics and computing it's coldly technical. Aimed at ideas, it turns faintly hostile — you *hold* a view, but you *propagate* a myth, and the verb implies you know better and are multiplying it anyway.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Propaganda** is hiding inside **propagate** — and propaganda isn't news, it's news *manufactured to breed*.

The buried word gives you both halves at once: relentless multiplication, plus the whiff of blame when the thing being multiplied is a belief.

### In sentences
1. Sound **propagates** roughly four times faster through water than through air.
2. One bad cell reference **propagated** through the whole spreadsheet and blew up the quarterly numbers.

### In the wild
Anyone who has changed a domain name has sat waiting for **DNS to propagate** — the new address copying itself server to server across the internet until every corner of the network has it. Same idea in hardware as **propagation delay**: how long a signal takes to work its way through a circuit.

### Where it comes from
Latin **propagare** = *"to set slips"* — gardening, literally: pin a cutting into the soil and let it become a whole new plant. From **pro-** (*forward*) + the root **pag-/pac-** (*to fasten, fix*), which also gives **pact** and **compact** — things fastened down.

That's the whole word. You don't broadcast a cutting; you plant it, and it grows into something that can be cut from in turn.

# **recourse** *(n.)* — **ri-KORS**

*Many Americans stress the first syllable: REE-kors. Both are fine.*

### Means
The help, remedy, or option you turn to when you're in trouble — and, by extension, the act of turning to it. Lives in fixed phrases: **have recourse to**, **without recourse to**, **legal recourse**, **no recourse**.

Trouble is built into the word. You never have recourse to a holiday; you have recourse to the courts, to violence, to a loan shark — something you'd rather not have needed. It's formal and faintly grim, the vocabulary of someone who has run out of better options. And the phrase that carries the most weight is the negative one: **no recourse** means the door is shut, nobody owes you a remedy.

**recourse ≠ resource.** A *resource* is a supply you already hold (money, staff, time); *recourse* is a remedy you run to when your own resources have failed.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Re-course**: your first course of action failed, so you run *back* for a remedy.

The *course* sitting inside the word is the running you do after Plan A collapses — which is why recourse always smells of last resort.

### In sentences
1. Stripped of any legal **recourse**, the tenants had nothing left but the press.
2. The Wi-Fi died, so we had **recourse** to actual paper maps like it was 1998.

### In the wild
A **non-recourse loan** — standard in mortgages and project finance — means that if you default, the lender can seize the property and nothing else. No recourse to your salary, your savings, or you.

### Where it comes from
Latin **re-** (*back*) + **currere** (*to run*) → *recurrere*, "to run back."

That root runs through half the GRE list: **occur, recur, concur, incur, current, cursory, precursor, excursion, discourse, curriculum**. Anything with *curr/curs* is something running.

# **refute** *(v.)* — **ri-FYOOT**

*Noun form shifts the stress: refutation = ref-yuu-TAY-shun.*

### Means
To **prove a claim false** — to defeat it with evidence, logic, or demonstration.

The word claims **success**, not effort. Denying is saying "that's not true"; refuting is *showing* it isn't, and winning. So it's a strong word to hand yourself: if you say you refuted someone, you're asserting the argument is over. Careful writers reserve it for the finished job; loose speakers use it as a fancy synonym for "deny," and the GRE will test the strict sense.

⚠️ **refute ≠ rebut.**
- **rebut** = *argue back* against a claim (attempt)
- **refute** = *disprove* it (result)

You can rebut badly. You cannot refute badly — if it didn't land, it wasn't a refutation.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Rebut** is a **butt** — a shove back. **Refute** rhymes with **rout** *(near enough)* — the claim is destroyed, not just pushed.

One is a push, the other is a defeat — refute always means you won.

### In sentences
1. A single dated photograph **refuted** the entire account he had given under oath.
2. Show me the receipts and I'll shut up — until then you haven't **refuted** anything, you've just disagreed loudly.

### In the wild
Corporate press releases love "**the company refutes these allegations**" — almost always wrong. They've denied them; refuting would take evidence. Spotting that misuse in the news is the fastest way to own the strict meaning.

### Where it comes from
Latin **refūtāre** = *"to beat back, drive off"* — **re-** (back) + a root meaning *to strike*. A refutation literally beats the claim back down.

Same root gives **confute** (to prove decisively wrong) and, distantly, **futile** — a blow that accomplishes nothing.

# **regress** *(v. / n.)* — **ri-GRES**

*As a noun, the stress moves to the front: REE-gres.*

### Means
To move **backwards** to an earlier, less developed, usually worse state — to undo progress already made.

The word presumes a ladder. You can only regress along ground you had already gained, which is why it stings: a beginner isn't regressing, a recovered patient is. Compare *relapse*, which is about a condition returning (a disease, an addiction), and *revert*, which is neutral and often deliberate (revert to the old file, the old plan). *Regress* is developmental and quietly damning — it says you had come further than this.

It also lives two technical lives with no judgement attached: in psychology, an adult regressing to childish behaviour under stress; in statistics, *regression* — fitting a line through data.

⚠️ **regress ≠ digress.** Same *-gress* (step), different direction: **re-** = step *back* along your own path; **di-** = step *aside* off the topic. A lecturer who digresses wanders; a lecturer who regresses gets worse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pro**gress and **re**gress are the same road — *pro-* = forward, *re-* = back.

That shared road is the point: regress only means something if there was progress to lose.

### In sentences
1. Deprived of routine and therapy, the patient **regressed** to behaviours the clinic had spent two years unlearning.
2. Three days back at my parents' house and I've **regressed** into a teenager sulking about the wifi.

### In the wild
Every software team runs **regression tests** — the suite whose only job is to catch code that has slipped back into a bug it was already fixed for. That's the word exactly: not a new failure, an old one returning.

### Where it comes from
Latin **regredi** = *re-* ("back") + **gradi** ("to step"), from **gradus**, "a step."

That root pays for itself across the exam: **progress** (step forward), **digress** (step aside), **transgress** (step across the line), **egress** / **ingress** (step out / in), **congress** (stepping together), plus **gradual**, **degree**, and **grade** — all of them steps.

### Also sits in
- **Opposites** · [Rising above vs. falling short](groups.md#antonym__rising-above-vs-falling-short) — with *outstrip, surmount, transcend, prevail, vanquish, falter, languish, wanting*

# **repercussion** *(n.)* — **ree-per-KUSH-uhn**

### Means
An indirect and usually unwelcome **consequence** of an action — one that arrives later, at some distance from what caused it.

A *consequence* can be immediate and direct; a **repercussion** travels. It's the second-order effect: the resignation that triggers a market slide that costs a stranger her job. It also carries a threat when used bluntly — *"there will be repercussions"* means punishment is coming, and everyone in the room knows it. Almost always plural, almost always bad; nobody speaks of the happy repercussions of a pay rise.

### Trick to lock it in
> **re- + percussion** — you hit the drum once, and the sound comes back at you from the far wall.

That delay and that bounce are the whole word: not the blow itself, but the echo of it reaching you later from somewhere else.

### In sentences
1. The collapse of one regional bank had **repercussions** across three continents within a fortnight.
2. Skip another shift and there'll be **repercussions** — HR's already asking about you.

### In the wild
The **percussion** section of any band — drums, cymbals, timpani. Instruments you play by striking. *Repercussion* is that strike bouncing back.

### Where it comes from
Latin **quatere** = *"to shake, to strike."* Add prefixes and you get a whole GRE family: **percussion** (strike through), **concussion** (the brain shaken *together*), **discussion** (shake a thing *apart* to examine it), and **quash** (shake a ruling to pieces).

**Re-percussion** = the striking-back, the rebound.

### Also sits in
- **Meaning cluster** · [What Follows After](groups.md#meaning__what-follows-after) — with *ramification, ensue*
- **Connotation** · [Consequences assumed unwelcome](groups.md#connotation__consequences-assumed-unwelcome) — with *ramification, exacerbate, escalate, compound*

# **replenish** *(v.)* — **ri-PLEN-ish**

### Means
To **fill something back up** after it has been drawn down — stocks, supplies, reserves, energy.

Two things are baked in: there was a **depletion**, and the refilling **restores a previous level** rather than adding something new. You *stock* an empty shelf; you *replenish* one that has sold down. Compare *refill* (plain, physical, one container) and *restore* (returns a state or condition, not a quantity) — *replenish* is the quantity word, and it carries a faint air of supply logistics and stewardship: fleets, larders, aquifers, reserves.

### Trick to lock it in
> **re-PLEN-ish** — *plenty* again.

The stressed syllable is literally *plen-*, the root of *plenty*, and *re-* means back to it — you had plenty, you spent it, you get plenty again.

### In sentences
1. Each spring the meltwater **replenishes** aquifers that the summer will drain again.
2. Grab a sandwich — you need to **replenish** after that ride.

### In the wild
It's the retail industry's own word: shops run on **inventory replenishment**, and the staff walking the aisles at midnight are on a *replenishment* shift.

### Where it comes from
Latin **plēnus** = *"full."* One root, a whole GRE cluster: **plenty**, **plenary** (a full session), **plenitude**, **replete** (stuffed full), **plethora** (too full), and **complete**.

- **Opposites** · [Fertile vs. barren](groups.md#antonym__fertile-vs-barren) — with *fecund, fruitful, burgeon, barren, fallow, desiccate, futile*

# **vigilant** *(adj.)* — **VIJ-uh-luhnt**

### Means
Keeping steady, alert watch for danger, error, or wrongdoing.

The word carries **duty and endurance**, not nerves. *Anxious* watching is jumpy and self-protective; *vigilant* watching is a post you hold — the night nurse, the sentry, the auditor. It's sustained over time and almost always approving: to call someone vigilant is to praise them. Compare **wary** (suspicious of a specific thing) and **observant** (notices detail, danger or not) — vigilant is watchfulness *aimed at threat*, kept up indefinitely.

⚠️ **vigilant ≠ vigilante.**
- **vigilant** *(adj.)* = watchful, and it's a compliment
- **vigilante** *(n., vij-uh-LAN-tee)* = a private citizen who punishes crime without legal authority — usually a criticism

A vigilant neighbourhood calls the police; a vigilante skips that step.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Vigilant** has **vigil** sitting inside it — and nobody keeps a vigil for five minutes.

A vigil is an all-night watch, which is exactly the endurance the word implies: not a startled glance but a shift you don't abandon.

### In sentences
1. Border officials were instructed to remain **vigilant** for forged documentation during the busiest weeks of the season.
2. Be **vigilant** at that crossing — half the drivers there treat the red light as a suggestion.

### In the wild
Every transit system in the world runs the same line: *"Please stay vigilant and report any unattended baggage."* You've heard it on a platform this year.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vigil** = *"awake, watchful"* → **vigilāre**, *"to keep watch."*

One root, a useful family: **vigil** (the all-night watch), **vigilante**, **reveille** (the wake-up bugle, via French *réveiller*), **surveillance** (*sur-* "over" + *veiller* "watch"), and — fittingly — **invigilate**, which is what the person pacing the aisles at your GRE centre is doing.
