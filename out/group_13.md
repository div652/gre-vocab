# **affinity** *(n.)* — **uh-FIN-uh-tee**

### Means
**1.** A natural liking or attraction toward something. **2.** A resemblance or kinship between two things — languages, species, ideas, substances.

The word claims the pull is *built in*, not chosen. You can *like* jazz after deciding to; an **affinity** for jazz says something in you already matched it. That's why it works equally well for chemistry (elements with an affinity for oxygen) and linguistics (the affinity between Sanskrit and Latin) — no feeling required, just an inherent fit.

Watch the preposition: an affinity **for** something is liking; an affinity **with** or **between** things is resemblance.

### Trick to lock it in
> **In**finity has *no* boundary; **af**finity is boundaries that *touch*.

Both hide Latin *finis* (boundary) — one negates it, the other brings two borders up against each other, which is exactly what a natural kinship is.

### In sentences
1. Nineteenth-century scholars detected a deep **affinity** between Sanskrit and Latin long before anyone could explain how it had arisen.
2. I've got zero **affinity** for spreadsheets — numbers just slide straight off me.

### In the wild
Corporate **affinity groups** — employee networks built around a shared background or interest — and **affinity credit cards**, co-branded with a team or charity you already feel attached to. Both use the word in its exact sense: a pre-existing pull, which the company merely organises.

### Where it comes from
Latin **affinis** = *"bordering on"* — literally *ad-* (to) + *finis* (boundary, end). In Roman law it meant **related by marriage**: your family's borders now touch mine.

*Finis* is a workhorse root: **finite, infinite, define, confine, final, finish, finance** (settling up = ending it).

# **altruistic** *(adj.)* — **al-troo-IS-tik**

*The noun shifts the stress forward: altruism = AL-troo-iz-uhm.*

### Means
Acting for the good of others **at a cost to yourself**, with no expectation of return.

*Generous* and *kind* describe what you did; **altruistic** describes **why** — it's a claim about motive, and an absolute one: zero self-interest. That absoluteness is what makes it a favourite of philosophers, economists and biologists (in evolutionary biology, altruism is the puzzle of why an animal would ever pay a cost for another's benefit). It's also why the word so often arrives pre-loaded with doubt: "purely altruistic motives" is a phrase people usually write in order to question it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Alt**ruistic — hit **alt** and you get the *other* option. Altruism is choosing the other person's option, and paying for it.

The buried **alt-** is the same *alter* = "other" you already know from *alternative*, and the cost to yourself is what separates altruistic from merely nice.

### In sentences
1. Her decision to donate the kidney to a stranger was **altruistic** in the strictest sense: she declined to learn the recipient's name.
2. He'll tell you he took the night shift for altruistic reasons, but he also really wanted the parking spot.

### In the wild
**Effective Altruism** — the movement that told donors to maximise lives saved per dollar, then became a byword for good intentions gone strange after the FTX collapse. The word's built-in tension between selflessness and suspicion, playing out in public.

### Where it comes from
Latin **alter** = *"the other."* French philosopher Auguste Comte coined **altruisme** in the 1830s as the exact opposite of *egoism* — living for the other rather than the self.

That root is everywhere once you see it: **alter** (make other), **alternative** (the other choice), **alter ego** (your other self), **altercation** (a quarrel — you and the *other* guy).

# **baroque** *(adj. / n.)* — **buh-ROHK**

*British speakers often say buh-ROK, rhyming with "rock."*

### Means
**Capital B:** the European style of art, architecture and music from roughly 1600–1750 — curling, gilded, dramatic, packed with detail.
**Lowercase:** so elaborately ornamented that the ornament has taken over.

Used of the period, it's a neutral technical label. Used of anything else — prose, software, a plot, an excuse — it's a jab: the thing is *fussily* complex, decorated past the point of usefulness. Note the flavour: baroque complexity is decorative, almost showy, not merely difficult. A tax code is *byzantine* (tangled, bureaucratic); a sentence with six subordinate clauses and three metaphors is *baroque*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **baroque = "broke"** — the clean plain line, *broken up* with curlicues until it's buried.

The word sounds like *broke*, and that's the charge against it: simplicity broken into ornament for ornament's sake.

### In sentences
1. The palace's **baroque** façade — cherubs, scrolls, gold leaf on every surface — left no square inch at rest.
2. His excuse got so **baroque** that by the third subplot I stopped believing any of it.

### In the wild
Vivaldi's *Four Seasons* and Bach's organ works **are** Baroque music — and you can hear the definition in them: trills, runs and curlicues stacked on top of a simple tune.

### Where it comes from
Portuguese **barroco** = an *irregular, misshapen pearl* — valuable, but lumpy and asymmetrical rather than clean. Jewellers' slang became art critics' insult, then the name of a whole era. The pearl is the meaning in miniature: ornate, showy, not smooth.

# **byzantine** *(adj.)* — **BIZ-uhn-teen**

*Also biz-AN-tyne or BIZ-uhn-tyne — all three are standard. Capital B for the empire, small b for the figurative sense.*

### Means
Excessively complicated and convoluted — a system of rules, procedures or plots so tangled that no outsider can follow it.

*Complex* is neutral and can be admiring; **byzantine** is always a complaint. It carries two charges at once: the thing is impossibly intricate, **and** the intricacy shields insiders — hidden channels, backroom deals, rules nobody will explain. A byzantine tax code isn't just hard; it's suspiciously hard.

### Trick to lock it in
> **BIZ**antine — a *biz* so knotted with rules and back channels that only insiders know how it really works.

The "biz" at the front pins both halves of the word: bureaucratic tangle plus insider scheming.

### In sentences
1. The committee's **byzantine** approval process required eleven signatures, three of them from officials whose roles no one could define.
2. Getting a refund from them is **byzantine** — I've been transferred four times and I still don't know who's in charge.

### In the wild
Computer science has the **Byzantine Generals Problem** — and its offspring, *Byzantine fault tolerance*, the property blockchains brag about. The name was chosen precisely because it means generals sending contradictory, possibly treacherous messages that no one can untangle.

### Where it comes from
From **Byzantium** — the Greek city Constantine rebuilt as Constantinople, capital of the Eastern Roman Empire (now Istanbul). Its court was legendary in the West for layered officialdom, elaborate ceremony and palace intrigue, so by the 1800s the empire's name became the adjective for that whole condition.

This is one of the rare words where the history *is* the definition: the Byzantine bureaucracy was byzantine.

# **compromise** *(n. / v.)* — **KOM-pruh-myze**

### Means
**Sense 1 (the everyday one):** a settlement where each side gives something up — or to reach one.
**Sense 2 (the one the GRE tests):** to **damage, weaken, or expose to danger** — a reputation, an immune system, a password, a principle.

The two senses split cleanly by grammar. The **noun** is respectable: *a compromise* is what reasonable adults arrive at. The **participle** is never respectable: something **compromised** is tainted, breached, no longer trustworthy. And *to compromise oneself* means to have surrendered a principle in a way you can't take back — the word carries the faint smell of a deal made in private.

⚠️ **compromise ≠ comprise.**
- **compromise** = *settle by concession*, or *endanger*
- **comprise** = *consist of* (the committee comprises nine members)

### Trick to lock it in
> **Hiding inside it is *promise* — a co-promise. Both sides promise away a piece; when *you* promise away a piece of yourself, you're compromised.**

The same act — giving part of something up — is a virtue between two parties and a defect within one.

### In sentences
1. Chemotherapy **compromises** the immune system, leaving patients vulnerable to infections a healthy body would shrug off.
2. We **compromised**: he got the aisle seat, I got the armrest.

### In the wild
Every breach notification you've ever received — *\"your credentials may have been compromised\"* — is sense 2 doing its job: not *negotiated*, but *broken into and no longer safe*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **com-** (*together*) + **promittere** (*to promise*) → a *compromissum*, the mutual promise two disputing parties made to accept an arbiter's ruling.

**promittere** = *pro-* (forth) + *mittere* (to send), and *mittere* is a workhorse root: **mission, transmit, permit, dismiss, emit, remit, submit**.

# **conciliatory** *(adj.)* — **kun-SIL-ee-uh-tor-ee**

### Means
Intended to **soothe someone's anger and win them back** — to end hostility rather than win the argument.

It describes the *manner*, not the surrender. A conciliatory remark concedes nothing; it just lowers the temperature. Note the distance from its neighbours: **apologetic** admits fault, **obsequious** grovels, **placatory** hints the other side is being humoured — **conciliatory** stays dignified, the register of diplomats, negotiators and HR. Its faint downside is only ever the suspicion of weakness: someone *too* conciliatory is caving.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Conciliatory** is **re*concil*e** with the *re-* stripped off — the opening move, not the finished peace.

A conciliatory gesture is what you offer *before* reconciliation, which is why it can be made and still refused.

### In sentences
1. After a week of trading insults, the minister struck an unexpectedly **conciliatory** note, conceding that both sides had grievances worth hearing.
2. He didn't apologise exactly, but he brought coffee and used his most **conciliatory** voice, which was close enough.

### In the wild
Political journalism runs on one collocation: **"struck a conciliatory tone."** Any time a leader softens after a row, that is the sentence the news writes.

### Where it comes from
Latin **concilium** = *"an assembly, a calling-together"* (*com-* "together" + *calāre* "to call"). To conciliate is literally to **call the two sides back into the same room**.

Same root: **council** (the assembly itself) and **reconcile** (call them together *again*).

# **countenance** *(n. / v.)* — **KOWN-tuh-nuhns**

*Four syllables run together — KOWN-tn-uhns in fast speech.*

### Means
**Noun:** a person's **face**, especially as it shows feeling — the expression, the look. ("a grave countenance")
**Verb:** to **tolerate, permit, or give approval to** something.

The noun is literary and slightly old-fashioned; *face* is the plain word, *countenance* is the one that implies you can read something off it. The verb is where the GRE lives, and it has a habit: it appears overwhelmingly in the **negative** — *would not countenance*, *refused to countenance*, *cannot countenance*. To countenance something isn't to like it; it's to let it stand without objecting. So the person who "will not countenance dissent" is drawing a line, not stating a preference.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Can you face it?** If you can't, you *will not countenance* it.

The noun (face) and the verb (allow) are the same act — keeping a straight face at something instead of refusing to look at it.

### In sentences
1. The headmaster would not **countenance** the slightest breach of the rules, and his **countenance** on the morning of the inquiry made that plain before he spoke.
2. I don't care how late it is — I'm not countenancing another round of "just one more episode."

### In the wild
Blake's hymn **"Jerusalem"** — *"And did the Countenance Divine / Shine forth upon our clouded hills?"* — the noun sense, a face shining down.

### Where it comes from
Latin **continēre** = *"to hold together"* → **continentia**, *"the way you hold yourself, self-control."* Your countenance is your face **held in place**.

Same root gives **contain**, **container**, **continent** (land held together), and **continence** (holding yourself in check).

# **covert** *(adj. (also n.))* — **KOH-vert**

*Older, still-correct pronunciation is KUV-ert, rhyming with *cover* — KOH-vert won out by analogy with *overt*.*

### Means
Concealed, disguised, not openly acknowledged.

Two flavours. In the institutional sense, **covert** means deliberately hidden by design — a covert operation is planned, funded and deniable, and the whole point is that the sponsor's hand never shows. In the everyday sense, it means *unstated rather than invisible*: covert hostility, a covert glance, covert racism — the thing is perfectly perceptible, it just never gets said out loud.

Compare the neighbours: **furtive** is nervous and gives you away; **clandestine** and **surreptitious** carry a whiff of the illicit. **Covert** is the coolest of the four — controlled, professional, often sanctioned from above.

**Noun:** a thicket that gives game animals cover.

⚠️ **covert ≠ overt.** They are exact opposites and one letter apart. **Covert** = covered up; **overt** = out in the open. Exam sentences love pairing them ("*not overt hostility but ___ resistance*").

### Trick to lock it in
> **Covert** has **cover** in it — and it's *under* cover, not *in a corner*.

The hidden word gives you the meaning, and "under cover, not in a corner" keeps the tone right — covert is organised concealment, not twitchy sneaking.

### In sentences
1. The committee concluded that the agency had run a **covert** campaign of influence for nearly a decade, funded through shell companies.
2. Nobody said anything, but there was a **covert** eye-roll going round the table every time he mentioned his promotion.

### In the wild
**Covert ops** — the standard term in intelligence and in every spy film about it. If the CIA denies it, it was covert.

### Where it comes from
Old French **covert**, past participle of *covrir* "to cover" (Latin *cooperire*). Covert is literally *covered*.

Its opposite is built the same way: **overt** is the past participle of Old French *ovrir* "to open" (Latin *aperire*) — literally *opened*. Covered vs opened, and the pair is locked.

Same family as *cover* and *coverage*; also **curfew** (*couvre-feu*, "cover the fire") and **kerchief** (*couvre-chef*, "cover the head"). From *aperire* on the other side: **aperture**, **overture**.

# **credible** *(adj.)* — **KRED-uh-buhl**

### Means
Believable; worthy of being taken seriously. Also of a person or effort: convincing enough to be a real contender (*a credible candidate*, *a credible attempt*).

Notice what it stops short of. **Credible** is a bar cleared, not a verdict delivered — a *credible witness* may still turn out to be lying, and a *credible threat* is one plausible enough to act on, not one confirmed. It sits above *conceivable* and well below *proven*. Journalists, lawyers and security officials live on this word precisely because it lets them signal "worth believing" without claiming "true."

⚠️ **credible ≠ credulous.**
- **credible** = *deserves* to be believed (the story)
- **credulous** = *too ready* to believe (the sucker who swallows it)

A credulous audience will believe things that aren't remotely credible.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-ible** means *able*: cred-ible is believ-**able** — able to be believed, which is a long way from being true.

The suffix only ever grants a capacity, so the word promises plausibility and nothing more.

### In sentences
1. The commission dismissed the memo for want of a **credible** account of where the money had gone.
2. Give me one **credible** reason you were three hours late and I'll drop it.

### In the wild
Every news bulletin that reports police found "no **credible** threat" — the phrase is doing exactly this job: plausible enough to take seriously, not confirmed.

### Where it comes from
Latin **crēdere** = *"to believe, to entrust."* One root, a whole GRE cluster: **creed** and **credo** (what you believe), **credentials** (papers that make you believable), **credence**, **credulous** (believes too easily), **incredulous** (refuses to believe) — and **credit**, which is literally someone believing you'll pay them back.

- **Meaning cluster** · [Convincing on the surface](groups.md#meaning__convincing-on-the-surface) — with *plausible, verisimilitude, specious*
- **Intensity scale** · [Strength of evidence: from guess to proof](groups.md#intensity__strength-of-evidence) — with *conjectural, plausible, conclusive, incontrovertible*

# **diffuse** *(v. / adj.)* — **dih-FYOOZ (v.) / dih-FYOOSS (adj.)**

*The verb ends in a z; the adjective hisses — the same split as abuse (v.) / abuse (n.).*

### Means
**Verb:** to spread something out over a wide area until it's evenly scattered — heat, light, scent, power, tension.
**Adjective:** spread out, not concentrated; of writing or speech, **rambling and unfocused**.

The adjective is the GRE's favourite, and it's a put-down. Calling an essay *diffuse* doesn't mean it's empty — it means the material is all there but pooled nowhere, so no point ever lands. It's the opposite of **concise**, **cogent**, **trenchant**: not a lack of content, a lack of a centre.

⚠️ **diffuse ≠ defuse.**
- **diffuse** = *spread it thin*
- **defuse** = *pull the fuse out* — make a bomb, or a crisis, safe

You **defuse** tension. Writing "diffuse the tension" is one of the most common errors in English — and it's testable.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Diffuse light has no beam; diffuse prose has no point.**

Either way the stuff is present and spread everywhere, which is exactly why nothing lands.

### In sentences
1. The committee's report was so **diffuse** that a third reading still failed to surrender a thesis.
2. Turn the lamp on — that overhead light's too **diffuse** to read by.

### In the wild
The reed **diffuser** on someone's shelf is named for its job: it spreads scent evenly through the whole room instead of concentrating it in one spot. Same with a photographer's flash diffuser — it kills the harsh beam.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dis-** (*apart*) + **fundere / fūsus** (*to pour*) — *poured apart*.

That pouring root runs everywhere: **confuse** (poured together), **profuse** (poured forth), **effusive** (gushing out), **infuse**, **transfusion**, **fusion**.

# **documentary** *(adj. / n.)* — **dok-yuh-MEN-tuh-ree**

### Means
**Adj.:** consisting of or based on **written records** — contracts, ledgers, letters, receipts.
**Noun:** a film built out of real footage and records rather than invented scenes.

The GRE almost always wants the adjective, and it lives in legal and historical prose: **documentary evidence** is what you can put in a folder, as opposed to *testimonial* evidence, which walks into court and talks. The word carries a quiet claim to reliability — a paper trail can't misremember or change its story. So calling a case "documentary" implies it rests on hard record, not on someone's account of events.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **documentary** shows you the footage; **documentary evidence** shows you the paperwork — either way, *here's the record, not my opinion*.

The familiar film sense and the exam sense are the same idea: something assembled out of actual documents rather than narrated from memory.

### In sentences
1. The prosecution's case was almost entirely **documentary** — bank transfers, signed invoices, and a single incriminating email.
2. There's no **documentary** proof he ever worked here; we're going off what two people vaguely remember.

### In the wild
Lawyers split evidence into three buckets in everyday practice: **documentary** (the paperwork), **testimonial** (the witness), and **physical** (the object). Hear one, you've placed the word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **docēre** = *"to teach"* → a **document** was originally a *lesson* or *proof*.

That root pays off across the GRE: **docile** (teachable, easily led), **doctrine** (what is taught), **indoctrinate** (to teach until it can't be questioned), **doctor** (originally a teacher), **docent** (museum guide who teaches you the room).

# **exhaustive** *(adj.)* — **ig-ZAWS-tiv**

### Means
Covering **every single case**, leaving nothing out.

Put it on a scale: *careful* < *thorough* < **exhaustive**. Thorough means you did the job properly; exhaustive means there is literally nothing left to check — every file opened, every name on the list, every possibility eliminated. It describes **coverage, not effort**, and it is almost always praise: an exhaustive study, an exhaustive search, an exhaustive account. The faint shadow it can cast is excess — an exhaustive answer to a simple question is more than anyone wanted.

⚠️ **exhaustive ≠ exhausting.**
- **exhaustive** = nothing was left out
- **exhausting** = it wore you out

An exhaustive review may well be exhausting, but the words are making completely different claims.

### Trick to lock it in
> **An exhaustive list is one nobody can add to.**

The test isn't how hard you worked — it's whether anything is left over.

### In sentences
1. The commission's **exhaustive** inquiry ran to nine volumes and interviewed every surviving witness.
2. I did an **exhaustive** search of the flat and the keys are just gone.

### In the wild
Almost every job description ends with the same line: *\"This list of responsibilities is not **exhaustive**.\"* — HR code for *and anything else we think of later*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex-** (*out*) + **haurīre** (*to draw water*) — to bail a well until it's dry. That's the whole word: an exhaustive search drains the subject dry; an **exhausted** person has been drained the same way; something **inexhaustible** can't be emptied no matter how much you take.

### Also sits in

# **exhilarating** *(adj.)* — **ig-ZIL-uh-ray-ting**

*The h is silent — never "eks-hil". Spelling trap: exhil**a**rating, not "exhilerating".*

### Means
Thrilling in a way that leaves you **elated and physically charged up** — heart going, grinning, more alive than before.

*Exciting* is broad and can shade into anxiety; *thrilling* leans on danger and suspense. **Exhilarating** is the joyful end of that family: the rush plus the lift. It describes cold air, first descents, standing ovations, winning arguments — the things that leave you *energised*, not drained. It's an unambiguously happy word, so it never carries dread the way "a thrilling near-miss" can.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside it: ex-**HILAR**-ating — the same *hilar* as **hilarious**.

Both words mean "made high-spirited," but hilarious lifts your laugh and exhilarating lifts your pulse.

### In sentences
1. The first unaided flight was, by every account in the diary, an **exhilarating** three minutes that no later triumph quite matched.
2. Cycling downhill in the rain with no brakes — terrifying, but honestly kind of **exhilarating**.

### In the wild
It's the default adjective of car advertising — every sports-saloon ad promises "an exhilarating drive," never merely an exciting one, because *exhilarating* sells the feeling you're left with rather than the risk.

### Where it comes from
Latin **hilaris** = *cheerful* (via Greek *hilaros*), with **ex-** as an intensifier: "to gladden thoroughly." Same root gives **hilarious** and **hilarity** — and the name **Hilary**, which simply meant "cheerful."

# **extraneous** *(adj.)* — **ek-STRAY-nee-uhs**

### Means
Coming from **outside** and therefore **not belonging** — irrelevant to the matter at hand.

*Extra* means there's more than you need; **extraneous** means it was never part of the job. A fourth paragraph restating your argument is superfluous; a paragraph about your childhood is extraneous. The word is a quiet dismissal — it says *this doesn't bear on the question*, which is colder than saying it's wrong. Register is formal and faintly clinical: editors, lawyers and scientists strip out extraneous detail, extraneous evidence, extraneous noise.

**extraneous ≠ erroneous.** *Erroneous* = **mistaken** (it's wrong). *Extraneous* = **irrelevant** (it may be perfectly true, it just doesn't belong here).

### Trick to lock it in
> **extra + strange** — not merely spare, but a **stranger**: it came in from outside.

That's the whole nuance — extraneous material isn't excess of the right stuff, it's foreign matter that wandered in.

### In sentences
1. The judge ruled the letters **extraneous** to the charge and refused to admit them.
2. Just answer the question — I don't need all the **extraneous** backstory about your flatmate.

### In the wild
Any lab report or psych study: you control for **extraneous variables** — the outside factors that sneak in and muddy your result.

### Where it comes from
Latin **extrāneus** = *"external, foreign,"* from **extra** = *"outside."*

Same root, unrecognisably worn down through French: **strange** and **stranger** — literally *the outside one*. Also **estranged** (pushed outside) and **extraordinary** (outside the ordinary).

# **fervor** *(n.)* — **FER-ver**

*British spelling: fervour. Same sound.*

### Means
Intense, burning warmth of feeling — passion at boiling point, usually about a cause, a faith, or a leader.

Set it against its neighbours: **passion** is generic and often private; **zeal** is passion *converted into action* (the zealot is out doing things); **fervor** is the heat itself, the emotional temperature of a person or a crowd. It's a slightly elevated, writerly word, and it travels in a small set of company — *religious*, *patriotic*, *revolutionary*, *nationalistic* fervor. That company is why it cuts both ways: a writer can use it to admire sincerity, or to hint that a crowd has stopped thinking and started burning.

**fervor ≠ furor.** *Fervor* is heat inside people who believe; *furor* (FYUUR-or) is a public uproar or scandal — the outrage a thing causes. A tweet sparks a **furor**; the believers who defend it act with **fervor**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ferv**or has a **fever** in it — a temperature you run, not a job you do.

Fever fixes both the heat and the key distinction from *zeal*: fervor is felt, zeal is done.

### In sentences
1. The pamphlet was written in the **fervor** of the first revolutionary summer, and every line reads like a man shouting.
2. He talks about his five-a-side team with a **fervor** most people save for religion.

### Where it comes from
Latin **fervēre** = *"to boil, to glow."* The heat is literal and still audible in the family: **fervent**, **fervid** (hotter, often too hot), **ferment** (bubbling away — also *political ferment*), and **effervescent** — *ex-* + *fervēre*, "boiling out," which is exactly what fizzy water does.

# **futile** *(adj.)* — **FYOO-tyle**

*British and older US: FYOO-tyle (rhymes with *tile*); most Americans say FYOO-tuhl.*

### Means
Incapable of producing any useful result — **doomed before it starts**, not merely unlucky.

*Unsuccessful* reports an outcome; **futile** passes judgement on the whole attempt. Calling an effort futile says the failure was structural — the thing could never have worked — which is why it usually comes with an implied *so stop*. It carries a weary, slightly superior tone: the speaker can see the pointlessness that the person still trying cannot. Reserve it for effort. A **futile** gesture is one that changes nothing; a *trivial* one is small; a *fruitless* one merely came back empty this time.

### Trick to lock it in
> **F + utile** — the *f* kills the *utility* before the effort even begins.

That "before" is the whole nuance: a futile attempt isn't one that failed, it's one that was never going to work.

### In sentences
1. Further negotiation proved **futile**; each delegation had arrived bearing terms the other had already refused twice.
2. Arguing with him about referees is **futile** — save your breath.

### In the wild
**"Resistance is futile."** The Borg's line in *Star Trek* — the perfect use of the word, because they don't mean *you'll probably lose*, they mean *your losing is already decided*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **futilis** = *"leaky, that pours out easily"* — said of a vessel that cannot hold what you put in it. From **fundere**, *"to pour."* Pour all you like into a cracked jug; you end up with nothing.

Same root, easy family: **fusion, infuse, profuse, effusive, refund, transfusion**.

# **illusory** *(adj.)* — **ih-LOO-suh-ree**

*Also spelled illusive in older writing — same meaning, rarer today.*

### Means
Based on an illusion: **appearing real, but having no substance** once you look properly.

No liar is required. *Fake* and *phony* imply someone manufactured the deception; *imaginary* means it was never there at all. **Illusory** sits between the two — the appearance is genuine, the thing behind it isn't. It's a formal, slightly cool word, and it almost always attaches to something people badly wanted to be true: an *illusory sense of security*, *illusory progress*, an *illusory recovery*.

**illusory ≠ elusive.**
- **illusory** = it isn't really there
- **elusive** = it's there, but it keeps slipping away

An elusive suspect exists and can't be caught. An illusory suspect was a shadow on the wall.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Illusory** = **"I-LOSE-ory."**

Say it and you hear the outcome: the gain, the safety, the lead — you lose it the moment you reach for it, because there was never anything to hold.

### In sentences
1. The apparent rise in wages proved **illusory** once inflation was subtracted.
2. We were two goals up at half-time, but that lead turned out to be totally **illusory**.

### In the wild
Contract law has a term of art — the **illusory promise**: wording that looks like an obligation ("I'll buy as much as I feel like") but binds you to nothing, so no contract forms. Looks like a commitment; contains no commitment.

### Where it comes from
Latin **illūdere** = *in-* ("at, upon") + **lūdere** ("to play") → *to play upon, to mock.* An illusory thing is playing a trick on your eyes.

**lūdere** is a workhorse root: **elude** (play away from), **delude** (play someone false), **allude** (play toward, hint), **collude** (play together, secretly), **ludicrous** (fit for play), **prelude / interlude**.

# **invidious** *(adj.)* — **in-VID-ee-uhs**

### Means
Likely to **provoke resentment, envy or ill will** — and unfair in doing so. An *invidious comparison* is one that unjustly ranks people; an *invidious distinction* singles someone out for no defensible reason; an *invidious position* is one where whatever you choose, somebody ends up aggrieved.

The word points outward, at the **effect on other people**, not at the speaker's feelings. You are not invidious because you envy someone — you are invidious because your act breeds bad blood. It's a cool, formal word of criticism, favoured by judges, editorials and academics: it says *unfair and needlessly divisive* without ever raising its voice.

⚠️ **invidious ≠ insidious.**
- **invidious** = causing resentment; unfair (Latin *videre*, to see — the envious look)
- **insidious** = creeping, sneakily harmful (Latin *sedere*, to sit — an ambush lying in wait)

An invidious rule offends openly; an insidious disease works quietly.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Invidious doesn't *feel* envy — it *causes* it.**

The envious person is the victim; the invidious act is what put the sour taste in their mouth.

### In sentences
1. Ranking colleagues by a single crude metric draws **invidious** distinctions between people doing quite different jobs.
2. Don't ask me to pick a favourite nephew — that's an **invidious** question and I'm not falling for it.

### In the wild
**NVIDIA** took its name from Latin *invidia* — the founders wanted a brand rivals would envy, and the logo came out **green**. Same root, and the green-with-envy joke is baked in.

### Where it comes from
Latin **invidere** = *"to look against, to look at with the evil eye"* → **invidia**, *envy*. From **in-** (against) + **vidēre** (to see) — the same *vidēre* behind **video, evident, vision, provide, visa**. And *invidia*, softened through French, is simply our word **envy**.

# **lethargic** *(adj.)* — **luh-THAR-jik**

### Means
Drained of energy — abnormally drowsy, sluggish, slow to move or react.

The key is that it's a **condition, not a character flaw**. *Lazy* and *indolent* judge you: you could work, you choose not to. *Lethargic* is something that happens **to** you — heat, flu, jet lag, a heavy lunch, grief. It's usually temporary, and it carries a faint clinical edge; a doctor will ask if a patient has been lethargic, never if they've been lazy. Applied to a market, a crowd, or a plot, it means the same drained slowness — nothing is moving, and nothing seems able to.

### Trick to lock it in
> le-**THAR**-gic — the stress lands on **TAR**, and that's what you're wading through.

Tar doesn't make you unwilling to move, it makes you unable to — which is exactly the involuntary sluggishness that separates *lethargic* from *lazy*.

### In sentences
1. By the fourth week of the heatwave the whole city had turned **lethargic**, its offices half-empty and its streets silent until dusk.
2. I had two slices of cake and now I'm too **lethargic** to even find the remote.

### In the wild
It's a live medical word, not just an exam word: paediatricians and vets treat "**lethargic**" as a red flag — *"Is she feeding normally? Is she lethargic?"* — meaning unusually limp and unresponsive, the symptom you call about.

### Where it comes from
Greek **lēthargos** = *lēthē* (forgetfulness) + *argos* (idle). Literally **"drowsy forgetfulness"** — the mind switched off along with the body.

*Lēthē* is the river in Hades whose water erased your memories; *argos* is *a-* (not) + *ergon* (work), the same *ergon* behind **energy**, **ergonomic**, and **allergy**. So lethargic is, precisely, *not-working*.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *lethal*.** That's Latin *letum*, "death" — pure sound coincidence.

# **metaphorical** *(adj.)* — **met-uh-FOR-i-kuhl**

### Means
Not literal — meaning something by comparison rather than by fact. A **metaphorical** wall isn't made of brick; a **metaphorical** death is a career ending.

The word is almost always doing defensive work: it flags that a statement should *not* be taken at face value. That makes it the standing opposite of **literal**, and on the GRE it usually turns up as exactly that hinge ("a metaphorical, not a literal, reading of the text"). Register is neutral and slightly bookish — critics and essayists use it constantly; in speech it survives mainly as the hedge *metaphorically speaking*.

⚠️ **metaphorical ≠ metaphysical.**
- **metaphorical** = figurative, by comparison
- **metaphysical** = to do with ultimate reality — being, time, existence

A metaphorical journey is a career change. A metaphysical question is whether the journey is real at all.

### Trick to lock it in
> **If you can add "…so to speak" after it, it's metaphorical.**

That little disclaimer is precisely the job the word does — warning you off the literal reading.

### In sentences
1. The poem's shipwreck is **metaphorical**: nothing sinks but the marriage.
2. "I said I'd kill for a coffee — that was **metaphorical**, put the knife down."

### Where it comes from
Greek **meta-** (*across*) + **pherein** (*to carry*) — a metaphor **carries meaning across** from one thing to another. Latin built the same word out of its own parts: *trans* + *ferre* = **transfer**. Metaphor and transfer are the same idea in two languages.

*pherein* keeps paying: **phosphorus** (light-carrier), **semaphore** (sign-carrier), **amphora** (carried by two handles), **euphoria** (carrying yourself well), **Christopher** (Christ-bearer).

# **mimic** *(v. / n. / adj.)* — **MIM-ik**

*Verb forms sneak in a k: mimicked, mimicking.*

### Means
**Verb:** to copy someone's speech, gestures or appearance closely — usually to amuse or mock. **Noun:** someone good at it. **Adj.:** imitative, fake (*a mimic battle*).

Mimicking is **surface copying, and it points at its target**. *Imitate* is neutral and can be sincere; *emulate* is admiring — you copy the excellence, not the mannerisms. A mimic copies the tics: the vowel, the walk, the throat-clearing. That's why it slides so easily into ridicule — reproducing someone exactly is one of the oldest ways of making a room laugh at them.

It also has a straight technical sense with no mockery at all: in biology, a harmless hoverfly **mimics** a wasp; in engineering, one system **mimics** another's output.

### Trick to lock it in
> **mi-mic** — the word copies its own first two letters. So does the mimic.

The doubling is the definition: a mimic reproduces the surface of a thing, exactly and visibly.

### In sentences
1. Certain harmless snakes survive by **mimicking** the banded colouring of far deadlier species.
2. He **mimicked** the manager's little cough so well that everyone stopped laughing when the manager walked in.

### In the wild
The mockingbird's scientific name is *Mimus polyglottos* — literally "many-tongued **mimic**". Its entire act is stealing other birds' songs.

### Where it comes from
Greek **mimos** = *"imitator, actor"* — a performer who copied people on stage.

One root, a full family: **mime**, **pantomime**, **mimicry**, **mimetic**, and **mimeograph** (the old duplicating machine — literally a "copy-writer").

# **numinous** *(adj.)* — **NOO-mi-nuhs**

*British speakers often say NYOO-mi-nuhs; stress never moves off the first syllable.*

### Means
Filled with, or suggesting, the presence of a divinity — spiritually charged, mysterious, awe-inspiring.

This is not the same as *beautiful*, *serene*, or even *sacred*. **Sacred** is a status something is granted; **numinous** is a sensation something inflicts on you. The word carries awe *and* a shiver of dread — the feeling of standing in a place where something vastly greater might be paying attention. Reserve it for cave paintings, empty cathedrals, the deep ocean, a total eclipse. Using it for a nice sunset is overclaiming.

**numinous ≠ luminous.** *Luminous* = giving off light. *Numinous* = giving off the presence of a god. A stained-glass window is luminous; the silence underneath it is numinous.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Numinous** hides **numen** — a god's *nod*. Not light in the room: Someone in the room.

The one-letter gap from *luminous* is the whole distinction — one is illuminated, the other is inhabited.

### In sentences
1. The chapel's unlit interior had a **numinous** stillness that stopped conversation at the door.
2. I'm not religious, but standing under those redwoods was genuinely **numinous** — I couldn't talk for a minute.

### In the wild
C.S. Lewis used *numinous* as a technical term in *The Problem of Pain*, and then built a character out of it: Aslan, who is "good" but explicitly "not a *tame* lion." That mix of awe and fear is exactly what the word names.

### Where it comes from
Latin **numen** = *"divine will, godhead"* — literally a **nod**, from **nuere**, "to nod." A god signalled assent by inclining the head, so the nod itself became the name for divine power.

Same root, wildly different word: **innuendo** (from *innuere*, "to nod at") — meaning conveyed by a gesture rather than said outright.

The US dollar bill's **"Annuit Coeptis"** — *"He nods at our undertakings"* — is the same *nuere* staring at you from above the pyramid.

# **obscure** *(adj. / v.)* — **uhb-SKYOOR**

### Means
**Adjective:** (1) not clearly expressed — hard to make sense of; (2) not well known — small, forgotten, off the map.
**Verb:** to cover, dim, or overshadow something so it can't be seen or understood clearly.

Both senses are the same idea: something is *in the way*. The adjective is usually flat and descriptive rather than hostile — *an obscure clause*, *an obscure Belgian poet* — though the "little-known" sense carries a faint sniff of insignificance when someone says *some obscure blog*. Compare the neighbours: **cryptic** implies the vagueness was deliberate, **arcane** implies you'd need specialist initiation, while *obscure* just reports that the light isn't getting through. The verb is the GRE's favourite — it often means to hide by *outshining*: a charming manner obscures a ruthless one.

⚠️ **obscure ≠ obtuse.**
- **obscure** = the *thing* is unclear
- **obtuse** = the *person* is slow to grasp it

A badly drafted contract is obscure. The man who still can't follow it after four readings is obtuse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Obscure = a screen pulled across — over the *meaning* (can't follow it) or over the *name* (never heard of it).**

One word, one image, both senses: whatever's behind the screen is still there, you just can't get at it.

### In sentences
1. The treaty's final article is deliberately **obscure**, and every signatory reads it to suit itself.
2. He kept quoting some **obscure** 90s band nobody at the table had heard of.

### In the wild
Thomas Hardy's novel **Jude the Obscure** — Jude is a stonemason who dreams of Oxford and dies unknown. The title *is* the definition: a life nobody ever heard of.

### Where it comes from
Latin **obscurus** = *"covered over, dark."* That's why the verb still means to *cover*, and the adjective means what's left when the light can't reach.

Same root in **chiaroscuro** — the painter's technique of *chiaro* (light) against *oscuro* (dark).

# **overt** *(adj.)* — **oh-VURT**

*OH-vurt (stress on the first syllable) is also standard, especially in the legal phrase "an overt act."*

### Means
Done or shown **openly**, in plain view, with no attempt to disguise it.

The word is almost never wasted on things nobody would hide. You call something *overt* precisely because concealment was the expected option — **overt hostility**, **overt racism**, **overt surveillance**, an **overt act** of war. So it carries a faint charge: either the person has stopped bothering to pretend, or the openness is itself the point (a show of force meant to be seen). Neutral cousins like *open* or *obvious* have none of that undertone.

⚠️ **overt vs. covert** — one letter, opposite meanings.
- **overt** = out in the open
- **covert** = hidden, concealed

They are *not* a prefix pair. **Covert** comes from *cover*; **overt** comes from *open*. Unrelated roots that happened to collide.

### Trick to lock it in
> **O**vert = **o**pen. **C**overt = **c**overed.

The first letter of each word is the first letter of its meaning, and *overt* is the one where the cover has been deliberately dropped.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal held that stockpiling weapons on the border constituted an **overt** act of aggression, not a defensive precaution.
2. He didn't even try to hide it — that was **overt** favouritism and everyone in the room saw it.

### In the wild
Every spy film — *Bourne*, *Bond*, *Mission: Impossible* — runs on **covert** ops. *Overt* is the same job done where everyone can watch: the aircraft carrier parked off your coast rather than the man in your hotel.

### Where it comes from
Old French **overt**, past participle of *ovrir* "to open" (modern French *ouvert*), from Latin **aperīre** = *to open*.

Same root: **aperture** (an opening), **overture** (the piece that opens the opera; to *make overtures* is to open negotiations), **aperitif** (the drink that opens the meal).

# **pellucid** *(adj.)* — **puh-LOO-sid**

### Means
**Transparently clear** — either physically (water, glass, air you can see straight through) or mentally (prose, an explanation, a mind that admits no murk).

*Clear* is a plain report; **pellucid** is a compliment. It carries light with it — the sense that the thing isn't merely see-through but luminous, unclouded, almost beautiful in its clarity. You'd call a mountain stream or a great teacher's explanation pellucid; you wouldn't say it of a windowpane unless you were feeling lyrical. It's a literary word, and it always praises.

### Trick to lock it in
> **pel-LUC-id** — the stress lands squarely on the **LUC**, the light.

Say it aloud and you hear *lucid* sitting inside the word with *per-* ("through") in front — clarity that light passes all the way through, not just clarity you can tolerate.

### In sentences
1. The lake was so **pellucid** that the boat seemed suspended above its own shadow.
2. His notes are **pellucid** — I understood the whole topic in one read, which never happens.

### Where it comes from
Latin **per-** (*through*) + **lucēre** (*to shine*) — light shining all the way through.

That *luc-* root is a GRE goldmine: **lucid** (clear-minded), **elucidate** (to shed light on), **translucent** (light passes, images don't), **lucent**, **lux/lumen** — and **Lucifer**, literally *"light-bearer."*

# **perpetuate** *(v.)* — **per-PECH-oo-ayt**

### Means
To cause something to **continue indefinitely** — to keep it alive, going, in force.

Technically neutral, but look at what people actually perpetuate: myths, stereotypes, cycles of poverty, an unjust system, a feud. The word carries a quiet accusation — you didn't start it, but you're keeping it running, and it should have died. *Preserve* protects something worth having; *prolong* stretches something that was going to end anyway; **perpetuate** hands the bad thing to the next generation. The rare positive use is deliberately grand: *to perpetuate her memory*.

**perpetuate ≠ perpetrate.**
- **perpetrate** = *commit* an act (a crime, a fraud, a hoax) — one deed, one doer
- **perpetuate** = *keep it going* — no single deed, just continuation

### Trick to lock it in
> **You perpe*trate* a crime once; you perpe*tuate* a stereotype forever.**

The **-trate** word is a single act with a culprit; the **-tuate** word is the endless continuation of something that ought to have stopped.

### In sentences
1. Far from correcting the record, the textbook **perpetuated** a version of the war that historians had abandoned decades earlier.
2. Paying him off just **perpetuates** the whole mess — he'll be back next month asking for more.

### In the wild
Legal and financial paperwork keeps its cousin alive: a trust that runs **in perpetuity** never ends — same root, same idea of something that refuses to stop.

### Where it comes from
Latin **perpetuus** = *"continuous, unbroken"* — *per-* (through) + *petere* (to go toward). It runs straight through without a break: **perpetual**, **perpetuity**, **perpetual motion**.

🚫 **Not *perpetrate*.** That's Latin *patrare*, "to bring about, accomplish" — a completed deed, not an unending one.

- **Opposites** · [Never stopping vs. stopping and starting](groups.md#antonym__never-stopping-vs-stopping-and-starting) — with *incessant, interminable, perennial, intermittent, sporadic, fitful, desultory*

# **rational** *(adj.)* — **RASH-uh-nuhl**

### Means
Based on reason and logic rather than emotion or impulse; also, *sane* — in possession of one's reasoning faculty ("he wasn't rational at the time"). In maths, a **rational number** is one expressible as a ratio of two integers.

*Reasonable* and *rational* are not the same praise. **Reasonable** is about fairness and moderation — a reasonable price, a reasonable person hears you out. **Rational** is about *process*: the conclusion follows from the premises. That's why a policy can be perfectly rational and still monstrous — cold arithmetic, correctly done. When someone says "let's be rational about this," they usually mean *stop feeling and start calculating*.

⚠️ **rational ≠ rationale.**
- **rational** *(adj., RASH-uh-nuhl)* = logical, sane
- **rationale** *(noun, rash-uh-NAL)* = the stated reasons behind a decision

You give the **rationale** for a plan; you hope the plan is **rational**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Rational** has **ratio** sitting inside it — running the numbers, not the feelings.

A rational person works out the ratio; a *reasonable* one hears you out — one is arithmetic, the other is fairness.

### In sentences
1. The committee's proposal was internally **rational** — every step followed from the last — and yet no one who read it could call it humane.
2. Give me one **rational** reason to drive four hours instead of taking the train.

### In the wild
Economics runs on the **rational actor** — the textbook consumer who always maximises his own payoff and never buys the shoes because they're pretty. Behavioural economics exists mostly to point out that no such person has ever been observed.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ratio** = *"reckoning, calculation, account"* (from *reri*, to reckon). The counting sense is the original one, and it never left: a **rational** number is literally one you can write as a **ratio**.

One root, a whole shelf of words: **ratio, rate, ration** (a calculated share), **reason, rationale, ratify**.

# **scathing** *(adj.)* — **SKAY-dhing**

*Rhymes with bathing, not with bath — long "ay", and the th is voiced.*

### Means
Of criticism: **severe enough to wound** — bitter, contemptuous, and aimed to leave a mark.

Harsh is blunt; **scathing is skilful**. It carries a note of scorn and usually a note of style — the scathing reviewer is enjoying himself, and the audience is enjoying it with him. It attaches to the *attack*, not the attacker: a **scathing** review, report, rebuke, dismissal. And it implies the target actually got hurt — you don't call a criticism scathing if it bounced off.

⚠️ **scathing ≠ seething.**
- **scathing** = lashing *out* — words that flay someone
- **seething** = boiling *inside* — silent, contained rage

A seething man says nothing. A scathing man says far too much.

### Trick to lock it in
> You already know **unscathed** — untouched, unharmed. Strip the *un-* and you get the thing doing the harm.

*Scathe* means to injure, and it survives in modern English almost only in its negative, so let *unscathed* hand you the meaning for free.

### In sentences
1. The committee's report was **scathing**, describing the ministry's oversight as "negligent to the point of complicity."
2. She sent one **scathing** text about his playlist and he hasn't recovered since.

### In the wild
Newsroom boilerplate: watchdogs and auditors release **"a scathing report,"** critics file **"a scathing review."** If you've read a headline this week, you've met the word in its natural habitat.

### Where it comes from
Old Norse **skaða** = *"to injure, to damage"* — the Vikings left it behind in English as **scathe**.

Same root as German **Schaden** (harm), which you already know from **Schadenfreude** — joy at someone else's *damage*. Fitting: that's roughly what a scathing review runs on.

# **subtle** *(adj.)* — **SUH-tuhl**

*The b is completely silent — never "SUB-tul". Noun: subtlety, SUH-tuhl-tee.*

### Means
So fine, delicate or understated that it's easy to miss — a **subtle** shade of blue, a **subtle** difference in wording, a **subtle** hint. Applied to people and minds it means the opposite of blunt: able to perceive or produce fine distinctions.

Praise, mostly — it credits precision and restraint, the skill of doing a lot with very little. But the same fineness can be sinister: a **subtle** poison, a **subtle** manipulator, a **subtle** argument that quietly cheats. When the object is a *person's* method rather than a *thing's* texture, listen for that second, craftier sense.

### Trick to lock it in
> Even the **b** in *subtle* is too subtle to hear.

The word performs itself — something is genuinely there, doing its work, without ever announcing itself.

### In sentences
1. The novel's power lies in its **subtle** shifts of tone; nothing is ever stated, and everything is understood.
2. He winked, nudged me, and mouthed the plan across the table — real **subtle**, mate.

### In the wild
The flat, sarcastic one-word verdict — **"Subtle."** — dropped after someone does something spectacularly unsubtle. You've heard it used almost exclusively as an insult.

### Where it comes from
Latin **subtilis** = *"finely woven"* — from **sub** (under) + **tela** (the warp threads on a loom). A subtle fabric is one whose threads are too fine to pick out individually; a subtle idea is the same thing in thought.

That **tela** comes from **texere**, "to weave" — which also gives you **textile**, **texture**, **text**, and **context**. Weaving is hiding in more words than you'd think.

# **superficial** *(adj.)* — **soo-per-FISH-uhl**

### Means
Existing at or affecting only the **surface** — and by extension, going no deeper than appearances: shallow in thought, feeling, or attention.

Two lives, one idea. Literally it is neutral and even reassuring: a **superficial wound** is the good kind, a **superficial crack** won't bring the wall down. Figuratively it is a quiet insult — a *superficial reading*, a *superficial charm*, a *superficial person*. The sting isn't that they're wrong, it's that they stopped at the surface when there was more underneath. Milder and more clinical than *shallow*, which attacks the person; *superficial* usually attacks the **effort**.

⚠️ **superficial ≠ superfluous.**
- **superficial** = only skin-deep
- **superfluous** = more than needed, unnecessary

A superficial edit skims the page; a superfluous edit shouldn't have been made at all.

### Trick to lock it in
> **super + FICIAL — it never gets past the *face*.**

Whatever it touches, it stays *above the face* of the thing and never goes in.

### In sentences
1. His knowledge of the treaty proved **superficial**: fluent on the preamble, silent on every clause that mattered.
2. We had a **superficial** chat about the weather and then both pretended we had somewhere to be.

### In the wild
In hospitals it's a compliment, not a jab — triage notes grade burns as **superficial** (first-degree, top layer only) before *partial-thickness* and *full-thickness*. Same word, zero insult: it just means the damage stayed on the surface.

### Where it comes from
Latin **super** (*above*) + **facies** (*face, surface*) — literally "on the face of it."

**facies** is a workhorse root: **face**, **surface**, **facade** (a front with nothing behind it), **facet**, **deface**, **prima facie** (*at first face* — how it looks before you look harder).
