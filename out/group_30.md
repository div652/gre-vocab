# **aberrant** *(adj.)* — **uh-BER-uhnt**

*Also heard as AB-er-uhnt; both are accepted.*

### Means
Departing from the normal, expected, or proper course — abnormal, deviant, off-pattern.

The word wears a lab coat. *Unusual* is neutral, *weird* is casual, but **aberrant** measures you against a **standard** and reports that you failed it: aberrant cells, aberrant data, aberrant behaviour. It's the vocabulary of doctors, statisticians and disciplinary reports, so it carries a faint chill — the thing isn't just different, it's *wrong in a way that needs explaining*.

⚠️ **aberrant ≠ abhorrent.**
- **aberrant** = *deviating* from the norm (clinical)
- **abhorrent** = *repugnant*, morally disgusting (emotional)

Aberrant conduct puzzles you; abhorrent conduct sickens you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ab-ERR-ant** — the word literally has **err** in the middle: it has *erred away* from the norm.

Stress the *err* when you say it and you've said the definition out loud.

### In sentences
1. The audit flagged a handful of **aberrant** transactions, each one a small departure from a decade of predictable ledgers.
2. One **aberrant** night out doesn't make you a party animal, mate.

### In the wild
Photographers fight **chromatic aberration** — the purple-green fringing you get when a lens lets colours *wander off* to slightly different focal points. Same root, visible on your screen.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ab-** (*away*) + **errāre** (*to wander*) — literally *wandering off*.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **err**, **error**, **erratic**, **errant**, **erroneous**, **knight-errant** (a knight who wanders looking for adventures), and **arrant** nonsense.

# **abide** *(v.)* — **uh-BEYED**

### Means
Three live senses, all descended from one idea — **to stay put**:
1. **To tolerate / put up with** — *I can't abide his whining.*
2. **abide by** — to stick to a rule, ruling, or promise.
3. **To remain, endure, dwell** (archaic/biblical) — *a faith that abides.*

Sense 1 almost never appears un-negated. Nobody says "I abide his whining"; they say **can't abide**, and it lands harder and more personally than *can't stand* — it suggests a settled, temperamental refusal rather than a flash of irritation. Sense 3 carries a King-James solemnity: what abides is what outlasts. And *abide by* is the one form fully at home in a contract or a courtroom.

### Trick to lock it in
> You **abide** in an **abode** — so what you *can't* abide, you can't live with.

Same root, same idea of staying put: an abode is where you remain, and to abide something is to let it remain near you.

### In sentences
1. The treaty held for a century, its terms **abided by** even during open hostilities.
2. She can't **abide** people who reply with 'per my last email'.

### In the wild
**"The Dude abides."** — the closing line of *The Big Lebowski*, and a perfect gloss on sense 3: he doesn't win anything, he just endures.

### Where it comes from
Old English **ābīdan** = *"to wait, remain"* — from **bīdan**, "to bide," which still survives in **bide your time**. The same root gives **abode**: the place where you remain.

That's why the senses hang together — tolerating something is letting it stay, and abiding by a rule is standing by it.

# **bravado** *(n.)* — **bruh-VAH-doh**

*Also heard as bruh-VAY-doh; both are accepted.*

### Means
A **swaggering show of courage** — bold talk, strutting, defiance staged for an audience.

The word quietly calls the bluff. Bravery is a fact; **bravado is a performance**, and using the word implies the performer is covering fear, doubt, or weakness. Nobody praises you for bravado — it's what onlookers call your courage when they don't believe it. Often paired with softeners that admit the hollowness: *empty bravado*, *false bravado*, *a touch of bravado*.

⚠️ **bravado ≠ bravura.**
- **bravado** = *hollow* display of boldness — negative
- **bravura** = *dazzling* display of technical skill (a pianist's bravura passage) — a compliment

### Trick to lock it in
> **Bravado** = bravery + **ado** — *much ado about nothing*.

The extra syllables are the fuss and the noise, and there's nothing underneath them.

### In sentences
1. The general's talk of marching on the capital by Friday was pure **bravado**; his supply lines had collapsed a week earlier.
2. He kept saying the interview went great, but you could hear the **bravado** in it — he was terrified.

### In the wild
A boxing weigh-in: two fighters nose to nose, snarling promises for the cameras. That's the standard word for it — all **bravado** until the bell.

### Where it comes from
Spanish **bravada** / Italian **bravata**, from **bravo** = *bold, wild, swaggering*.

Same root family: **brave**, **bravo!** (shouted at a bold performance), **bravura**, and **desperado** — the *-ado* ending is Spanish swagger, and it's doing the same work in both outlaw words.

# **callow** *(adj.)* — **KAL-oh**

### Means
Young and **inexperienced** — immature not through stupidity but through never having been tested by life.

The word is a put-down delivered from above: an older, wiser speaker looking down at someone who talks confidently about things he hasn't lived through. Milder than *naive* (which implies you'll be conned) and softer than *green* (which is about job skills) — *callow* is about **character not yet formed**. It clings to the noun *youth* almost as a fixed phrase: **a callow youth**.

⚠️ **callow ≠ callous.** They are near-opposites.
- **callow** = *un*hardened — soft, raw, unformed
- **callous** = hardened — unfeeling, indifferent to others' pain

A callow recruit flinches at the sight of blood; a callous one doesn't.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Callow** rhymes with **fallow** — a field nothing has grown in yet.

Neither the field nor the boy has produced anything yet, and both are judged on that emptiness.

### In sentences
1. His maiden speech in the House betrayed a **callow** confidence that the older members listened to in amused silence.
2. I was twenty-two and completely **callow** — I thought quitting a job by text was a power move.

### Where it comes from
Old English **calu** = *"bald, featherless."* A callow bird is a chick that hasn't grown its flight feathers — it physically cannot leave the nest yet. That's the whole picture the word still carries: not ready, not fledged.

🚫 **Not *callous*.** That's Latin *callum*, "hard skin" — same root as **callus**, the thickened patch on a guitarist's fingertip. Unrelated word, opposite idea.

# **capitulate** *(v.)* — **kuh-PICH-uh-layt**

### Means
To stop resisting and **accept the other side's terms** — to surrender, usually on agreed conditions.

The word presupposes a fight. You cannot capitulate to something you never opposed: there was a siege, a standoff, a months-long argument, and then it broke. *Acquiesce* means you went along quietly from the start; *concede* can be gracious, even sporting; *capitulate* is the white flag going up after the resistance has been ground down. It usually casts the surrenderer as the weaker party, so it stings when applied to a person — "the board capitulated to shareholder pressure" is not a compliment.

⚠️ **capitulate ≠ recapitulate.**
- **capitulate** = *give in*
- **recapitulate** (recap) = *summarise, run back over the main points*

Same root, opposite exam answers.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cap it.** You finally put a lid on the fight — but only because you'd been fighting.

The "cap it" in the first syllables marks the moment resistance gets sealed off, which is the part *surrender* alone doesn't tell you.

### In sentences
1. After eleven weeks of siege the garrison **capitulated**, marching out under terms that spared the town.
2. I held out on the puppy for a year, then **capitulated** the second she started crying at dinner.

### In the wild
Traders talk about **capitulation** — the point in a crash where the last stubborn holders finally give up and dump everything. It's the word doing exactly its job: they resisted the whole way down, then broke.

### Where it comes from
Latin **caput, capitis** = *"head"* → **capitulum**, *"little head"*, i.e. a **chapter heading**. To *capitulate* was originally to draw up terms **under headings** — the surrender document was written in numbered articles.

That head-root is everywhere: **capital**, **captain**, **chapter**, **decapitate**, **per capita**, and **recapitulate** (go back over the headings).

- **Intensity scale** · [Giving in: from softening to surrender](groups.md#intensity__giving-in) — with *relent, acquiesce*

# **cogitate** *(v.)* — **KOJ-i-tayt**

### Means
To **think hard about something**; to turn a matter over in the mind, deliberately and at length.

Plain *thinking* is what everyone does; **cogitating** is thinking with the effort showing. The word is heavy, Latinate, and faintly pompous — which is why it's so often used tongue-in-cheek, about someone staring at the ceiling doing nothing visible. Used straight, it suggests slow, sustained deliberation rather than a flash of insight; you *cogitate on* a problem for a week, you don't cogitate for a second.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cog**itate — the **cogs** are visibly turning.

It's not quick thought but grinding, laborious machinery of thought — which is exactly why the word so easily turns comic.

### In sentences
1. The committee **cogitated** for three months and produced a report recommending further cogitation.
2. He's been **cogitating** over which laptop to buy since March — just pick one.

### In the wild
Descartes' **_cogito ergo sum_** — "I think, therefore I am." *Cogito* is literally this verb in the first person.

### Where it comes from
Latin **cogitāre** = *"to think"*, from **co-** (together) + **agitāre** (*to drive, to turn over*) — thoughts churned around in the head.

That **agitāre** gives you **agitate**, **agitator**, and (via *agere*, to drive) **agent**, **agenda**, **exigent**.

# **deportment** *(n.)* — **dih-PORT-muhnt**

### Means
The way a person **carries and conducts themselves** — posture, bearing, and outward manners.

This is behaviour *as graded by an onlooker*. It's a schoolmaster's word, faintly old-fashioned and institutional: the thing finishing schools taught and report cards scored. Where **demeanour** is the air or mood you give off, **deportment** is the visible discipline of the body and its manners — how you stand, sit, enter a room, address your elders — measured against a standard of propriety.

⚠️ **deportment ≠ deportation.** Both grew out of *deport*, but they split: **deportment** = how you carry *yourself*; **deportation** = being carried *out of the country*. The exam likes this gap.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Port** = carry, so deportment is how you *carry yourself* — which is exactly why its synonym is **bearing**.

Both words are the same idea twice: *deportment* carries the Latin for "carry," and *bearing* carries the English one.

### In sentences
1. The headmistress praised her **deportment** — spine straight, hands still, not a word out of turn.
2. Loved the interview answers, but his **deportment** was a mess — slouched, fidgeting, checking his phone.

### In the wild
The old finishing-school exercise of walking across a room with a book balanced on your head — that class was literally called **deportment**, and British school reports once carried a grade for it beside conduct.

### Where it comes from
Latin **portāre** = *"to carry."* One root, a large family: **portable**, **transport**, **export**, **porter**, **portfolio** (a case for carrying loose leaves), **comport** and **comportment** (a near-twin of *deportment*).

### Also sits in
- **Meaning cluster** · [Correct conduct](groups.md#meaning__correct-conduct) — with *decorum, propriety, comity*

# **extemporize** *(v.)* — **ik-STEM-puh-ryze**

### Means
To speak, perform, or compose **on the spot, without preparation** — no notes, no script, no rehearsal.

Unlike *wing it* (which admits you're bluffing) or *improvise* (broad — you improvise a meal, a splint, a plan), **extemporize** is formal and almost always about *performance in front of people*: a speech, a sermon, a solo. And it quietly flatters. Someone who extemporizes is holding the room without a net; the word grants them poise rather than exposing them as unprepared.

⚠️ **extemporize ≠ temporize.**
- **extemporize** = *improvise* — speak with no preparation
- **temporize** = *stall* — draw things out to buy time and avoid committing

The minister who extemporized gave a speech. The minister who temporized gave no answer.

### Trick to lock it in
> **TEMPO** is sitting inside it — the extemporizer keeps the tempo with no score in front of him.

The image is a musician, not a panicking student: unprepared but perfectly in time, which is exactly the competence the word implies.

### In sentences
1. When the teleprompter failed, she abandoned the prepared text and **extemporized** for eleven minutes without a stumble.
2. He forgot his cue cards, so he just **extemporized** the toast — honestly it was better than the written one.

### In the wild
School and college competitions still run an **Extempore** event — you're handed a slip of paper with a topic and get sixty seconds before you speak. That's the word in its native habitat.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ex tempore** = *"out of the time"* — i.e. straight out of the moment, with none set aside to prepare.

From **tempus, temporis** (time), which pays out widely: **tempo**, **temporary**, **temporal**, **contemporary**, **extemporaneous**.

# **factious** *(adj.)* — **FAK-shuhs**

### Means
Given to forming **factions** — creating, or arising from, dissension inside a group that is supposed to be one body: a party, a cabinet, a church, a committee.

The word is always spoken from the standpoint of the whole. Calling a bloc *factious* is a charge that they've put their sub-group ahead of the organisation, and usually that the quarrel is self-interested rather than principled — it's the vocabulary of political science and of people who think unity was the point. Note the scope: it describes **splits between groups**, never a single person's bad temper.

⚠️ **factious ≠ fractious.**
- **factious** = *splitting into rival camps* (a factious senate)
- **fractious** = *irritable, unruly* (a fractious toddler, a fractious horse)

Fra**ct**ious has the *fract* of **fracture** and **fractious** tempers snap. Factious has **faction**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **FACTIOUS = FACTION + -OUS.** The word literally has the answer inside it.

If a group is full of factions, it is factious — the split is between camps, not inside one cranky person.

### In sentences
1. The republic's collapse owed less to foreign armies than to the **factious** nobility, each house pursuing its own advantage.
2. Two months in, the book club had gone **factious** — the literary-fiction crowd wasn't speaking to the thriller people.

### In the wild
Madison's **Federalist No. 10** is the most famous warning about faction ever written — the whole essay is about how a republic can survive citizens splitting into rival interest groups.

### Where it comes from
Latin **facere** = *"to do, to make."* A *factio* was a **group that acts together** — originally a chariot-racing team at the Roman circus, later a political bloc.

That root is everywhere: **factory**, **manufacture**, **benefactor**, **artifact**, **facile**, **affect**.

- **Lookalike** · [facetious / factious / fractious](groups.md#lookalike__facetious-factious) — with *facetious, fractious*

# **fallow** *(adj. / n.)* — **FAL-oh**

### Means
Of farmland: **ploughed but deliberately left unsown** for a season so the soil can recover. Figuratively: a stretch of time that produces nothing — a career, a mind, a talent lying idle.

The word insists the ground is still **good**. *Barren* means nothing will grow; *fallow* means nothing is growing *yet*, by choice or by pause, and the harvest is expected back. So calling a writer's decade fallow is gentler than calling it wasted — it grants that something may be replenishing underneath.

⚠️ **fallow ≠ callow.** *Fallow* = resting, unproductive for now. *Callow* = young and inexperienced (a callow recruit). One letter, two unrelated ideas — and the GRE knows it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **f-ALLOW** — the field is *allowed* to rest; it isn't dead, it's off duty.

The hidden **allow** carries the whole nuance: the idleness is permitted and temporary, not a verdict of infertility.

### In sentences
1. Half the estate lay **fallow** that year, the soil left to gather back what three seasons of wheat had stripped from it.
2. He calls it a **fallow** stretch; his bandmates call it four years of not writing anything.

### In the wild
Standard in arts and sports writing — a band's "fallow period" between albums, a striker's "fallow spell" in front of goal. Always implies the form is expected to return.

# **feint** *(n. / v.)* — **FAYNT**

### Means
**Noun:** a mock attack or deceptive movement made to draw an opponent's guard away from where you'll actually strike.
**Verb:** to make such a move.

A feint is deception *designed to be seen* — it fails if the other side doesn't take the bait. That makes it tactical rather than dishonest: boxers, fencers, generals and negotiators all feint, and nobody calls it lying. Compare a **ruse** (any trick) or a **bluff** (pretending to hold strength you lack) — a feint is narrower and more physical: a committed-looking movement in the wrong direction, made so the right one lands.

⚠️ **feint ≠ faint.** Identical sound.
- **feint** = a fake move to mislead
- **faint** = to pass out (or: barely perceptible — *a faint smell*)

You feint to the left; you faint on the floor.

### Trick to lock it in
> **feint = feign + t** — you *feign* the punch to the head so the real one lands on the ribs.

The extra *t* is the only thing separating the word from *feign*, and the whole point of a feint is that it must look real enough to pull a reaction.

### In sentences
1. Napoleon's **feint** toward the enemy's left drew the reserves out of position, and the true assault fell on a hollowed-out centre.
2. He **feinted** like he was going to pass, then just walked it straight past the keeper.

### In the wild
Any football stepover — Ronaldo's shimmy, Neymar's shoulder drop — is a textbook feint: the body says left so the ball can go right.

### Where it comes from
French **feindre** = *"to pretend,"* from Latin **fingere** = *"to shape, mould, invent."*

That root is worth banking: **feign**, **fiction**, **figment**, **effigy**, **fictitious** — all things *moulded up* rather than found.

# **flagrant** *(adj.)* — **FLAY-gruhnt**

*First syllable rhymes with play, not with flag.*

### Means
Of an offence: **so obvious and so outrageous that it can't be excused or ignored**. A flagrant violation isn't just visible — it's shameless.

Compare **blatant**: blatant is about *loudness* and can be almost neutral (a blatant lie, a blatant plug). Flagrant carries **moral heat** — it's reserved for breaches of law, duty, or decency, and it implies the offender didn't even bother to hide. That's why it lives with a fixed set of nouns: flagrant *violation, abuse, breach, disregard, misconduct*. The word condemns the act, not the person.

⚠️ **flagrant ≠ fragrant.** One letter apart, opposite worlds: **flagrant** = scandalously obvious wrongdoing; **fragrant** = sweet-smelling. A flagrant garden is a crime scene; a fragrant one is roses.

### Trick to lock it in
> There is no such thing as **flagrant kindness** — flagrant only ever modifies a wrong.

If the noun after it isn't a violation, abuse, breach, or disregard, you've picked the wrong word.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal found a **flagrant** disregard for the safety rules: the alarms had been disconnected months before the fire.
2. Parking across two disabled bays outside the hospital — that's just **flagrant**.

### In the wild
The NBA grades its worst fouls as **Flagrant 1** and **Flagrant 2** — not the sneaky trips, the unnecessary-and-excessive contact everyone in the arena saw.

### Where it comes from
Latin **flagrāre** = *"to blaze, to burn."* A flagrant offence is one still burning in plain sight.

Same fire gives you **conflagration** (a huge blaze) and the legal phrase **in flagrante delicto** — literally *"while the crime is blazing,"* i.e. caught red-handed.

# **gratuitous** *(adj.)* — **gruh-TOO-uh-tuhs**

### Means
Done or given **without cause, need, or justification** — nobody asked for it and nothing warranted it. A secondary, older sense survives in formal and legal use: **free of charge, given without expectation of return**.

The everyday sense is almost always an accusation. *Unnecessary* is a neutral measurement; **gratuitous** says the excess was gleeful — the insult was thrown in for sport, the gore lingered because someone enjoyed filming it. It carries a whiff of bad taste and self-indulgence on the part of whoever did it. Note the collocations that do the heavy lifting on the GRE: gratuitous **violence, cruelty, insult, swipe**.

⚠️ **gratuitous ≠ fortuitous.** Same ending, opposite worlds: **gratuitous** = uncalled for (and unwelcome); **fortuitous** = happening by chance (and usually lucky).

### Trick to lock it in
> A **gratuity** is money nobody made you give — so anything **gratuitous** is thrown in unasked.

The tip is the harmless version of the same idea; swap the money for an insult or a bloodbath and you have the modern meaning.

### In sentences
1. The judge dismissed the passage as a **gratuitous** attack on a witness whose credibility was never in question.
2. Great film, but did we really need that **gratuitous** ten-minute torture scene?

### In the wild
It's the standard phrase of every film critic and ratings board: a movie flagged for **"gratuitous violence"** isn't being called violent, it's being called violent *for no reason*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **grātis** (*"for free"*), from **grātia** = *"favour, grace, thanks."* Free-of-charge came first; "unearned" slid into "unjustified."

The root is a GRE goldmine: **gratis, gratuity, gratitude, grateful, ingratiate, congratulate, grace**.

🚫 **Not related to *fortuitous*** — that's Latin *fors*, "chance" (also *fortune*, *fortunate*).

# **grovel** *(v.)* — **GROV-uhl**

*Rhymes with *novel*; many Americans say GRUV-uhl.*

### Means
To lie or crawl face-down before someone; figuratively, to beg, flatter, or apologise in a way that throws away your dignity.

The word carries the observer's contempt. **Apologising** is neutral, **pleading** can be dignified — *grovelling* is what you call an apology that has gone too far, performed upwards at someone with power over you. Nobody describes their own behaviour as grovelling except as a joke ("I grovelled, and he gave me the extension"). It implies self-abasement bought cheaply, and it makes the reader think less of the one doing it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Grovel = gro(und) level** — nose in the dirt, looking up at the boss.

The word puts you physically below the person you're addressing, which is exactly the loss of standing that separates grovelling from merely apologising.

### In sentences
1. Courtiers **grovelled** before the emperor, foreheads pressed to the flagstones until he deigned to speak.
2. I had to **grovel** to the landlord for about ten minutes before he agreed to fix the boiler.

### In the wild
Its natural habitat is the apology email — *"so sorry to chase, entirely my fault, whenever you get a moment"* — the grovelling register everyone recognises at work.

### Where it comes from
From Middle English **grovelling** = *"face downward"* (Old Norse *á grúfu*, "on one's face"). That was an adverb, but people heard the *-ing* as a verb and invented **grovel** to fit — so the word literally means *lying flat on the ground*, and the figurative sense never lost that posture.

# **indecorous** *(adj.)* — **in-DEK-uh-ruhs**

*Stress on DEK, not on the third syllable — in-di-KOR-uhs is a common slip (though some dictionaries now list it).*

### Means
Not in keeping with good taste or propriety; **socially improper**, unbecoming, in breach of decorum.

The offence is against **manners, not morals**. An indecorous remark at a funeral is one that was too jolly, too loud, too familiar — nobody was harmed, the *occasion* was. The word also carries a whiff of the speaker's own primness: it's the vocabulary of etiquette columns, obituaries and disapproving aunts, so it often sounds gently arch rather than genuinely outraged.

**indecorous ≠ indecent.**
- **indecorous** = bad *manners* — wrong for the occasion
- **indecent** = bad *morals* — obscene, offensive

Dancing on the table at a wake is indecorous. Doing it without trousers is indecent.

### Trick to lock it in
> **In-DECOR-ous** — your behaviour doesn't match the **decor**.

The word literally has *decor* sitting inside it: the room, the occasion, the dress code all set a tone, and you clashed with it.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador's laughter during the minute's silence was widely reported as **indecorous**, though no one dared call it worse.
2. Texting your ex from the wedding reception is a bit **indecorous**, mate.

### Where it comes from
Latin **decorus** = *"seemly, fitting"*, from **decēre**, *"to be becoming."* One root, a whole cluster: **decorum** (the standard you breached), **decor** (what suits a room), **decorate**, **decorous** — and **decent**, which started life meaning *"appropriate"* before it drifted toward morality. That drift is exactly why *indecorous* and *indecent* now part company.

# **intrigue** *(v. / n.)* — **in-TREEG**

*The noun is often front-stressed: IN-treeg. The verb is always in-TREEG.*

### Means
**Verb (1):** to arouse someone's curiosity — *the offer intrigued her*.
**Verb (2):** to plot secretly — *he intrigued against the king*.
**Noun:** covert scheming; a secret plot (or, older, a secret love affair).

One word, two moods. *Intrigued* is warm, common and slightly flattering — you're leaning in, not merely interested. The noun is shady: **intrigue** is politics conducted in corridors, alliances made behind a hand. Both senses share one idea — something tangled enough that you can't see the whole of it, which is exactly what pulls you closer.

Note that the GRE prefers the sense you use least: the plotting one.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in-TRIG** — a plot **tricks** you, and a mystery **tricks** you into leaning in.

The buried *trick* is literal — the Latin root is *tricae*, "tricks" — and it covers both halves: the scheming and the fascination.

### In sentences
1. The final years of the court were consumed by **intrigue**: whispered alliances, forged letters, and two poisonings no one investigated.
2. Okay, I'm **intrigued** — go on.

### In the wild
Political journalists live on the phrase **"palace intrigue"** — who's briefing against whom inside the White House. It's the noun in its natural habitat: not a scandal, just endless private manoeuvring.

### Where it comes from
Latin **intricare** = *"to entangle"*, from *in-* + **tricae** = *"tricks, complications, tangles."*

That root pays out three ways: **intricate** (tangled together), **extricate** (untangle *out* of), **intrigue** (tangled up in). A plot and a puzzle are the same shape — a knot you can't yet see through.

# **nominal** *(adj.)* — **NOM-uh-nuhl**

### Means
**1.** Existing **in name only** — the title is real, the power isn't. *The nominal head of the family.*
**2.** Of a price or sum: **token**, so small it's barely a real charge. *A nominal fee of £1.*
**3.** Technical (engineering, finance): **as specified, on-spec** — the stated or expected value, not the adjusted one. *Nominal wages, nominal thrust.*

Sense 1 is a quiet demotion. It doesn't accuse anyone of fraud — the label is genuine — it just drains it of substance, and it's usually said flatly by someone in a position to know. Sense 2 follows straight from it: a nominal fee is a price *in name only*, charged so the transaction exists on paper, not to make money.

⚠️ **nominal ≠ minimal.** *Minimal* just means very small. *Nominal* means small **because it's only a token** — the amount exists to name the thing, not to matter. A nominal charge is symbolic; a minimal charge is merely cheap.

### Trick to lock it in
> **NOM = name.** A nominal king is a king *in name*; a nominal fee is a price *in name*.

Whatever follows the word is real on the label and hollow in fact.

### In sentences
1. The monarch remains the **nominal** commander of the armed forces, though no sovereign has issued an order in two centuries.
2. They charge a **nominal** two quid for the tour — basically just to stop people wandering in.

### In the wild
NASA flight controllers: **"All systems are nominal."** Not \"good\" — *exactly as specified*. It's the word Mission Control keeps saying in *Apollo 13*, right up until things stop being nominal.

### Where it comes from
Latin **nomen, nominis** = *"name."* One root, a lot of GRE words: **nominate** (put a name forward), **nomenclature** (naming system), **misnomer** (wrong name), **denomination** (a named unit — of money or of church), and **ignominy** (*in-* "without" + *nomen* — namelessness, hence disgrace).

# **obdurate** *(adj.)* — **OB-duu-rit**

*Also heard as ob-DUUR-it; the first-syllable stress is the safer default.*

### Means
Stubbornly refusing to change course — and, crucially, **refusing to be softened**: unmoved by pleading, pity, or remorse.

*Stubborn* and *obstinate* are about holding an **opinion**. **Obdurate** is about a hardened **heart**. You are obdurate when the widow begs and you say no, when the evidence of suffering is right there and you feel nothing. It carries a moral charge — the speaker is not describing you, they are condemning you — and it lives in formal, often near-biblical register: an obdurate sinner, an obdurate creditor, an obdurate refusal to apologise.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ob-DUR-ate** — too **dur**able to break, no matter how hard you beg.

The *dur* is the same *hard* you hear in **durable** and **endure**, and the point of the word is that pleading bounces straight off.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal was **obdurate**: no appeal to hardship, however well documented, would reopen the case.
2. I said sorry about four times and he just sat there, arms folded, completely **obdurate**.

### In the wild
Milton's Satan, in *Paradise Lost* Book I, glares from the burning lake "with **obdurate** pride and steadfast hate" — the literal definition of a heart that will not soften.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob-** (*against*) + **durus** (*hard*) — hardened **against** you.

*Durus* is a workhorse root: **durable** (lasting because hard), **endure** (stay hard through it), **duress** (hardship used as force), **dour** (a hard, grim face), **indurate** (to harden).

# **obstreperous** *(adj.)* — **uhb-STREP-er-uhs**

*Four syllables — not "ob-STROP-." The middle is *strep*, as in strep throat.*

### Means
Noisily and stubbornly resistant to control — loud, unruly, and hard to manage.

Two ingredients, both required: **volume** and **defiance**. *Boisterous* is just high spirits and nobody minds; *obstreperous* is the noise of someone refusing to be handled. It's also a word spoken from above — teachers about children, judges about witnesses, flight crews about passengers — so it carries a faint dryness, as if the speaker is too composed to say "screaming brat."

⚠️ **obstreperous ≠ obsequious.** Same *ob-* opening, opposite behaviour: **obstreperous** = loudly defying you; **obsequious** = fawning all over you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Ob-STREP-erous: strep throat** — a raw, roaring throat that will not shut up.

*Ob-* means *against*, so the noise is aimed at you: it's not cheerful loudness, it's loudness as resistance.

### In sentences
1. The chairman adjourned the meeting when the shareholders grew too **obstreperous** to be gavelled into silence.
2. One glass of wine and my uncle turns **obstreperous** — arguing with the TV, arguing with the waiter, arguing with me.

### In the wild
It's the standard airline-and-courtroom euphemism: passengers get removed for "obstreperous behaviour," witnesses get warned for it. Whenever an official body needs a polite word for *making a scene and refusing to stop*, this is the one.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob-** (*against*) + **strepere** (*to make a noise*) — literally *to roar back at*. The *ob-* is the same "against" you already know from **obstruct**, **oppose**, **obstinate**: everything pushing the wrong way.

# **odious** *(adj.)* — **OH-dee-uhs**

### Means
Extremely unpleasant — repulsive enough to **deserve hatred**, not merely dislike.

Weight is the whole difference. *Unpleasant* is mild, *disagreeable* is polite, **odious** is a denunciation: it carries moral disgust and asks the listener to share it. It attaches to regimes, laws, comparisons, duties and people — a tyrant is odious, a chore can be odious, but nobody calls a bad sandwich odious. Formal, faintly literary, and never neutral.

⚠️ **odious ≠ invidious.** Both love the noun *comparison*, so the GRE loves the pair.
- **odious** = hateful in itself; it disgusts.
- **invidious** = likely to breed ill-will; unfair singling-out that makes people resent you (or each other).

An odious remark revolts the room; an invidious distinction divides it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Odious** sounds like it *stinks* — and it does, but what it gives off is **hate**: *odi-* = hatred.

Reach for it only when something is repellent enough to deserve loathing, not just to be a nuisance.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal called the practice of collective punishment **odious**, and unjustifiable in any legal tradition.
2. Cleaning out the office fridge is the most **odious** job on the rota, and somehow it's always mine.

### In the wild
The old proverb **"comparisons are odious"** — Shakespeare hands it to the idiot constable Dogberry in *Much Ado About Nothing*, who mangles it into **"comparisons are odorous."** The joke only lands because the two words sound alike and don't mean alike.

### Where it comes from
Latin **odium** = *hatred* → *odiosus*, "hateful." **Odium** survives untouched in English (*public odium*).

The root then goes underground in a way worth knowing: Latin *inodiare*, "to make hateful," softened through French into **annoy**, **noisome** (from the clipped form *noy*) and **ennui** — three GRE words that all began life as *hatred* and cooled into irritation and boredom.

🚫 **Not related to *odour*.** That's Latin *odor*, "smell" — *deodorant, malodorous*. Same sound, different family.

- **Lookalike** · [insidious / invidious / insipid / vapid — near-twins, different verdicts](groups.md#lookalike__insidious-insipid) — with *insidious, invidious, insipid, vapid, vacuous, intrepid*

# **plucky** *(adj.)* — **PLUK-ee**

### Means
Brave and spirited **in the face of long odds** — determined when you have every excuse to quit.

Two things narrow it. First, size: *plucky* is almost always used of someone small, young, poor, injured, or outgunned — you can't call a superpower plucky. Second, tone: it's warm but faintly patronising, the word a commentator uses for the team that fought hard and lost 4–1. *Brave* respects you; *plucky* pats you on the head while it admires you.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Plucky** is one letter off **lucky** — and you only get called plucky when luck was never on your side.

The word only attaches to the underdog: no favourite is ever plucky.

### In sentences
1. Outnumbered and out of ammunition, the **plucky** garrison held the pass for three more days.
2. She's tiny, but she's **plucky** — she'll argue with a bouncer twice her size.

### In the wild
British sports and news commentary runs on it — the **plucky underdog**, the **plucky** minnows knocked out in the quarter-finals. The phrase that fixed it in the language was WWI propaganda's **"plucky little Belgium"**: small, overrun, admired.

### Where it comes from
A **pluck** was a butcher's word for the heart, liver and lungs — the organs *plucked* out of a slaughtered animal. Nineteenth-century boxing slang borrowed it for courage, so a fighter with pluck literally had **guts**. Same logic that gives us *heart*, *spine*, *stomach for a fight*.

# **precocious** *(adj.)* — **pri-KOH-shuhs**

*Middle syllable rhymes with go — not "pree-KOK-ee-us."*

### Means
Showing an ability or maturity **far earlier than the usual age** — a five-year-old playing Chopin, a nine-year-old who argues like a barrister.

The word is nearly always aimed at **children**, and it carries a small barb. *Gifted* is pure praise; **precocious** admires the speed while hinting that something has arrived out of order — the child is charming, a little too knowing, possibly insufferable at dinner. Applied to an adult it's almost a joke. Used of talent (a *precocious* striker, a *precocious* debut novel) the barb softens into "astonishingly young."

### Trick to lock it in
> **PRE-COCIOUS = PRE-COOKED** — done long before the rest of the batch is even warm.

Being finished early is impressive, but something rushed to the table ahead of schedule always feels faintly unnatural — which is exactly the word's undertone.

### In sentences
1. Mozart's **precocious** command of counterpoint had emptied the courts of Europe of rivals before he turned fifteen.
2. She's seven and she just corrected my grammar — **precocious** is one word for it.

### In the wild
Sports commentary runs on this word: every 17-year-old who scores on debut is a "**precocious** talent." Same in book reviews — a "precociously assured first novel" means *she's twenty-three and it's not fair*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **praecox** = *"ripening early"* — **prae-** (before) + **coquere** (to cook, to ripen). Fruit that came in ahead of season was *praecox*; so is the child.

**coquere** pays out elsewhere: **concoct** (cook together), **decoction**, **cuisine**, **kitchen** — and, best of all, **apricot**, the early-ripening fruit, literally the same root.

# **remuneration** *(n.)* — **ri-myoo-nuh-RAY-shuhn**

*Not re-NUM-er-ay-shuhn — there is no "num" in this word, however often you hear it.*

### Means
**Payment for work or services rendered** — and usually the *whole* package: salary, bonus, shares, benefits, the lot.

This is the boardroom-and-contract word for money. *Pay* and *wages* are what you get; **remuneration** is what the agreement says you are owed. It's formal, slightly grand, and faintly impersonal — nobody complains their *remuneration* is late, they complain their pay hasn't landed. It also carries a whiff of *fair return for effort*, which is why it turns up whenever a company wants to sound like it has thought carefully about what an executive deserves.

The adjective **remunerative** shifts the sense: a *remunerative* line of work is one that pays **well**, not merely one that pays.

### Trick to lock it in
> **MUNE**, not **NUME** — remuneration is **muni**ficent, not **nume**rical.

Fix the spelling by hearing *munificent* inside it, and you also get the flavour: the generous, full-package word for pay, with nothing to do with counting.

### In sentences
1. The committee resolved that no director's **remuneration** should exceed twenty times the median salary of the staff.
2. Great job, genuinely — but the **remuneration** is insulting.

### In the wild
Look at any listed company's annual report: there is a **Remuneration Committee** and a **Remuneration Report**, dozens of pages on what the CEO got paid. Job ads do it too — "competitive remuneration package" is corporate for "we'd rather not say the number."

### Where it comes from
Latin **mūnus** = *"gift, duty, service"* → *remūnerārī*, "to give back in return for a service."

That root pays out across the GRE: **munificent** (giving lavishly), **municipal** (a town that took on public *duties*), **immune** (exempt *from* duty), **common** and **commune** (duties shared together).

🚫 **Nothing to do with *number*** (Latin *numerus* → *numeral, enumerate*). That false link is exactly why people write and say *renumeration*.

# **slovenly** *(adj.)* — **SLUV-uhn-lee**

*Rhymes with oven, not woven — SLUV-, never SLOH-.*

### Means
Untidy and careless — in dress, in habits, or in work. A slovenly room, a slovenly clerk, slovenly reasoning.

*Messy* describes a state; **slovenly** delivers a verdict. It says the disorder is habitual and the person can't be bothered — grubby collar, unwashed dishes, corners cut. The word also carries the speaker's disdain, which is why it lands as an insult and almost never as self-description. Note the reach beyond appearance: careless thinking and slapdash prose get called slovenly too.

### Trick to lock it in
> **sl-** is English's disgust cluster: **sl**ime, **sl**op, **sl**udge, **sl**ouch, **sl**ob — **sloven**ly is the *person*.

If a word starts with *sl-* it's usually something you'd rather not touch, and *slovenly* is that judgment aimed at someone's habits.

### In sentences
1. The inspector's report condemned the ward's **slovenly** record-keeping: undated charts, missing signatures, notes scrawled in three different hands.
2. He turned up to the interview in a **slovenly** state — creased shirt, yesterday's stubble — and wondered why he didn't get the job.

### In the wild
Orwell, *Politics and the English Language*: "our thoughts are foolish, but the **slovenliness** of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts." Proof the word attacks sloppy *thinking*, not just sloppy shirts.

# **soliloquy** *(n.)* — **suh-LIL-uh-kwee**

*The -quy ending is "kwee", never "kwy".*

### Means
A speech in which a character, **alone**, speaks their thoughts aloud — to themselves and to the audience, not to anyone in the scene. By extension, any extended bout of talking to oneself.

The defining condition is **no listener**. A **monologue** is aimed at people: a stand-up set, a villain's gloat, a rant at a dinner table. A soliloquy is private thought that happens to be audible — which is why it reads as sincere; there's no one to perform for, so no reason to lie. Almost always a theatre or literary word; calling your flatmate's kitchen mutterings a soliloquy is a deliberate joke.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Solo** is hiding in the front of it — a soliloquy is a **solo**, and a solo has nobody else on stage.

The moment another character is being addressed, it stops being a soliloquy and becomes a monologue.

### In sentences
1. Iago's **soliloquies** are the only place in the play where he tells the truth, because they are the only place he has no one to deceive.
2. He wandered off into a ten-minute **soliloquy** about his fantasy team while the rest of us ate in silence.

### In the wild
**"To be, or not to be"** — Hamlet, alone on stage, arguing with himself about whether to go on living. The most famous soliloquy in English, and a perfect specimen: nobody is listening, so nothing is being sold.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sōlus** (*alone*) + **loquī** (*to speak*).

**loquī** is a GRE goldmine: **loquacious** (talkative), **eloquent** (well-spoken), **colloquial** (conversational), **grandiloquent** (pompously grand), **circumlocution** (talking around a point), **ventriloquist** (one who "speaks from the belly"), **obloquy** (speech *against* someone — public disgrace).

### Also sits in
- **Root family** · [Root: loquī (to speak) — how well, how much, to whom](groups.md#root__loqui) — with *eloquent, loquacious*

# **spurn** *(v. (occasionally n.))* — **SPERN**

### Means
To **reject something with contempt** — to turn down an offer, a gift, a person, or advice in a way that insults whoever made it.

You can only spurn what was *offered*. Decline is polite, refuse is firm, reject is neutral — **spurn adds scorn**, and the sting lands on the person doing the offering, not the thing offered. It's why the standard collocations are all about wounded pride: *a spurned lover*, *spurned advances*, *spurned his outstretched hand*.

⚠️ **spur ≠ spurn.**
- **spur** = drive someone *onward*
- **spurn** = kick someone *away*

One letter, opposite directions.

### Trick to lock it in
> **spurn = spur + turn** — you turn your back and give it the heel.

The word literally hides the boot in it, which is why spurning is always insulting rather than merely negative.

### In sentences
1. Having **spurned** every offer of mediation, the union found itself bargaining alone.
2. He asked her out three times and got **spurned** three times — you'd think he'd take the hint.

### In the wild
Hamlet's *"To be or not to be"* lists what makes life unbearable: *"the pangs of despised love, the law's delay, the insolence of office, and the **spurns** that patient merit of the unworthy takes"* — the snubs a decent person swallows from lesser people.

### Where it comes from
Old English **spurnan** = *"to kick, to strike with the foot"* — the same family as **spur**, the thing on a rider's heel.

The physical sense never really left: to spurn is to shove something away with your foot. That's the whole nuance in one root — a refusal delivered as a kick.

# **stolid** *(adj.)* — **STOL-id**

*First syllable is short, rhyming with solid — not STOH-lid.*

### Means
Showing **no emotion or reaction**; heavy, impassive, unexcitable.

The word carries a faint insult. Describing someone as stolid is not praising their composure — it hints that nothing much is going on behind the face. A stolid clerk stamps your form while you weep at the counter; a stolid ox stands in the rain. It suggests **dullness and slow blood**, not discipline. Used of faces, expressions, crowds, and peasants in nineteenth-century novels.

⚠️ **stolid ≠ stoic.**
- **stoic** = feels the pain, refuses to show it — *admirable*
- **stolid** = doesn't seem to feel much at all — *unflattering*

The stoic soldier is brave; the stolid soldier is thick.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Stolid** is **solid** with a **T** — a solid lump of a person, and the T is for *thick*.

Solid is a compliment, stolid is the same immovability turned into a criticism.

### In sentences
1. The jury remained **stolid** throughout the testimony, not a flicker of feeling crossing a single face.
2. He just gave me this **stolid** stare, like I'd told him the weather instead of that I was quitting.

### Where it comes from
Latin **stolidus** = *"dull, stupid, unmovable"* — a sibling of **stultus** (*"foolish"*), which gives you **stultify** (to make something look stupid or futile). The Latin meant *stupid* outright; English softened it to *unexpressive*, but the sneer never fully washed out.

# **temerity** *(n.)* — **tuh-MERR-uh-tee**

### Means
Reckless boldness — nerve, gall, audacity in the face of someone or something you should have deferred to.

Courage is admired, audacity can be admired, **temerity never is**. The word is a verdict, and it's always the offended party's verdict: whoever says it thinks you overstepped, overreached, and didn't know your place. A private who questions the general has temerity; the general who charges the hill has courage. Same nerve, different standing.

⚠️ **temerity ≠ timidity.** They look like cousins and mean opposites: **temerity** = too much nerve, **timidity** = too little. If a sentence rewards you for pairing it with *shy* or *meek*, you've picked the wrong word.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nobody ever simply *has* temerity — they always **"had the temerity to,"** and somebody is always furious about it.

The word arrives welded to that phrase and to an affronted speaker, so if you can hear the indignation, you've got the nuance.

### In sentences
1. Having lost the case, counsel then had the **temerity** to bill the estate for his travel.
2. He forgot my birthday and then had the **temerity** to ask why I was quiet.

### Where it comes from
Latin **temere** = *"blindly, at random, without thinking"* — temerity is boldness with the eyes shut.

🚫 **Not related to *timid*.** That's *timēre*, "to fear" — *timid, timorous, intimidate*. One letter apart, opposite ideas.

# **tenuous** *(adj.)* — **TEN-yoo-uhs**

*Three syllables, not four — not "ten-YOO-ee-us."*

### Means
**Thin, slight, insubstantial** — and so weak that it may not hold. Used of connections, arguments, evidence, claims, and grips: a *tenuous link*, a *tenuous grasp on power*.

A tenuous claim isn't a lie — it's a claim resting on almost nothing. That's the sting: you're not accused of fabricating, you're told your reasoning is a thread. Compare **flimsy**, which is blunter and more dismissive, and **dubious**, which questions honesty rather than strength. *Tenuous* is the polite, academic word for "this barely holds together," which is exactly why reviewers and judges reach for it.

⚠️ **tenuous ≠ tenacious.** They look like siblings and are opposites in spirit: **tenuous** = *thin, barely holding* (Latin *tenuis*, thin); **tenacious** = *gripping hard, refusing to let go* (Latin *tenēre*, to hold). A tenacious lawyer can still have a tenuous case.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Tenuous almost never travels alone — it comes with a *link*, a *grasp*, a *hold*, a *connection*.**

Every one of those nouns is something that can snap, which tells you the word is about the strength of an attachment, not the truth of a fact.

### In sentences
1. The prosecution's case rested on a **tenuous** chain of inference, each link plausible and none of them proven.
2. He's got a pretty **tenuous** grasp of how the code works — he just restarts it when it breaks.

### In the wild
Newsroom staple: a **tenuous ceasefire**, a **tenuous connection to the suspect** — it's the word editors use when something is technically holding but nobody expects it to.

### Where it comes from
Latin **tenuis** = *"thin"*. The payoff is the family: **attenuate** = thin something down, weaken it (attenuated virus, attenuated signal), and **extenuating circumstances** — facts that literally *thin out* your guilt.

# **verve** *(n.)* — **VERV**

### Means
**Energy, spirit and enthusiasm — especially the stylish kind shown in performance or expression.**

Vigour is force; **verve is force with flair**. You praise a pianist, a writer, a winger, a lawyer's closing argument for verve — never a person merely working hard. It implies dash, confidence and a bit of swagger in the *execution*, so it's almost always admiring, and it sits slightly above everyday speech: reviewers, critics and match reports use it far more than friends do.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Verve rhymes with swerve** — not just speed, but a stylish swerve.

Anyone can run straight at full effort; verve is the flourish you put on it.

### In sentences
1. She played the Rachmaninoff with a **verve** that made the older, tidier recordings sound embalmed.
2. He can't cook much, but he chops onions with such **verve** you'd think he trained in Paris.

### In the wild
**The Verve** — the Britpop band behind *Bittersweet Symphony*. Bands don't name themselves "Diligence"; they pick a word that means *swagger and style*.

### Where it comes from
Straight from French **verve**, "enthusiasm," which from the beginning meant *artistic* inspiration — the fire in a writer or performer. That's why English verve still attaches to prose, playing and performance rather than to plain hard graft.
