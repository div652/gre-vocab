# **abound** *(v.)* — **uh-BOWND**

### Means
To exist in **great numbers or great quantity** — to be plentiful to the point of overflow. Two shapes: *opportunities abound* (the things abound), or *the river abounds in fish* (the place abounds **in/with** them).

It is stronger than "there are many." *Abound* implies the supply is past counting — teeming, everywhere you look. It's also strictly intransitive: things abound, but you can never abound something. The word itself is neutral and slightly bookish, so it takes its colour from its subject: *wildflowers abound* is lush, *errors abound* is damning, and *rumours abound* is the journalist's polite way of saying nobody has checked.

### Trick to lock it in
> **abound** ≈ **"all around"** — not just present, but present *all around you*.

The near-rhyme carries the nuance: abundance plus everywhere-ness, not a mere count.

### In sentences
1. Theories about the collapse **abound**, though hard evidence remains scarce.
2. Don't stress about lunch — cheap noodle places **abound** round here.

### In the wild
**"Rumours abound"** is a newsroom reflex — you'll meet it in headlines and match reports far more often than in conversation.

### Where it comes from
Latin **abundāre** = *"to overflow"* — *ab-* (off, away) + **unda** (*wave*). The wave breaks past its banks: too much, spilling over.

That *unda* pays out across the exam: **abundant**, **inundate** (flood in), **undulate** (move in waves), **redundant** (literally *"overflowing"* — surplus to need).

🚫 Nothing to do with **bound**. No leaping, no boundary.

# **amorphous** *(adj.)* — **uh-MOR-fuhs**

### Means
Without a definite shape — and, by extension, without definite structure: a blob, a fog, a committee with no chain of command, a draft with no argument.

In science it's purely descriptive: an **amorphous** solid (glass, plastic) is one whose atoms sit in no crystal lattice. Outside the lab it usually carries quiet criticism, but of a cool, diagnostic kind. *Vague* and *wishy-washy* complain about how something was expressed; **amorphous** says the thing itself has no edges yet — nobody has drawn the outline. It describes the object, not the speaker's irritation, which is exactly why it sounds clinical rather than cross.

⚠️ **amorphous ≠ amorous.** *Amorous* is Latin *amor*, "love" — full of desire. One letter-cluster apart, opposite worlds: an amorous glance, an amorphous plan.

### Trick to lock it in
> **a-MORPH-ous** — nothing to *morph*, because there's no shape there to begin with.

Note where the word actually turns up: less often on clouds and jellyfish than on strategies, movements and first drafts whose outlines nobody has bothered to draw.

### In sentences
1. The protest remained **amorphous** — no leaders, no demands, no obvious way for the government to negotiate with it.
2. Right now the plan's pretty **amorphous**; give me a week and I'll turn it into actual dates.

### In the wild
**Morpheus** in *The Matrix* is the same root — Greek *morphē*, "form," the god who takes any shape in dreams. He's the one who tells Neo none of the shapes around him are real.

### Where it comes from
Greek **a-** (*without*) + **morphē** (*form*). One root, a lot of GRE mileage: **metamorphosis** (change of form), **morpheme** (smallest unit of meaning-form), **anthropomorphic** (given human form), **polymorphous** (many forms), and **morphine** — named for **Morpheus**, the shape-shifting god of dreams.

# **austere** *(adj.)* — **aw-STEER**

### Means
**1.** Severe or stern in manner and appearance. **2.** Plain to the point of bareness — no ornament, no comfort, no indulgence.

The word describes a *stripping away*, not a cruelty. An austere man isn't vicious, he's unsmiling and unbending; an austere room isn't ugly, it's empty of anything you'd want. Crucially, it can cut either way: austerity is a discipline, so the word carries a faint respect that *grim*, *bleak*, or *harsh* never do — a monastery, a Bauhaus chair, and a budget with the perks cut out are all austere. Say it of a person and it chills; say it of a design and it can be praise.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Austere rhymes with severe — and it means the bare, stripped kind of severe.**

Lock the rhyme, then add the one thing severe lacks: what's austere has had everything soft *removed*.

### In sentences
1. His prose is **austere** — no adjectives, no flourishes, nothing but what the sentence needs to stand.
2. The flat was clean but weirdly **austere**: one chair, one lamp, and not a single photo on the wall.

### In the wild
**Austerity** — the word every finance minister reaches for when the budget is being cut to the bone. Same word, wearing a suit.

### Where it comes from
Greek **austērós** = *"drying, harsh to the tongue"* — first said of wine and unripe fruit that puckers your mouth. That's the whole flavour of the word: something with all the sweetness taken out.

# **belie** *(v.)* — **bih-LEYE**

### Means
Two senses, one idea:
**1.** To **give a false impression of** — the surface misrepresents what's underneath. *Her steady voice belied her panic.*
**2.** To **show something to be false** — evidence contradicts a claim. *The sales figures belie his optimism.*

The direction is what gets tested. The **subject of *belie* is the liar**: the calm face, the young skin, the cheerful forecast. Whatever follows the verb is the truth being covered up or knocked down. So *belie* never means "reveal" — though the sentence usually leaves you knowing the hidden truth anyway, which is exactly why people flip it.

Register is written and formal — reviews, obituaries, analysis. Said aloud it sounds bookish.

⚠️ **belie ≠ betray.** They fit the identical sentence frame and point opposite ways:
- *His grin **belied** his nerves* = the grin **hid** them.
- *His grin **betrayed** his nerves* = the grin **gave them away**.

One conceals, one leaks.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Be-lie — the appearance *be a lie*.**

The thing doing the belying is the one telling the lie, so whatever comes after it is the truth being denied.

### In sentences
1. The manor's peeling shutters **belied** the fortune still sitting in the family trust.
2. Don't let the tiny shopfront fool you — it **belies** how much money they move.

### In the wild
A stock line in sports and music writing: *"her slight frame belies the power in her serve," "his boyish face belies twenty years on the road."* Same shape every time — small surface, big hidden truth.

### Where it comes from
Old English **belēogan** = *"to deceive by lying"* — **be-** ("about, over") + **lēogan**, "to tell a lie." The word is literally *to lie about something*.

🚫 Not the *lie* that means recline (Old English *licgan*), and no relation to **believe**, despite the look.

# **capricious** *(adj.)* — **kuh-PRISH-uhs**

*Also heard as kuh-PREE-shuhs; both are accepted.*

### Means
Given to **sudden, unaccountable changes** of mood, opinion or behaviour — acting on whim rather than reason.

*Fickle* is intimate and emotional (a fickle lover, a fickle fanbase). *Capricious* is bigger and colder: it attaches to whoever holds the power — a capricious king, a capricious examiner, capricious weather, capricious fate. The sting isn't that they change, it's that the change **obeys no logic you can predict or appeal to**. And note the tonal split from *whimsical*: whimsy is charming, caprice is unnerving, because you have to live under it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Capricious = Capri-CORN, the goat** — it bolts up the cliff the instant the mood takes it, and nobody gets a reason.

Goats don't wander, they *lurch* — which is the word exactly: not gradual change but abrupt, unexplained swerves.

### In sentences
1. The emperor's justice was **capricious**: two men committed the same offence, and one was pardoned before lunch, the other exiled by evening.
2. Don't bother planning the barbecue — April weather here is **capricious** as hell.

### In the wild
In US law, courts throw out a government agency's decision for being **"arbitrary and capricious"** — the standard phrase for a ruling made on no rational basis. If you ever read a news story about a policy being struck down, this is the wording.

### Where it comes from
Italian **capriccio** = *"a whim, a sudden start"* — and Italians linked it to **capra**, *"goat"*, for the animal's abrupt leaping.

The goat runs through the family: **Capricorn** (the goat-horned sign), **caper** (to skip about), **capriole** (a horse's leap), **caprice** (the whim itself). In music a **capriccio** is a piece that changes direction on impulse — same idea, scored.

# **cerebral** *(adj.)* — **suh-REE-bruhl**

*Also heard as SER-uh-bruhl, especially in British English — both are standard.*

### Means
Literally **of the brain**; figuratively, **driven by intellect rather than emotion or instinct** — analytical, cool-headed, demanding thought.

Applied to a person or a work, it's admiration with a slight chill in it. A **cerebral** film makes you think; it doesn't make you cry. Calling a footballer cerebral means he reads the game rather than outrunning people. And it tips easily into criticism — *too cerebral* means bloodless, over-clever, all head and no pulse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Visceral** hits you in the guts; **cerebral** hits you in the brain.

Same grammar, opposite organ — and the organ tells you the temperature, since guts are hot and immediate while brains are cool and slow.

### In sentences
1. His novels are admired more than they are loved: **cerebral**, exact, and reluctant to raise their voice.
2. It's a fun match but a **cerebral** one — nobody's charging around, they're just picking each other apart.

### In the wild
**Cerebral palsy** — the everyday medical term that pins the root down: a condition originating in the **brain**, not the muscles.

# **congenial** *(adj.)* — **kuhn-JEE-nee-uhl**

### Means
**1.** (of people) pleasant, friendly, easy to get along with — because their temperament suits yours. **2.** (of places, work, conditions) agreeable, well-suited to your needs or tastes.

The key is **fit**, not virtue. *Kind* and *generous* describe a person in isolation; **congenial** is relational — it says this person or place agrees *with you*. A dour recluse can be congenial company for another dour recluse. It's a mild, civilised, slightly bookish compliment: warm but unexcited, the word for a good dinner guest rather than a beloved friend.

⚠️ **congenial ≠ congenital.**
- **congenial** = *agreeable, well-suited* — a congenial host
- **congenital** = *present from birth* — a congenital heart defect

One letter apart, and only one of them is a compliment.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Congenial** = **genial** *with* (**con-**) you — the "con" makes it a match, not just a mood.

*Genial* describes someone's warmth on its own; the *con-* adds the second party, which is why congenial always implies suitability to somebody or something.

### In sentences
1. The delegates found the Geneva sessions unexpectedly **congenial**, the disputes conducted over coffee rather than through lawyers.
2. Rainy Sunday, nobody talking, cat on the radiator — honestly the most **congenial** afternoon I've had all year.

### In the wild
Job ads and hotel listings run on this word — "a **congenial** working environment," "a **congenial** atmosphere." It's the register of polite advertising: promising you'll fit in without promising anything specific.

### Where it comes from
Latin **con-** (*together*) + **genialis**, from **genius** — your guardian spirit, from **gignere** *"to beget."* So: *sharing a spirit with*.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **genius, genial, genuine, genus, generate, gene, progeny** — and **congenital**, literally *"born together with you,"* which is exactly why it means "from birth" and not "friendly."

# **conspicuous** *(adj.)* — **kuhn-SPIK-yoo-uhs**

### Means
Easy to see or notice; standing out so plainly that it attracts attention.

*Obvious* is about the mind — the point lands. **Conspicuous** is about the eye — the thing sticks out of its surroundings. And it usually carries a faint discomfort: the conspicuous person is the one everybody clocks, the conspicuous spending is the kind meant to be seen. Turn it up and you get vulgarity (**conspicuous consumption**); invert it and you get the sly idiom **conspicuous by his absence** — so expected that the gap itself is loud.

⚠️ **conspicuous ≠ perspicuous.**
- **conspicuous** = *easily seen* — it stands out
- **perspicuous** = *clearly expressed* — a perspicuous explanation leaves no confusion

(And **perspicacious** = *sharp-sighted, shrewd*, said of a person.)

### Trick to lock it in
> **Conspicuous** hides ***spic*** — the same *spec-* as **specs**: you don't need your glasses to spot it.

Conspicuous isn't merely visible — it's visible to someone who wasn't even looking.

### In sentences
1. Her red coat made her **conspicuous** in a hall of grey suits — which was, of course, the point.
2. Don't be so **conspicuous**, mate — you're the only one in the library wearing sunglasses.

### In the wild
**Conspicuous consumption** — Thorstein Veblen's phrase for buying things purely so other people see you can afford them. It's the standard label for the flex: the logo bag, the supercar in a city with a 20 mph limit.

### Where it comes from
Latin **specere** = *"to look"*, with **con-** as an intensifier: *fully in view*.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **spectacle, inspect, prospect, suspicion, specimen, perspicacious** — all of them about looking.

# **cursory** *(adj.)* — **KER-suh-ree**

### Means
Done **quickly and without attention to detail** — a fast pass over the surface, skipping anything that would take time.

The word almost always carries a shrug of disapproval: it names an inspection that *should* have been thorough and wasn't. *Brief* is neutral, *concise* is a compliment, but **cursory** is a quiet accusation of laziness — a cursory apology isn't really an apology, and a cursory safety check is how people get hurt. It attaches by habit to acts of looking: a cursory **glance**, a cursory **reading**, a cursory **inspection**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Curs**ory moves at **cursor** speed — it runs across the page and stops for nothing.

Same root, same image: the blinking cursor sweeps over every word and reads none of them.

### In sentences
1. Even a **cursory** examination of the accounts would have revealed the discrepancy; the auditors managed to miss it for three years.
2. He gave the contract a **cursory** skim, signed it, and spent the next year regretting clause 14.

### In the wild
Lawyers and journalists lean on one stock phrase: **\"even a cursory glance at the evidence shows…\"** — a polite way of saying *anyone who bothered to look for five seconds would know you're wrong*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **currere** = *"to run"* (via *cursor*, "a runner"). A cursory look literally *runs past* its subject.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **current** (water running), **occur**, **recur**, **incur**, **concur**, **excursion** (a run out), **precursor** (one who runs ahead), **courier**, **discourse**.

# **daunting** *(adj.)* — **DAWN-ting**

*Some American speakers say DAHN-ting.*

### Means
So large, difficult or forbidding that it drains your confidence **before you begin**.

The word describes an effect on the person facing the thing, not a property of the thing itself — a mountain, a reading list and a job interview can all be daunting, and none of them are dangerous. It sits well below *terrifying*: it's the sinking feeling at the size of the task, not fear for your safety. Note also that it's polite, faintly formal office English — "a daunting challenge" is what you call something you fully intend to do anyway.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Daunting** rhymes with **haunting** — it looms over you before you've even started.

Both words are about something hanging over you rather than attacking you, which is exactly why *daunting* is intimidation, not danger.

### In sentences
1. The prospect of reconstructing the archive from water-damaged fragments was **daunting**, but she began that morning regardless.
2. Forty tabs open and a deadline tomorrow — honestly, it's a bit **daunting**.

### In the wild
In *Divergent*, the fearless faction is called **Dauntless** — literally "the un-daunt-able ones", the people nothing looms over.

### Where it comes from
Latin **domāre** = *"to tame"*, via *domitāre* and Old French *danter*. To daunt someone is to **tame** them — break their spirit so they back down.

That gives you the high-value GRE cousin: **indomitable** = *"un-tame-able"*, the person who cannot be daunted. Same root, opposite end.

# **deify** *(v.)* — **DEE-uh-fye**

*DAY-uh-fye is also heard.*

### Means
To **make a god of** something — literally, in the religious sense (Rome deified its dead emperors), or figuratively, to treat a person or thing as **above criticism**.

In modern use it's almost always the figurative one, and it usually carries a shrug of disapproval. *Admire* is fine, *idolise* is a bit much, **deify** says the worshipper has lost all judgment — the object can now do no wrong. Note the direction: you deify someone *else*. It describes the fans, the biographers, the party faithful, and quietly indicts them.

⚠️ **deify ≠ edify.** One letter apart, both about raising someone up — but **edify** = *instruct, improve morally* (an edifying book), while **deify** = *worship*. A sermon edifies the congregation; the congregation deifies the preacher.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-fy = make it so: simpl*ify*, pur*ify*, dei*fy* — make a *deity* out of a man.**

The word is just *deity* with the factory suffix bolted on, and the promotion is always undeserved — men don't qualify.

### In sentences
1. Later historians **deified** the founder, scrubbing the massacres out of the record and leaving only the marble.
2. Stop **deifying** your manager — he's a guy with a spreadsheet, not an oracle.

### In the wild
The Roman Senate really did vote emperors into godhood after death — which is why Vespasian, dying, is said to have cracked: *"Vae, puto deus fio"* — "Dear me, I think I'm becoming a god."

### Where it comes from
Latin **deus** (*god*) + **-ficare** (*to make*, from *facere*). Literally "god-make."

*Deus* is a workhorse root: **deity**, **divine**, **deism**, **deus ex machina** — and **adieu**, which is French *à Dieu*, "to God."

# **didactic** *(adj.)* — **dye-DAK-tik**

### Means
Designed to **teach** — especially to teach a moral lesson rather than simply entertain or inform.

The word swings on tone. Applied to a textbook, a museum label, or a fable, it's flatly descriptive: instruction is the point. Applied to a novel, a film, or a person, it's almost always a complaint — the work has stopped trusting you and started **lecturing**. A didactic writer wants a verdict delivered, not a story told; a didactic friend answers a question you didn't ask and adds the lesson at the end. Most GRE passages use it in this second, faintly weary sense.

⚠️ **didactic ≠ pedantic.**
- **didactic** = preachy — pushing a *lesson* on you
- **pedantic** = fussy — correcting your *details*

The didactic uncle tells you what the story means. The pedantic one tells you it's "fewer," not "less."

### Trick to lock it in
> **Didactic** — the lesson you **didn't ask** for.

The sound of the first two syllables carries the sting: instruction arriving uninvited.

### In sentences
1. Her later novels grew increasingly **didactic**, each character reduced to a mouthpiece for a position the author had already settled.
2. Great film, but that last speech was so **didactic** — we'd got the point twenty minutes earlier.

### In the wild
Every Aesop fable that ends **"and the moral of the story is…"** is didactic literature in its purest form — the tale exists only to deliver the line.

### Where it comes from
Greek **didaskein** = *"to teach."*

Worth the detour for its cousin: **autodidact** — *self*-teacher, someone educated without schooling. That one shows up on the GRE more often than *didactic* does.

# **disseminate** *(v.)* — **dih-SEM-ih-nayt**

### Means
To **spread something widely** — information, ideas, findings, sometimes spores or disease — outward from one source to many recipients.

The shape of the word is one-to-many and *deliberate*. You don't disseminate to a friend; you disseminate to a field, a public, a population. Compare the neighbours: *circulate* passes a thing around inside a closed group, *divulge* lets out one secret, *broadcast* is the plain-English version — **disseminate** is the institutional register, the verb of ministries, journals, and press offices. It says nothing about whether the content is true, only that it went far.

⚠️ **disseminate ≠ dissemble.**
- **disseminate** = *scatter it outward* (spread widely)
- **dissemble** = *cover it up* (hide your real feelings or motives)

Opposite directions: one publishes, one conceals.

### Trick to lock it in
> **dis-SEMIN-ate** — the same SEMIN as *seminar* and *semen*: **seed**, flung outward.

One sower, a whole field — which is why you disseminate findings to thousands rather than to one person.

### In sentences
1. The committee's mandate was to **disseminate** the trial results to clinicians before the paper cleared peer review.
2. Someone screenshotted the memo and it got **disseminated** across half the company by lunchtime.

### In the wild
Research grant applications routinely require a **\"dissemination plan\"** — a section spelling out how you'll push the results out to the world once the money's spent.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dis-** (*apart*) + **sēmināre** (*to sow*), from **sēmen** = *seed*. Literally: scatter the seed in all directions.

That root pays off across the exam: **seminal** (an idea that seeds everything after it), **seminar** (a seed-plot for young minds), **seminary**, **inseminate**.

English did the same metaphor twice — *broadcast* originally meant sowing seed by hand across a wide arc, long before radio borrowed it.

# **feasible** *(adj.)* — **FEE-zuh-buhl**

### Means
Capable of being **done** — a plan that can actually be carried out with the time, money, and materials available.

The word judges *execution*, never merit. A feasible scheme may still be pointless, unethical, or doomed; all *feasible* certifies is that the mechanics work out. It's the vocabulary of engineers, planners, and budget committees — cool, measured, faintly grudging. "That's feasible" is not enthusiasm; it's a green light on the arithmetic.

⚠️ **feasible ≠ plausible.**
- **feasible** = it can be *done* (plans, projects, routes)
- **plausible** = it can be *believed* (excuses, theories, alibis)

A plausible explanation, a feasible plan — swap them and the sentence breaks.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Feasible answers "can we?" — never "should we?"**

A feasibility study asks whether the bridge can be built, not whether anyone wants it there.

### In sentences
1. The committee concluded that a tunnel was technically **feasible** but ruinously expensive.
2. Sure, we could rewrite the whole thing by Friday — it's **feasible**, just miserable.

### In the wild
**Feasibility study** — the standard first phase of any construction, software, or startup project. If you've ever sat through a meeting about whether something *can* be built before anyone argues about whether it *should* be, you've met the word in its natural habitat.

### Where it comes from
Old French **faisable**, "doable," from **faire** ← Latin **facere** = *"to do, to make."*

That root is a GRE goldmine: **facile** (easily done), **factotum** (does everything), **efficacious** (gets it done), **artifice**, **benefactor**, **deficient**, **feat**.

# **flout** *(v.)* — **FLOWT**

### Means
To **openly disregard** a rule, law, or convention — and to make no secret of it.

Breaking a rule and flouting one are different acts. You can break a rule quietly, by accident, in shame. Flouting is **defiance on display**: the rule is known, the breach is deliberate, and the contempt is part of the point. It takes rules as its object — *flout the law, flout convention, flout the ban* — never people. Journalists live on this word, so it carries a faint tone of official disapproval, as though a clipboard is being filled in nearby.

**flout ≠ flaunt.**
- **flout** = *defy* a rule openly
- **flaunt** = *show off* something you have

You flaunt your wealth; you flout the tax code. Mixing them up is one of the most common errors in edited English — and the GRE knows it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Flout** rhymes with **shout** — and hides **out**: you break the rule *out* in the open, loudly.

A secret violation is never a flout; the whole word depends on being seen.

### In sentences
1. The company **flouted** environmental regulations for a decade, treating the fines as a routine cost of doing business.
2. He **flouted** the dress code so hard he showed up to the wedding in flip-flops.

### In the wild
Pandemic headlines ran on this verb — "partygoers **flouting** lockdown rules" was in the news almost daily in 2020, always with that tut-tutting official tone.

# **homogeneous** *(adj.)* — **hoh-muh-JEE-nee-us**

*Five syllables — the **-e-** before *-ous* is sounded: *-JEE-nee-us*, not *-JEE-nus*.*

### Means
Made up entirely of **the same kind of thing** — uniform in composition throughout, with no variation between the parts. Opposite of *heterogeneous*.

The word swings on context. In science and statistics it's flat description and often a virtue: a **homogeneous mixture**, a homogeneous sample, homogeneous variance. Applied to people — a homogeneous workforce, a homogeneous suburb — it becomes a quiet criticism, the polite word for *everyone here is the same*. Note also that it describes **composition, not quality**: a homogeneous group is all alike, not necessarily all good.

⚠️ **homogeneous ≠ homogenous.**
- **homogeneous** (hoh-muh-JEE-nee-us) = *uniform in kind* — the everyday word.
- **homogenous** (huh-MOJ-uh-nus) = a **biology** term: similar because of **shared ancestry**.

They're one letter apart and the shorter one is widely used as a sloppy misspelling of the longer. On the GRE, write the *-e-*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Homo- + genus = same genus** — everything in the batch is filed under one heading.

The biologist's *genus* is literally sitting inside the word, and the missing extra **-e-** is exactly what the fake-friend *homogenous* drops.

### In sentences
1. Critics faulted the shortlist as **homogeneous** — six novels, all by writers from the same two universities.
2. Their playlists are weirdly **homogeneous**; every song sounds like it came off the same synth.

### In the wild
The **homogenized** milk in your fridge — the fat is broken up and spread evenly so the cream never rises to the top. Same root, and you can see what it means: one uniform liquid, no separate layers.

### Where it comes from
Greek **homos** = *"same"* + **genos** = *"kind, race."*

*Homos* is a workhorse: **homonym** (same name), **homophone** (same sound), **homogenize**. Its partner **heteros** (*other*) gives you **heterogeneous**, **heterodox** — learn the pair and you get both halves of any GRE contrast sentence.

# **humdrum** *(adj. / n.)* — **HUM-drum**

### Means
Dull and boring **because nothing ever changes** — routine, monotonous, samey.

The complaint is about *repetition*, not quality. A bad film is tedious; a **humdrum** job is perfectly fine and simply identical every single day. That's why it attaches to lives, routines, jobs, towns, marriages — things measured in weeks and years, not minutes. Tonally it's mild and slightly homely, the kind of grumble you aim at your own existence ("my humdrum little life") rather than an insult you throw at someone else. Sharper GRE cousins — *insipid*, *banal*, *vapid* — carry contempt; *humdrum* just sighs.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Hum** and **drum** — two flat noises, one note each, no tune between them.

The word is literally the sound of something droning on unchanged, which is exactly what it accuses life of doing.

### In sentences
1. After the expedition, he found the **humdrum** rhythm of committee meetings and commuter trains almost unbearable.
2. Same desk, same sandwich, same bus home — it's all a bit **humdrum**, honestly.

# **insipid** *(adj.)* — **in-SIP-id**

### Means
Without flavour; by extension, without interest, character, or spark.

The word is a sneer. *Mild* and *plain* are neutral descriptions; **insipid** is a verdict — it says the thing *should* have had flavour and failed to deliver any. It works on soup, on prose, on a melody, on a person, and in every case it implies the critic tasted carefully and found nothing there. Slightly more literary and more withering than *boring*: you call a lecture boring, you call a novel insipid.

⚠️ **insipid ≠ insidious.** Both open *in-si-*, and there the resemblance ends: **insipid** = flavourless, dull; **insidious** = creeping and treacherous, harmful by stealth. An insipid remark bores you; an insidious one damages you quietly.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in-SIP-id** — take a **sip**, taste nothing, and say so with a curl of the lip.

The hidden *sip* fixes the tasting metaphor, and the curled lip fixes the contempt that separates *insipid* from a neutral word like *mild*.

### In sentences
1. Reviewers dismissed the novel as an **insipid** imitation of far braver books.
2. The tea was **insipid** — basically hot water with ambitions.

### In the wild
Standard wine- and food-critic vocabulary: an **insipid** wine is one with no acid, no fruit, nothing to say — the polite word for "watery."

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** (*not*) + **sapidus** (*tasty*), from **sapere** = *"to taste"* — and, because taste and judgement were one idea to the Romans, also *"to be wise."*

That double sense pays out across the exam: **sapid** (full of flavour), **savour**, **savoury**, **sage**, **sapient**, **Homo sapiens** — the "wise" ape, literally the one with taste.

# **loquacious** *(adj.)* — **loh-KWAY-shuss**

### Means
Very talkative; inclined to talk a great deal.

The word is itself too fancy to be innocent — calling someone **loquacious** is a dry, faintly amused way of saying they don't stop. It sits between neutral and critical: *talkative* is a plain fact, *garrulous* is a complaint (rambling, tedious, usually about the elderly), *voluble* praises fluency and speed, *verbose* attacks wordiness on the page. **Loquacious** describes a person in flow — often charming, occasionally exhausting — and lets the speaker stay polite while noting the excess. It also does duty as an ironic understatement: *"a loquacious witness"* means one who told the court far more than anyone asked.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Lo-QUACK-ious** — they *quack* on and on.

The buried "quack" catches both halves of the word: nonstop noise, and the mild comedy in how you'd describe it.

### In sentences
1. Ordinarily reserved, he became **loquacious** after the second glass of wine, holding the table for an hour on the subject of bridges.
2. Our cab driver was **loquacious** the entire way to the airport — I learned his whole divorce.

### Where it comes from
Latin **loquī** = *"to speak."* One of the highest-yield GRE roots going:

- **eloquent** — speaking *out* well
- **colloquial** — speech *with* others, i.e. casual
- **soliloquy** — speaking *alone*
- **circumlocution** — talking *around* the point
- **ventriloquist** — literally *"belly-speaker"*
- **grandiloquent** — speaking *grandly*, pompously

Spot *loqu-* or *locu-* and you're looking at a word about talking.

# **misanthropic** *(adj.)* — **mis-uhn-THROP-ik**

*The noun shifts its stress: misanthrope = MIZ-uhn-throhp.*

### Means
Hating, distrusting, or wanting nothing to do with **humankind in general**.

The scope is what defines it: this isn't a grudge against your neighbour, it's a low opinion of the whole species — people are selfish, stupid, and best avoided. It's a settled worldview rather than a mood, which is why it sounds almost philosophical, and why it's often used with a grin (*a misanthropic old bachelor who feeds the cat and no one else*). Compare **hostile** (aimed at someone), **cynical** (doubts motives, still shows up), **antisocial** (avoids people without necessarily despising them).

### Trick to lock it in
> **Phil**anthropic loves *anthropos*; **mis**anthropic hates him — same humans, opposite prefix.

Both words point at humanity as a whole, so the misanthrope doesn't hate *you* — he hates humans, and you happen to be one.

### In sentences
1. His later essays grew steadily more **misanthropic**, treating civilisation as a mistake that had merely taken a long time to become obvious.
2. Two days of airport queues and I was feeling pretty **misanthropic** by the end of it.

### In the wild
The **Grinch** is the pop-culture misanthrope in full: he doesn't have a quarrel with any particular Who, he just can't stand Whos.

### Where it comes from
Greek **misein** (*to hate*) + **anthrōpos** (*human being*).

Both halves pay off elsewhere: *misein* gives **misogyny** (+ *gynē*, woman) and **misandry** (+ *anēr*, man); *anthrōpos* gives **anthropology**, **anthropomorphic**, and — with *philein*, to love — **philanthropy**, the exact mirror image.

# **misnomer** *(n.)* — **mis-NOH-mer**

### Means
A **name or term that is wrong or misleading** for the thing it's attached to — *"jellyfish"* is a misnomer, since it's neither jelly nor fish.

The word polices **labels, not facts**. A misnomer says the *name* is inaccurate; it makes no claim that people are fooled or that anything important is at stake. Tone is dry and mildly pedantic — the voice of someone correcting a caption, not exposing a lie. It carries no accusation of dishonesty: most misnomers are historical accidents nobody bothered to fix.

⚠️ **misnomer ≠ misconception.**
- **misnomer** = the **name** is wrong (*"peanut" is a misnomer — it's a legume*)
- **misconception** = the **belief** is wrong (*that peanuts are nuts is a misconception*)

"It's a misnomer that goldfish have three-second memories" is the classic misuse — that's a misconception. No name is being challenged.

### Trick to lock it in
> **mis-NOM-er — a mis-*nom*. *Nom* is French for *name*, as in *nom de plume*.**

Hear the *nom* buried in the middle and the word tells you it's the label that's wrong, not the idea.

### In sentences
1. "Civil war" is something of a **misnomer** for a conflict fought largely by foreign mercenaries.
2. Calling that a starter salad is a **misnomer** — it's three olives and a leaf.

### In the wild
Voltaire on the **Holy Roman Empire**: *"neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire."* Three misnomers in one name.

### Where it comes from
Old French **mesnomer** = *mes-* ("wrongly") + **nommer** ("to name"), from Latin **nomen**, *name*.

That root is everywhere on the GRE: **nominate** (put a name forward), **nominal** (in name only), **nomenclature** (a naming system), **denomination**, **noun**, and **ignominy** (*ig-* + *nomen* — literally *namelessness*, i.e. disgrace).

# **negligent** *(adj.)* — **NEG-li-juhnt**

### Means
Failing to give something the care it required — **and being at fault for it.**

*Careless* describes a temperament; **negligent** delivers a judgement. The word presupposes a **duty owed** — a parent, a driver, a surgeon, a landlord — and says you fell below the standard that duty demanded. That's why it lives so naturally in courtrooms and disciplinary hearings: calling someone careless is a complaint, calling them negligent is an accusation of blame. It also implies *omission* rather than action: the negligent party didn't do something wrong so much as fail to do the right thing.

⚠️ **negligent ≠ negligible.**
- **negligent** = *culpably careless*
- **negligible** = *so small it can be ignored*

A negligent engineer; a negligible difference. The shared root is real — both are things *not picked up* — but one blames a person, the other dismisses a quantity.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Careless** is a habit; **negligent** is a verdict — you can only be negligent about something you were *supposed* to be doing.

Test it by asking "who was owed the care?" — if there's an answer, the word is *negligent*.

### In sentences
1. The inquiry found the shipping line **negligent** in its maintenance of the hull, and liability followed within the hour.
2. I was **negligent** with the houseplants for about ten days and now I own six sticks.

### In the wild
Every insurance policy, rental agreement and terms-of-service you've ever clicked through contains a **gross negligence** clause — the carve-out saying the company still pays if it was *really* their fault.

### Where it comes from
Latin **neglegere** = *nec* ("not") + *legere* ("to pick up, gather, read") — literally **to not pick something up**. What was lying there needing attention, you walked past.

*legere* is a workhorse root: **legible**, **lecture**, **elect**, **select**, **collect**, **intelligent** (picking *between* things).

Best of all, its exact opposite comes from the same place: **diligent** — the one who picks things up carefully.

# **obsequious** *(adj.)* — **uhb-SEE-kwee-uhs**

*Four syllables — the *-quious* is "kwee-uhs", not "kwuss".*

### Means
Excessively eager to serve, please, or agree — fawning, servile, all deference and no spine.

The word is never a compliment. **Deferential** is respectful, **attentive** is good service; **obsequious** is what you call it when the pleasing has gone past dignity and started to look like a strategy. It carries a whiff of self-interest — the flatterer wants something — and it describes how the behaviour *looks to a third party*, not how it feels to the person being flattered. Classic targets: the yes-man, the courtier, the waiter who hovers, the junior who laughs a beat too long at the boss's joke.

**obsequious ≠ obsequies.** *Obsequies* (OB-si-kweez) are **funeral rites** — the ceremony, not the crawling. Same Latin root, opposite worlds: one follows a coffin, the other follows a boss.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ob-SEE-kwee-us** — he'll do anything to **seek** to please **us**.

The buried "seek" is the whole nuance: this isn't natural politeness but pleasing that is aimed, effortful, and after something.

### In sentences
1. Every biographer notes the same failing: brilliant with his equals, **obsequious** the moment a duke entered the room.
2. He's not being nice, he's being **obsequious** — nobody compliments your parking that much for free.

### In the wild
Uriah Heep in Dickens's *David Copperfield*, forever wringing his hands and calling himself "ever so 'umble" — the textbook obsequious creep, and the humility is a con from the first page.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob-** ("toward") + **sequi** ("to follow") — literally *following close behind you*, which is exactly what the word still describes.

**sequi** is a high-yield GRE root: **sequel, sequence, consequence, subsequent, non sequitur** ("it does not follow"), and — with a sound shift — **pursue, persecute, prosecute**.

# **placate** *(v.)* — **pluh-KAYT**

*Some British speakers say PLAK-ayt.*

### Means
To calm someone's **anger or hostility**, usually by giving them something — a concession, an apology, a gesture.

The word assumes real anger already exists: you don't placate a happy person. And it quietly implies the fix is **transactional and often token** — "a few tweaks to placate the shareholders" says the tweaks were cheap and the shareholders were a nuisance. *Appease* carries the stink of surrendering to a bully; *mollify* is gentler and softer, about feelings; *placate* sits in between, faintly weary, faintly cynical about the person being soothed.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Plac-ate** = hand them a **plaque** and hope they **ate** it up.

You're buying off someone's anger with a token offering — which is exactly the slightly hollow flavour of *placate*.

### In sentences
1. The minister released a token draft of the report, hoping to **placate** a press corps that had scented a cover-up.
2. I bought him a coffee to **placate** him after I lost his charger.'

### Where it comes from
Latin **placāre** = *"to soothe"* — the causative of **placēre**, *"to please."* One root, a lot of GRE words: **placid** (calm), **complacent** (pleased with yourself), **placebo** (Latin for *"I shall please"* — a pill that soothes and nothing more), and the useful opposite **implacable** (*not* able to be soothed — a rage or an enemy that no concession will touch).

# **proclivity** *(n.)* — **pruh-KLIV-uh-tee**

### Means
A **natural, habitual leaning** toward some behaviour — an inclination you were born with or fell into, not one you chose.

The word carries a faint smell of vice. A *penchant* is for good coffee; a **proclivity** is for gambling, brawling, or lying, and it's the word prosecutors and psychiatrists reach for. It also implies the tendency is *settled and characteristic* — not a mood but a disposition. Used of something innocent ("a proclivity for crosswords"), it sounds either arch or faintly like an accusation.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Proclivity** hides **cliv-** = *slope* — and slopes run **downhill**.

You're already tilted that way and gravity does the rest, which is why proclivities tend toward the vices rather than the virtues.

### In sentences
1. The biography is candid about the composer's **proclivity** for ruinous debt.
2. He's got a real **proclivity** for picking fights he can't win.

### In the wild
Listen to any true-crime documentary or courtroom scene: nobody has a *liking* for anything — they have **\"violent proclivities.\"** The word almost never leaves that neighbourhood.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pro-** (*forward*) + **clivus** (*slope*) — literally "a slope forward," a tilt.

Same slope gives you **declivity** (a downward slope) and **acclivity** (an upward one), both GRE-worthy; the related root *clinare* ("to lean") gives **incline, decline, recline, proclivity**'s whole leaning family.

# **puerile** *(adj.)* — **PYOOR-ile**

*Also PYOO-uh-ruhl in American speech; the "pyoo" start is the same as in *pure*.*

### Means
Childishly silly — immature in a way that embarrasses adults: snickering, name-calling, toilet jokes, sulking.

The word is a verdict, not a description. **Childlike** is warm (wonder, innocence); **childish** is a plain complaint; **puerile** is the same complaint delivered from a height — it says the behaviour is beneath the speaker and beneath the occasion. It attaches especially to humour and to public conduct: a *puerile* stunt in parliament, a *puerile* joke in a eulogy. Note the sting works only against someone old enough to know better; a child cannot be puerile.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pue**rile — **poo** jokes, said by someone too grand to laugh at them.

The spelling hands you the schoolboy humour the word describes, and the sniffy tone of the person using it.

### In sentences
1. The committee dismissed his objection as **puerile** point-scoring unworthy of the office he held.
2. Grow up — arguing about who called shotgun is so **puerile**.

### In the wild
It's the film critic's standard sneer for gross-out comedy: a review calling a film "puerile" isn't saying it's for kids, it's saying grown men made it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **puer** = *"boy."* Puerile is literally *boyish* — which tells you why it's never a compliment about an adult. Same root in **puerperal** (*puer* + *parere*, "to bear") — puerperal fever, the infection of childbirth.

🚫 **Not related to *pure***, despite the sound. That's Latin *purus* — *purify, puritan, purge*.

# **quixotic** *(adj.)* — **kwik-SOT-ik**

*The man is kee-HOH-tay, but the adjective was anglicised centuries ago — say kwik-SOT-ik, never kee-HOH-tik.*

### Means
Idealistic to the point of impracticality — chasing a noble goal that reality will not allow.

The word carries **affection, not contempt**. Calling a plan *foolish* is a verdict; calling it **quixotic** grants the dreamer his gallantry and only regrets his aim. There's usually something chivalrous, self-sacrificing, slightly absurd about it — a one-man campaign against an entire industry, a doomed rescue mission. *Idealistic* praises; *delusional* diagnoses; *quixotic* smiles and shakes its head.

### Trick to lock it in
> **kwik-SOT-ik** — a **quick shot** at a windmill: brave, instant, and hopeless.

The sound of the word gives you both the charge and the futility of it — the aim is heroic, the target was never an enemy.

### In sentences
1. His **quixotic** campaign to restore the canal drew admiration from everyone and funding from no one.
2. Learning three languages before the trip was a bit **quixotic**, but you have to love him for trying.

### In the wild
**"The Impossible Dream"** — the standard from *Man of La Mancha*, sung by Don Quixote himself: *to fight the unbeatable foe*. That song is the definition set to music.

### Where it comes from
Straight from **Don Quixote**, the hero of Cervantes' 1605 novel — an ageing gentleman who reads too many chivalric romances, straps on rusted armour, and rides out to right the world's wrongs. His most famous act is charging a row of windmills he mistakes for giants.

That scene gave English a second phrase you already know: **tilting at windmills** — attacking imaginary enemies with total sincerity.

# **spendthrift** *(n. / adj.)* — **SPEND-thrift**

### Means
A person who spends money **extravagantly and wastefully** — and, as an adjective, the habit itself (*spendthrift ways, spendthrift heir*).

The disapproval is about **squandering**, not about amount. A billionaire buying a jet isn't a spendthrift; an heir burning through an inheritance he can never rebuild is. The word carries the sense of a finite pot being drained — which is why it clings to inheritances, trust funds, and lottery winners. Tone is faintly old-fashioned and more exasperated than damning: a raised eyebrow, not a moral charge.

### Trick to lock it in
> A spendthrift **spends the thrift** — the savings *are* the thing being burned.

The word looks like it should mean "frugal", but *thrift* here is the **object** of the spending, not the manner of it — so it means the exact opposite of *thrifty*.

### In sentences
1. The estate passed to a **spendthrift** nephew who had sold off the last of the paintings before the year was out.
2. One bonus and he turns into a total **spendthrift** — new speakers, new bike, rent still unpaid.

### In the wild
**Spendthrift trust** is a live term in estate law: money left to someone whose relatives assume they can't be trusted with a lump sum, so the trust pays it out in a controlled drip and creditors can't touch it. If a lawyer says it about you, it isn't a compliment.

### Where it comes from
**Thrift** originally meant *"thriving, prosperity, accumulated savings"* — it's the noun of **thrive**. So a spendthrift is literally one who **spends his thriving**.

Only later did *thrift* narrow into "frugality" (the habit that produces the savings), which is what makes the word look self-contradictory today. Same root: **thrive, thrifty, thrift shop**.

# **taciturn** *(adj.)* — **TAS-i-tern**

*Stress at the front, like tacit — not "tuh-SIT-urn".*

### Means
Habitually silent; saying little by temperament, not by mood.

This is a **standing character trait**, not a one-off silence. A *quiet* person happens not to be talking; a **taciturn** person is *built* that way — you could know him for ten years and still get three-word answers. It carries a faint chill: not shy, not sad, just closed. Applied to a friend it's affectionate-gruff; applied to a witness or a colleague it hints at something withheld.

⚠️ **taciturn ≠ reticent.**
- **taciturn** = silent *by nature*, about everything
- **reticent** = holding back on *one particular subject*

A taciturn man rarely speaks; a chatty man can still be reticent about his divorce.

### Trick to lock it in
> **TACIT**urn — *tacit* means unspoken, and the **-urn** ending makes it permanent, like *nocturnal* and *saturnine*.

The word literally has "tacit" sitting inside it, and the tacked-on ending turns a single unspoken thing into a lifelong disposition.

### In sentences
1. The new ambassador was **taciturn** to the point of discourtesy, offering nothing beyond the sentence his brief required.
2. My grandad's **taciturn** — you get "fine" for how was your day and that's the whole conversation.

### In the wild
Calvin Coolidge was nicknamed **"Silent Cal"** for exactly this. The famous story: a dinner guest told him she'd bet she could get more than two words out of him. His reply — *"You lose."*

### Where it comes from
Latin **tacēre** = *"to be silent"* — which also gives you **tacit** (understood without being said), **tacitly**, and **reticent** (*re-* + *tacēre*, to keep silent about). The Roman historian **Tacitus** carried the same name.

# **wary** *(adj.)* — **WAIR-ee**

*Rhymes with fairy — not with cheery.*

### Means
On guard; watchful because you suspect danger or a trick.

*Cautious* is a general policy — you're careful crossing any road. **Wary** is aimed at something in particular: a person, an offer, a promise that doesn't quite add up. It carries distrust, and it keeps its eyes open rather than backing away — you can be wary of someone and still shake their hand, watching the whole time. Slightly milder and more everyday than *leery*, more suspicious than *prudent*.

**wary ≠ weary.** *Wary* (WAIR-ee) = watchful, suspicious. *Weary* (WEER-ee) = exhausted. A wary traveller checks the locks; a weary traveller falls asleep on top of them.

### Trick to lock it in
> **bewar**e → be **war**y. Same word, one letter lighter.

*Beware* is the warning shouted at you; *wary* is the state you're in once you've heard it.

### In sentences
1. Investors remained **wary** of the company's projections, which had been revised upward three times in a single quarter.
2. He gave me a **wary** look, like he was still deciding whether I'd actually pay him back.

### Where it comes from
Old English **wær** = *"watchful, on one's guard."* The same root gives **aware** (watching outward), **beware** (be on guard), and the *-ward/guard* family — **ward, warden, guard, regard** all come from the sense of keeping watch over something.
