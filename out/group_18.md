# **abet** *(v.)* — **uh-BET**

### Means
To **help or encourage someone in doing something wrong** — supplying the means, the cover, or the push.

The word is welded to wrongdoing: you can assist a rescue, but you cannot *abet* one. And it isn't passive help — abetting leans toward **egging on**, the encouragement that makes the deed happen, which is why it sits one notch guiltier than "helped." In practice it rarely travels alone; the phrase is **aid and abet**, where *aid* covers the hands and *abet* covers the mouth.

⚠️ **abet ≠ abate.**
- **abet** = egg someone on into wrongdoing
- **abate** = die down, lessen (*the storm abated*)

### Trick to lock it in
> **A-bet**: you don't commit the crime — you *bet* on the guy who does.

The abettor stays one step back, funding or urging or driving the getaway car, which is exactly why the law needs a word other than *commit*.

### In sentences
1. The bank's compliance officer was accused of **abetting** the fraud by waving through transfers he knew were fictitious.
2. I'm on a diet, so don't **abet** me — hide the biscuits.

### In the wild
**"Aiding and abetting"** is the charge read out in every courtroom scene and police procedural — the standard formula for people who didn't pull the trigger but made sure it got pulled.

### Where it comes from
Old French **abeter** = *"to hound on, incite"* — literally **to bait**, as in setting a dog on something. Same root as English **bait** and **bite**: the abettor is the one snapping at your heels until you go through with it.

# **accessible** *(adj.)* — **uhk-SES-uh-buhl**

### Means
Able to be **reached, entered, obtained, or understood** — the barrier is low.

Three shades the GRE plays with. Physical: a road, a summit, a building. Available: records, funding, evidence *accessible to researchers*. And the one that actually gets tested — **intelligible to a non-specialist**: an accessible history of the Reformation is one a beginner can follow.

Applied to a person it means **approachable** — the professor who keeps real office hours, the boss who answers his own email. Praise, usually. But in criticism it can turn faintly dismissive: *accessible* poetry, *accessible* pop, meaning it asks nothing of you. The word never describes the seeker's effort, only the thing's openness.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-ible** sits on the **thing**: a book is accessible, a reader never is.

The suffix marks what *can be got at*, so the word always measures how open the object is, not how clever or determined you are.

### In sentences
1. Her lectures were prized for making quantum mechanics **accessible** without ever making it wrong.
2. He runs the whole company and he's still weirdly **accessible** — he'll actually reply to your email.

### In the wild
The **Accessibility** menu on your phone — bigger text, voice control, colour filters — is this word as a product feature: removing the barrier rather than blaming the user. Same idea as the ♿ symbol on a ramp.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** (*to*) + **cedere** (*to go, to yield*) → *accedere*, "to approach."

**cedere** is a GRE workhorse: **recede** (go back), **secede** (go apart), **precede** (go before), **concede** and **cede** (yield), **accede** (go along with), **exceed** (go beyond).

# **acquisitive** *(adj.)* — **uh-KWIZ-uh-tiv**

### Means
Keen to **get and keep more** — possessions, property, wealth, sometimes knowledge.

Where *greedy* is appetite and *grasping* is ugly, **acquisitive** is cold and settled: a standing disposition to accumulate rather than a flash of hunger. It's the word for the collector, the empire-builder, the person who converts every impulse into a purchase. Mildly disapproving of people; entirely neutral in business ("an acquisitive conglomerate") and even complimentary of a mind ("an acquisitive intellect" soaks things up).

⚠️ **acquisitive ≠ inquisitive.** *Acquisitive* wants to **own** it; *inquisitive* wants to **know** it. One letter apart on the page, opposite appetites.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Acquire** is sitting whole inside it — and *acquire* is a cold, transactional verb. Nobody "acquires" a sandwich.

That chill in *acquire* is exactly what separates acquisitive from plain greedy: accumulation as policy, not as hunger.

### In sentences
1. Gilded Age fortunes were built by acquisitive men who bought the railway, then the town the railway ran through.
2. Three years at that firm and he's gone properly acquisitive — everything's an "investment" now, even the watch.

### In the wild
**M&A** — *mergers and acquisitions*, the department whose entire job is buying other companies. Any financial paper describing a firm as "highly acquisitive" means exactly that: it grows by swallowing rivals.

### Where it comes from
Latin **quaerere** = *"to seek, to ask,"* plus *ad-* ("toward") → *acquirere*, to seek out and get.

That root is a GRE goldmine: **inquire, require, query, quest, conquest, request, perquisite** (your "perks"), **exquisite** (sought *out* — hence choice).

# **amalgamate** *(v.)* — **uh-MAL-guh-mayt**

### Means
To **combine separate things into one unified whole** — companies, departments, traditions, populations.

Weight matters here. *Mix* leaves the ingredients recognisable; *amalgamate* implies the parts genuinely fuse and stop being distinguishable — two banks amalgamate and there is now one bank, with one name and one balance sheet. It's a formal, institutional word: boards, regulators and historians amalgamate things, nobody amalgamates a salad. It also carries a faint sense of scale and officialdom that *merge* doesn't — which is why press releases love it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A MALL** is hiding in **a-mal-gamate** — a hundred separate shops pulled under one roof, trading as one address.

The point isn't that the shops sit near each other, it's that they've become a single entity with one name — which is exactly the fusion *amalgamate* demands.

### In sentences
1. The three parish schools were **amalgamated** into a single academy in 1974, and the old names survive only on the war memorials.
2. They **amalgamated** both teams into one after the layoffs — same work, half the people, new org chart.

### In the wild
The silver filling in a back tooth is called an **amalgam** — mercury dissolved into silver and tin until you can't tell which is which. Same word, same idea: not a mixture, a fusion.

### Where it comes from
Medieval Latin **amalgama**, "an alloy of mercury with another metal" (probably via Arabic *al-malgham*). Mercury is the key: it doesn't sit alongside another metal, it swallows it into one soft mass.

Family: **amalgam** (the alloy, and by extension any true blend), **amalgamation** (the corporate merger sense you meet in every business headline).

# **attenuate** *(v.)* — **uh-TEN-yoo-ayt**

### Means
**To weaken** — to reduce something in force, intensity, quantity, or value. Literally, to make thin or slender.

This is the cool, technical word for weakening. Engineers attenuate signals, virologists attenuate viruses, lawyers say a fact attenuates a claim. Two things are built in: the reduction is **gradual, along a scale**, and the thing **survives** — what's attenuated is still there, just fainter. *Undermine* hints at sabotage, *diminish* is plain and general; *attenuate* is measured, usually deliberate, and sounds like it came off a lab report.

⚠️ **attenuate ≠ accentuate.** Two letters apart, near-opposite meanings: **attenuate** turns the volume *down*, **accentuate** turns it *up* (to emphasise). Getting them backwards flips the whole sentence.

### Trick to lock it in
> at**TENU**ate hides *tenu-* = **thin**: it thins a thing out, it never cuts it off.

An attenuated signal is still a signal — stretched thin, not severed.

### In sentences
1. Distance and two concrete walls **attenuated** the transmission until the broadcast was nothing but hiss.
2. Coffee and a big breakfast **attenuated** the hangover, but only just.

### In the wild
The MMR and yellow-fever shots are **live-attenuated** vaccines: the virus isn't killed, it's weakened until it's survivable — the definition of the word doing its job on a label you've actually read.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ad-** ("to") + **tenuāre** ("make thin"), from **tenuis** = *"thin."*

Same root gives you **tenuous** (a thin, barely-holding argument) and **extenuate** — literally *to thin out the blame*, which is exactly what **extenuating circumstances** do. English **thin** is a distant cousin of the same ancient root.

# **augment** *(v.)* — **awg-MENT**

### Means
To **make something greater by adding to it** — in size, amount, strength, or value.

The key restriction: there must already be something there. You create from nothing, but you *augment* a base that already stands — a salary, a force, a collection, a signal. **Increase** is the plain all-purpose word; **augment** is its formal cousin and usually implies a deliberate addition brought in from outside. It also differs from **supplement**, which quietly implies the original was deficient; augmenting carries no such insult — you can augment something already perfectly good.

### Trick to lock it in
> Nobody augments from zero — an augmented *thing* is a thing that was already there.

That base requirement is the whole line between *augment* and *create*: you add to, never start.

### In sentences
1. The bequest **augmented** an endowment that was already the largest in the country.
2. He drives weekends to **augment** his paycheck.

### In the wild
**Augmented reality** — Pokémon GO, Snapchat filters. The camera keeps your actual room and lays extra things on top of it; that's exactly the word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **augēre** = *"to increase, to make grow."* It pays out across the exam: **auction** (bidding drives the price up), **auxiliary** (extra troops added to the main force), **august** / **Augustus** (grand, magnified), and **author** (Latin *auctor*, the one who makes a thing grow).

# **aversion** *(n.)* — **uh-VER-zhun**

*Also heard as uh-VER-shun; both are fine.*

### Means
A strong, settled **dislike** of something — and the wish to **stay away from it**.

Hatred wants to destroy; aversion just wants distance. It is a recoil, not an attack, and it is usually aimed at a *thing or activity* rather than a person: an aversion to conflict, to paperwork, to cold water. Cool and slightly clinical in tone — it's the word a psychologist or an economist reaches for (*aversion therapy*, *risk aversion*), which is why it can describe a gut-level revulsion without the speaker sounding heated. Note the fixed preposition: an aversion **to**, never *of*.

⚠️ **averse ≠ adverse.**
- **averse** (*to*) = a person **unwilling**, disliking — *she's averse to flying*
- **adverse** = conditions that are **harmful or unfavourable** — *adverse weather, adverse effects*

People have averse feelings; circumstances turn adverse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Aversion** hides **avert** — you *avert your eyes*, you don't raise your fists.

The word is about turning away from something, not going to war with it.

### In sentences
1. He had a lifelong **aversion** to publicity, refusing every interview the prize brought him.
2. I don't hate the gym, I've just got a serious **aversion** to anyone watching me lift.

### In the wild
Every hiring manager and investor says **"risk-averse"** — the person who turns away from the risky option rather than fighting it. Behavioural economics adds **loss aversion**: we feel a £100 loss more sharply than a £100 gain.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ab-** (*away*) + **vertere** (*to turn*) = "a turning away."

*Vertere* is a GRE workhorse: **avert** (turn away), **divert** (turn aside), **invert**, **revert**, **convert**, **subvert**, **controversy** (turned against each other), even **vertigo** (the spinning world).

And it explains the trap: **adverse** is *ad-* (*toward*) + *vertere* — conditions turned **against** you.

# **blithe** *(adj.)* — **BLEYEDH**

*One syllable, rhyming with lithe/scythe. Many Americans harden the ending to a "th" as in thin (BLEYETH) — both are accepted.*

### Means
**Cheerful and carefree** — and, in most modern use, *carefree about something you should have taken seriously*.

The old sense is pure sunshine: light-hearted, unclouded, happy. The GRE sense has an edge on it. Look at the company it keeps — **blithe disregard, blithe indifference, blithe assumption, blithely ignored**. That pairing tells you the whole story: the cheerfulness is real, but it's cheerfulness made possible by not looking. *Careless* is just sloppy; *blithe* is sloppy **while smiling**, which is what makes it damning. The word rarely accuses someone of malice — it accuses them of a lightness that costs other people something.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Blithe** hides **lithe** — it glides lightly over everything, including the thing it should have stopped for.

The lightness is the point: a blithe person doesn't push through the problem, they skim right past it without noticing it was there.

### In sentences
1. The inquiry condemned the ministry's **blithe** assumption that the flood defences would hold for another decade.
2. He **blithely** told me the flights were "basically sorted" — three days before we were meant to leave.

### In the wild
Noël Coward's comedy **Blithe Spirit** — a man haunted by the ghost of his first wife, who is cheerfully, maddeningly unbothered by the chaos she causes. Coward lifted the title from Shelley's skylark: *"Hail to thee, blithe Spirit!"*

### Where it comes from
Old English **blīþe** = *"joyous, gentle, kind"* — the same root that gives you **bliss**. Blithe is bliss worn on the outside: bliss is the feeling, blithe is the manner. The disapproving edge is a modern addition; the root itself is entirely sunny.

# **contempt** *(n.)* — **kuhn-TEMPT**

### Means
The feeling that a person or thing is **worthless, beneath serious consideration** — scorn delivered as a verdict.

Anger says *you wronged me*; contempt says *you are beneath me*. That's the register difference: hatred grants the enemy status, contempt withholds it. It runs cold and looks downward, which is why it reads as arrogance in the person feeling it. Note the fixed phrases: to **hold someone in contempt**, and **beneath contempt** — so worthless it doesn't even merit the sneer.

Second, legal sense: **contempt of court** is open defiance or disrespect of a court's authority, and it will get you jailed.

⚠️ **contemptuous ≠ contemptible.**
- **contemptuous** = *feeling* it — the sneering one
- **contemptible** = *deserving* it — the despicable one

A contemptuous critic; a contemptible excuse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-uous feels it, -ible earns it:** a contempt**uous** judge holds a contempt**ible** man in contempt.

The same suffix split runs through *pitiable / pitying* and *despicable / disdainful* — *-ible* always points at the target, not the sneerer.

### In sentences
1. He treated the committee's findings with undisguised **contempt**, not troubling to read past the first page.
2. She didn't even argue with him — just a little laugh, pure **contempt**, and walked off.

### In the wild
Every courtroom drama's "**I'll hold you in contempt!**" — the judge's threat when someone won't stop talking back. Real procedure, and the one place the word gets shouted.

### Where it comes from
Latin **contemnere** = *"to scorn, to think nothing of"* (*con-* intensive + *temnere*, "to despise").

🚫 **Nothing to do with *tempt*.** That's Latin *temptare*, "to test, to try" — *attempt, temptation*. The shared spelling is an accident.

# **dawdle** *(v.)* — **DAW-duhl**

### Means
To move or do something **far more slowly than needed**, drifting along, wasting time without meaning to.

The tone is mild and often exasperated-but-fond — it's the word for children trailing behind on the walk home, not for serious obstruction. A *procrastinator* never starts; a *loiterer* hangs about one spot and looks slightly suspicious; a **dawdler is genuinely on the way**, just taking forever about it. There's no defiance in it, only aimlessness, which is why "stop dawdling" is a nudge rather than a rebuke.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dawdle** rhymes with **waddle** — you *are* moving, just uselessly slowly.

The rhyme locks in the key nuance: a dawdler hasn't refused the task or stopped in place, they're waddling toward it at a useless pace.

### In sentences
1. He **dawdled** over the last chapter for three months, rereading paragraphs he had already approved.
2. Don't **dawdle** — the film starts in ten minutes and you're still looking for socks.

# **deflect** *(v.)* — **dih-FLEKT**

### Means
To turn something **aside from its course** — a bullet, a blow, a beam of light. Figuratively: to fend off criticism, blame, or an awkward question by **steering attention elsewhere**.

The key is that nothing is stopped or absorbed — it is *angled away*. A blocker takes the hit; a deflector lets it glance off. That's why the figurative use carries a faint whiff of evasion: you *rebut* a charge by answering it, you **deflect** it by making it someone else's problem. Compared with *dodge* or *duck*, though, deflect sounds practised and controlled — the politician who deflects is smooth, not panicking.

**deflect ≠ defect** — one letter apart. **Deflect** = bend aside. **Defect** *(n.)* = a flaw; *(v., dee-FEKT)* = to desert your country or side.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Re**flect bends it **back**; **de**flect bends it **away** — either way, it never sticks to you.

Both share *flect* ("bend"), so the prefix does all the work: *re-* returns the thing to its source, *de-* just sends it off sideways.

### In sentences
1. Pressed on the missing funds, the minister **deflected** the question toward his predecessor and moved briskly on.
2. Every time I ask him about the wedding date he **deflects** — suddenly he's very interested in the dog.

### In the wild
**Deflector shields** in *Star Wars* and *Star Trek*: they don't absorb incoming fire, they bend it away from the hull — the word's literal job, in one bit of sci-fi hardware.

### Where it comes from
Latin **de-** (*away*) + **flectere** (*to bend*). That root is a GRE goldmine: **flexible** (bendable), **reflect** (bend back), **inflection** (a bend in a curve, or in the voice), **genuflect** (bend the knee), **flexor** (the muscle that bends a joint).

# **discount** *(v. / n.)* — **dis-KOWNT**

*Verb: dis-KOWNT. Noun (the price cut): DIS-kownt.*

### Means
The GRE sense is not shopping. To **discount** a claim, a rumour, a witness, or a theory is to **treat it as less important than it appears** — to knock down the weight you give it.

The force of the word is *partial*. You aren't calling it false; you're marking it down. A historian discounts a chronicle because the author had an axe to grind — the chronicle still gets used, just cautiously. Compare the neighbours: *refute* kills a claim with argument, *dismiss* waves it away entirely, *discount* quietly reduces its value. It also carries a faint suggestion that the discounter may be **too quick** — "he discounted her warning" almost always ends badly.

### Trick to lock it in
> A shop discount takes money **off the price**; a mental discount takes credibility **off the claim**.

Same move either way — the thing is still there, just worth less than it was asking for.

### In sentences
1. Later scholars **discounted** his memoir, noting that every page positioned him beside a great man at a decisive hour.
2. Yeah, I'd **discount** anything he says about the restaurant — his brother owns it.

### In the wild
Finance uses the word in exactly this sense: a **discounted** cash flow values money arriving in ten years at less than money arriving today, and when analysts say "the market has already discounted the news," they mean it has been weighed and marked down, not ignored.

### Where it comes from
Old French *desconter*, from Latin **dis-** (*away*) + **computare** (*to reckon, to count up*) — literally *to count away*, to subtract from the reckoning.

*Computare* is a workhorse root: **compute**, **computer**, **count**, **recount**, **account**. Keep the literal image and the abstract sense falls out — discounting an argument is subtracting it from your total.

# **dissident** *(n. / adj.)* — **DIS-i-duhnt**

### Means
**Noun:** someone who openly opposes an official policy or ruling power. **Adj.:** holding and voicing such opposition.

The word carries **risk**. Anyone can be a critic; a dissident disagrees with a government, church, or party that can *punish them for it* — exile, prison, a lost career. Compare the neighbours: a **rebel** takes up arms, a **heretic** offends doctrine, a **contrarian** disagrees for sport. The dissident's weapon is only speech, and the price is personal. Note also who's speaking: the regime says "dissident" to mark you as a problem; the outside world says it to mean you're brave.

⚠️ **dissident ≠ diffident.**
- **dissident** = *loudly opposed to authority*
- **diffident** = *shy, lacking self-confidence*

Nearly the opposite temperament, one letter apart.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Dis-SID-ent — he sits apart** (*sedere*, to sit), and in a police state an empty chair gets noticed.

The word isn't about arguing, it's about visibly refusing to sit with everyone else — which is why it's dangerous.

### In sentences
1. Samizdat manuscripts circulated hand to hand, keeping **dissident** thought alive through decades of censorship.
2. She was the one **dissident** voice on a board that had already decided everything over lunch.

### In the wild
The phrase you've read a hundred times in headlines: **"Russian dissident Alexei Navalny"** — poisoned, jailed, and dead in an Arctic prison. That's the whole meaning of the word in three words of news copy.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dis-** (*apart*) + **sedēre** (*to sit*) — literally *"to sit apart."*

That root is a GRE goldmine: **reside** (sit back), **preside** (sit in front), **subside** (settle down), **sedentary** (sitting), **sediment** (what sits at the bottom), **assiduous** (sitting at it doggedly), **insidious** (sitting in ambush).

# **efficacious** *(adj.)* — **ef-ih-KAY-shuhs**

*The noun shifts the stress right back: efficacy = EF-ih-kuh-see, never "eh-FIK-uh-see."*

### Means
Capable of producing the intended effect — it **actually works**.

Where *effective* is the everyday word for anything that works, **efficacious** is the lab-coat version, reserved almost entirely for **remedies and measures**: a drug, a treatment, a vaccine, a policy, a ritual. You'd call a manager effective; calling him efficacious sounds like you're prescribing him. It also carries a whiff of *proven under test conditions* rather than merely useful in practice — which is exactly why medicine loves it.

⚠️ **efficacious ≠ efficient.**
- **efficacious** = it produces the result
- **efficient** = it produces the result *without waste*

A treatment can be efficacious and wildly inefficient: it cures you, at ten thousand pounds a dose.

### Trick to lock it in
> The **-acious** family is all about being *full of* something — aud**acious**, ten**acious**, vor**acious**. Efficacious = full of effect.

Once you hear the suffix as "bursting with," the word stops being a fancy synonym and becomes a claim that the thing is loaded with actual working power.

### In sentences
1. Trials found the compound **efficacious** against the resistant strain, though its side effects kept it off the market.
2. Honestly, tea and eight hours' sleep is the most **efficacious** thing I've tried for a hangover.

### In the wild
The 2020–21 vaccine headlines ran on this word's noun form — "95% **efficacy** in trials." That's the natural habitat: a number from a controlled study, not a vibe.

### Where it comes from
Latin **efficere** = *"to bring about"* — from *ex-* ("out") + *facere* ("to make, to do").

*Facere* is one of the great GRE roots: **factory, manufacture, benefactor, facile, feasible, affect, effect**. Anything with *-fac-, -fic-, -fect-* is doing or making something.

# **equitable** *(adj.)* — **EK-wi-tuh-buhl**

*Stress on the first syllable, never on "quit" — not "ee-QUIT-uh-buhl."*

### Means
**Fair and impartial** — dealing with everyone justly, giving each party what they are properly due. In law, it also means *relating to equity*: the branch of justice that softens rigid rules to reach a fair result.

The word most people reach for instead is *fair*, but **equitable** is the courtroom-and-boardroom register: it implies a **considered judgement about who deserves what**, not a warm feeling. And it is not a synonym for *equal*. Equal means identical shares regardless of circumstance; equitable means shares calibrated to circumstance — which is often deliberately unequal. A settlement can be perfectly equal and grossly inequitable.

⚠️ **equitable ≠ equable.**
- **equitable** = *fair, just* (EK-wi-tuh-buhl)
- **equable** = *even, unvarying* — an equable temper, an equable climate (EK-wuh-buhl)

A judge is equitable. A judge who never loses her temper is equable.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Equal** cuts the cake into identical slices; **equitable** cuts it so everyone leaves fed.

The whole exam value of the word lives in that gap — equitable answers to *fairness*, not to *sameness*.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal's task was to devise an **equitable** division of the estate, weighing each heir's contribution rather than simply counting heads.
2. Splitting the bill down the middle isn't **equitable** when I had a salad and you had three cocktails.

### In the wild
The **"E" in DEI** — *diversity, **equity**, inclusion*. Organisations swapped "equality" for "equity" precisely to make this distinction: not the same treatment for everyone, but fair treatment given where each person starts.

### Where it comes from
Latin **aequus** = *"level, even."* From it: **equal**, **equity**, **adequate**, **equanimity** (a level mind), **equilibrium**, **equinox** (day level with night).

The high-value one is the negative: **in- + aequus → iniquity**, literally *un-levelness* — which is why "iniquity" means wickedness rather than mere inequality.

🚫 Nothing to do with **equestrian** or **equine** — those are *equus*, "horse."

# **erratic** *(adj.)* — **ih-RAT-ik**

### Means
Irregular and unpredictable — moving, behaving, or performing with no consistent pattern.

The complaint isn't that something is *bad*, it's that you can't rely on it. An erratic student aces one paper and fails the next; an erratic pulse skips and races. The word implies a baseline that *should* be steady, and isn't — so it carries a faint suggestion of instability, even mental instability, when applied to a person ("erratic behaviour" is what witnesses report before something goes wrong).

**erratic ≠ erroneous.** Both come from *errare*, but **erratic** = *inconsistent* (wandering), **erroneous** = *wrong* (mistaken). An erratic calculator sometimes gives you the right answer; an erroneous one never does.

### Trick to lock it in
> Er-**RAT**-ic — watch a **rat** cross a room: darting, doubling back, no straight line anywhere.

The stressed syllable hands you the image, and a rat's path is the exact quality of the word — not wrong, just impossible to predict.

### In sentences
1. The composer's late works are **erratic** — a masterpiece and a shapeless fragment written in the same month.
2. His sleep schedule's been so **erratic** lately that he's basically living in a different time zone every day.

### In the wild
"Driving **erratically**" is the standard phrase in police reports and news bulletins — swerving, speeding up, braking for nothing. It's the everyday use that fixes the meaning: not necessarily illegal, just unpredictable enough to alarm everyone nearby.

### Where it comes from
Latin **errāre** = *"to wander."* Not "to be wrong" originally — the wrongness came later, from the idea of straying off the path.

That root fans out usefully: **err**, **error**, **erroneous** (strayed into wrongness), **errant** / **knight-errant** (literally a wandering knight), **aberrant** (wandered *away* from the norm).

Geologists still use the old sense: an **erratic** is a boulder a glacier carried miles from its home rock and abandoned in a field where it doesn't belong.

# **industrious** *(adj.)* — **in-DUS-tree-uhs**

### Means
Hardworking in a steady, energetic, productive way — someone who is always at it and has the output to show for it.

Compare the neighbours: *diligent* stresses care and attention to detail, *assiduous* stresses stubborn persistence, *industrious* stresses **sheer productive activity** — work getting done, visibly and continuously. It is always praise, but praise for effort rather than brilliance, which is why it can land as a consolation prize: the industrious student is the one who grinds, not the one who dazzles.

⚠️ **industrious ≠ industrial.**
- **industrious** = *a person* who works hard
- **industrial** = *of factories*, manufacturing, heavy production

An industrious worker; an industrial estate. Never the reverse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **An industrious person is a one-man industry** — always something in production.

The word literally has *industry* inside it, and the point is output: not just trying hard, but steadily turning out work.

### In sentences
1. Over three industrious decades she built a modest practice into the largest firm in the county.
2. He's not the cleverest on the team, but he's dead industrious — first one in, last one out.

### In the wild
It's the classic school-report word: *"a quiet, industrious pupil"* — teacher code for works hard, isn't a star. That double edge is the whole flavour of the word.

### Where it comes from
Latin **industria** = *"diligence, purposeful activity."*

The useful bit: **industry** originally meant *hard work*, not factories — that older sense survives fossilised in *industrious*. Learn it that way and *industrious* vs *industrial* stops being confusing.

# **inform** *(v.)* — **in-FORM**

### Means
**Everyday sense:** to tell someone something — pass on facts.

**The sense the GRE actually tests:** to **pervade and give shape to** something from the inside. A conviction, an experience, a tradition *informs* a body of work — it isn't mentioned in it, it's the thing quietly determining its form.

The difference is direction. Information is handed *over*, external and detachable; when grief **informs** a painter's late canvases, it's soaked *in*, inseparable, visible in every choice. So "a deep suspicion of authority informs his journalism" is not saying anyone told him anything. Note the third sense too — **inform on/against** someone is to snitch, and that one is squarely negative.

### Trick to lock it in
> **FORM** is sitting right there in the middle of it — to in-**form** is to put form *into* a thing.

Whatever informs a work is the invisible mould it was shaped in, not a message delivered to it.

### In sentences
1. A stubborn empiricism **informs** every argument in the book, even where the author never names it.
2. Three years of waiting tables **informs** how much I tip, honestly.

### In the wild
Corporate and design-speak leans on exactly this sense all day: *"the user research **informs** the redesign," "these findings will **inform** our strategy."* Nobody was told anything — it means shaped the outcome.

### Where it comes from
Latin **informāre** = *"to give form to, to shape"* — **in-** (into) + **forma** (form). The "tell someone" meaning is the later, watered-down one; shaping came first.

Same root, free of charge: **form, conform** (shape yourself to fit), **reform** (shape again), **deform, formation, formula, format**.

# **irksome** *(adj.)* — **IRK-suhm**

### Means
Annoying, tedious, wearing — something that grates by being petty and repeated.

Scale matters: *infuriating* is a fire, *irksome* is a drip. Irksome things are never dramatic — a form you must re-sign, a colleague who clears his throat, a rule that adds one pointless step. It's also a slightly bookish word, so it carries a note of restraint: the speaker is registering annoyance while making clear they are too civilised to shout about it.

**irksome ≠ irascible.** *Irksome* describes a **thing that annoys you**; *irascible* describes a **person quick to anger**. The irascible boss finds everything irksome.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Irk** rhymes with **quirk** — irksome things are irritating quirks, not catastrophes.

The rhyme pins the word to the low end of the annoyance scale: small, recurring, wearing, never enraging.

### In sentences
1. The committee's habit of minuting every trivial decision was **irksome**, though no member was rude enough to say so.
2. Having to log in twice every single morning is just **irksome**.

### In the wild
Headline-ese lives on the verb: *"Tariff plan irks allies."* Newspapers reach for **irks** because it's four letters and means "annoyed, but not a crisis" — exactly the word's register.

# **manacle** *(n. / v.)* — **MAN-uh-kuhl**

*Stress the first syllable — not muh-NAK-ul.*

### Means
**Noun:** an iron cuff for the **wrists**, usually one of a joined pair. **Verb:** to chain someone's hands — or, figuratively, to bind and hobble them.

Handcuffs are police procedure; **manacles** are dungeons, slave ships, chain gangs. The word carries weight, rust and cruelty, and it belongs to the wrists specifically — **fetters** go on the ankles. Used figuratively, it names a constraint *imposed by an oppressor*, never a mild self-inflicted inconvenience: you are manacled by debt or by law, not by a busy calendar.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Man**acle = **manual** + sh**ackle** — the iron that owns your hands.

Manual work is hand work, so a manacle binds the hands — and whatever manacles you has taken away your power to act.

### In sentences
1. Prisoners were led from the hold still **manacled**, wrists raw where the iron had sat for weeks.
2. Three loans and a lease — he's completely **manacled** to that job.

### In the wild
Martin Luther King Jr., *I Have a Dream*: the Black American is "still sadly crippled by the **manacles** of segregation and the chains of discrimination."

### Where it comes from
Latin **manus** = *"hand"*, via *manicula*, "little hand." One root, a large family: **manual**, **manuscript** (hand-written), **manufacture** (hand-made), **manicure**, **manipulate**.

Best pairing: **manacle** and **emancipate** are opposites from the same root — *ex + manus + capere*, "to take out of the hand" of an owner. One puts the iron on; the other takes it off.

# **modest** *(adj.)* — **MOD-ist**

### Means
Three related senses, and the GRE leans on the second one:

1. **Unassuming** — not boastful about your abilities or achievements.
2. **Moderate in size or amount** — *a modest raise, a modest apartment, modest gains*.
3. **Decorous** — restrained in dress or behaviour.

All three share one idea: **staying within measure, not overreaching.** Sense 2 is where students get caught, because it's evaluative and slightly deflating — a "modest improvement" is real but underwhelming, and calling a house modest is a polite way of saying small. Sense 1 is warm praise, but only when the modesty looks genuine; *falsely modest* is a standing accusation. Compare **humble**, which can imply low status or self-abasement — modest implies you *have* something worth boasting about and decline to.

⚠️ **modest ≠ modish.** *Modish* (**MOH-dish**) means *fashionable, in vogue* — from *mode* as in style. One letter apart, near-opposite vibe: the modest dresser avoids attention, the modish dresser chases it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Modest** hides **mode** — you keep to the *measure*: modest ego, modest salary, modest hemline.

One idea covers all three senses — nothing exceeds its proper size, whether it's your self-regard, your paycheque, or your outfit.

### In sentences
1. Despite two decades at the frontier of the field, she remained **modest** about her contribution, crediting her collaborators at every turn.
2. It's a **modest** flat — one bedroom, no balcony — but the rent is half what I was paying.

### In the wild
Swift's **"A Modest Proposal"** (1729) — the essay that suggests the Irish poor sell their babies as food. The title is the whole joke: *modest* promises something small and reasonable, and the proposal is monstrous.

### Where it comes from
Latin **modus** = *"measure, limit."* A modest person keeps within the measure.

That root is worth banking: **moderate, modify, modulate, mode, modicum** (a small measure), **commodious** (well-measured, roomy), **accommodate**.

# **noxious** *(adj.)* — **NOK-shuhs**

### Means
Harmful to health or life — **poisonous, injurious**. Most often of fumes, gases, chemicals, weeds; by extension of ideas and influences that corrupt (*a noxious ideology*).

The word claims real damage, not mere unpleasantness. A smell can be disgusting without being noxious; noxious fumes actually injure the lungs. Note the contrast with its own relative: an **obnoxious** guest is unbearable, a **noxious** gas is lethal — one offends, the other harms. When applied to ideas, the metaphor is deliberately medical: the belief is treated as a toxin seeping into people.

⚠️ **noxious ≠ noisome.**
- **noxious** = *harmful* (it damages you)
- **noisome** = *foul-smelling* (it offends your nose)

And *noisome* has nothing to do with noise — it's from *annoy*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Strip the *ob-* off *obnoxious* and it stops being annoying and starts being deadly.**

Same root, harder edge: obnoxious hurts your patience, noxious hurts your body.

### In sentences
1. The inquiry found that workers had been inhaling **noxious** vapours for years without protective equipment.
2. Don't run the generator in the garage — you'll fill the house with **noxious** fumes.

### In the wild
Governments publish official **noxious weed** lists — plants legally classed as harmful enough to be banned from sale. Not weeds that look untidy; weeds that damage crops, livestock or people. That's the whole word in one phrase.

### Where it comes from
Latin **nocēre** = *"to harm."* It runs through a useful family: **innocuous** (*in-* + *nocuous* = not harmful), **innocent** (doing no harm), **nuisance** (via French *nuire*), and **obnoxious** (originally *exposed to harm*).

🚫 **Not Latin *nox*, "night."** That gives *nocturnal, equinox*. Same-looking syllable, unrelated word.

# **pernicious** *(adj.)* — **per-NISH-uhs**

### Means
Causing serious harm — but **gradually, quietly, and often invisibly** until the damage is done.

The word is almost never used for a punch, a bomb, or a car crash. It's reserved for the slow poison: a **pernicious influence**, a **pernicious doctrine**, a **pernicious habit**, a **pernicious myth**. *Harmful* states the fact; *destructive* is loud and visible; **pernicious** adds the creep — it works on you while you're not looking, and it's usually said with moral disapproval by someone educated and alarmed.

⚠️ **pernicious ≠ perfidious.**
- **pernicious** = quietly *destructive* (a harmful effect)
- **perfidious** = treacherous, *faithless* (a betrayer)

A perfidious ally stabs you in the back; a pernicious rumour rots you from the inside.

### Trick to lock it in
> It has **"nice"** sitting right in the middle — per-**NICI**-ous — and that's the whole point: it looks nice while it kills you.

Pernicious harm is the kind that doesn't announce itself, so the friendly-looking spelling matches the word's disguise.

### In sentences
1. The report identified social media's **pernicious** effect on adolescent attention spans, accumulating year by year until it was mistaken for normal.
2. Skipping breakfast started as a time-saver, but it turned into a **pernicious** little habit that wrecked my afternoons.

### In the wild
**Pernicious anaemia** is still the standard medical name for B12-deficiency anaemia — christened before the cause was known, when it was a disease that quietly wasted you away and then killed you. The label survives as a fossil of exactly this meaning: slow, hidden, fatal.

### Where it comes from
Latin **per-** (*thoroughly*) + **nex, necis** (*violent death*) → **perniciosus**, "thoroughly deadly."

That death-root reappears in **internecine** (mutually destructive — *inter* + *necāre*, to kill) and, via its Greek cousin *nekros*, in **necrosis** and **necromancy**.

# **predicament** *(n.)* — **pri-DIK-uh-muhnt**

### Means
A difficult, awkward or unpleasant situation — especially one you're **stuck in**, where every way out costs you something.

Scale matters: a *problem* is something to solve, a *crisis* is an emergency, a *predicament* is a **bind**. It's mid-sized trouble with a whiff of the wry — you can be in a predicament and still be embarrassed rather than terrified. It also carries a faint hint that you walked into it yourself, which is why "he's got himself into a right predicament" sounds natural and "he's got himself into a right catastrophe" doesn't.

### Trick to lock it in
> **pre-DIC-a-ment** ≈ **"predict a mess"** — you can see the mess coming and still can't step out of it.

The sound hook also fixes the size of the trouble: a *mess*, not a disaster.

### In sentences
1. Caught between a contract he could not honour and a client he could not afford to lose, the young architect described his **predicament** with careful understatement.
2. I'd told both of them I was free on Saturday — so yeah, bit of a **predicament**.

### Where it comes from
Latin **praedicāre** = *"to proclaim, declare publicly"* (prae- "before" + dicāre "to make known"). Same root gives you **preach** and **predicate** — all about asserting something about someone.

It began as dry logic jargon: a *praedicamentum* was one of Aristotle's **categories**, a state or condition you could assert of a thing. Neutral. Then English speakers started using "what a predicament!" about *unfortunate* conditions, and the sourness stuck to the word permanently.

🚫 **Not from *predict*.** That's *prae-* + *dīcere* ("to say beforehand") — a cousin, not a parent. A predicament isn't a forecast; it's a fix you're already in.

# **proficient** *(adj.)* — **pruh-FISH-uhnt**

### Means
Competent at something through training and practice — you can do the thing reliably and well.

Note where it sits on the scale: above *adequate*, well below *masterful*. **Proficient** is earned, unglamorous praise — it implies drilled skill rather than talent, and it quietly stops short of brilliance. Call a surgeon *proficient* and you've said she won't kill you; call her *virtuosic* and you've said something else entirely. It also wants an object — you're proficient *in* French, *at* soldering — where *skilled* or *able* can stand alone.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pro-fic-ient** rhymes with **suf-fic-ient** — and that's exactly the level: sufficient skill, not stunning skill.

The rhyme pins the ceiling of the word — solidly good enough, never dazzling.

### In sentences
1. Two years in Lisbon left him **proficient** in Portuguese, though he still lost every argument in it.
2. She's **proficient** enough with Excel to fake her way through the interview.

### In the wild
LinkedIn and CV language levels: *Elementary → Limited working → **Professional working proficiency** → Full professional → Native*. School test rubrics do the same — *Below Basic, Basic, **Proficient**, Advanced* — where "Proficient" is literally the band just under the top one.

### Where it comes from
Latin **proficere** = *"to make progress, get ahead"* (**pro-** forward + **facere** to make/do) — so a proficient person is one who has *advanced*, not one born able.

The **-ficient / -fect** family from *facere* is GRE gold: **efficient** (working well), **sufficient** (enough made), **deficient** (falling short), **munificent**, **perfect**, **profit** (also from *proficere* — what advances you).

# **prolix** *(adj.)* — **PROH-liks**

*proh-LIKS is also heard; the noun is prolixity — proh-LIK-si-tee.*

### Means
Using far **too many words** — long-winded to the point of tedium.

The complaint is length, not content: a prolix argument may be perfectly sound, just drowned. It belongs to **prose and formal speech** rather than chatter — *garrulous* is the neighbour who won't stop talking, *prolix* is the memo that takes four pages to say no. It's a critic's word, cool and bookish, and it always disapproves.

⚠️ **prolix ≠ prolific.**
- **prolix** = too many *words* (an insult)
- **prolific** = produces a lot of *work* (a compliment)

A prolific novelist writes twenty books; a prolix one writes a thousand pages of the first.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Prolix** ≈ **prolongs** — it prolongs the sentence, the speech, the meeting; never the point.

The fault named by *prolix* is purely duration: the idea arrives eventually, but only after everyone has stopped listening.

### In sentences
1. The judgment was learned but hopelessly **prolix**, burying a simple finding under ninety pages of citation.
2. His toast was so **prolix** that the starters went cold before anyone got to drink.

### In the wild
Polonius in *Hamlet* is prolixity itself — he rambles his way to "brevity is the soul of wit," and Gertrude cuts him off with **"More matter, with less art."**

### Where it comes from
Latin **prolixus** = *"poured forth, stretched out"* — **pro-** (forth) + **liquere** (to flow), the root of **liquid**, **liquor**, **liquefy**. A prolix writer's words simply keep flowing out.

# **scorn** *(n. / v.)* — **SKORN**

### Means
**Noun:** open contempt — the feeling that something or someone is beneath consideration, *and* the show of it.
**Verb:** to treat with that contempt; also, to reject something flatly as unworthy ("she scorned the offer").

Contempt can sit silently behind a polite face, and disdain is cool and aloof — a raised eyebrow, a turned shoulder. Scorn is the loud one: it curls the lip, it speaks. You **pour** scorn, **heap** scorn, laugh someone to scorn. And its second sense adds a refusal — to scorn help isn't merely to despise it, it's to push it away.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Scorn** scorches — disdain is cold, scorn burns.

The near-rhyme fixes the temperature: this is contempt that comes out hot and audible, not the chilly silence of disdain.

### In sentences
1. The pamphlet was met with **scorn** by the very clergymen it hoped to persuade.
2. He **scorned** the umbrella, walked out, and came back looking like a drowned cat.

### In the wild
**"Hell hath no fury like a woman scorned"** — the most-quoted line in English about being rejected with contempt (Congreve's original: *"nor hell a fury like a woman scorned"*).

# **subordinate** *(adj. / n. / v.)* — **suh-BOR-din-uht**

*Noun and adjective: suh-BOR-din-uht. Verb: suh-BOR-din-AYT.*

### Means
**Adj./noun:** lower in rank; a person who works under someone else's authority.
**Verb:** to place or treat something as less important than something else.

The word is neutral and structural — it describes a position on an org chart, not a character flaw. A **subordinate** simply reports to someone; a **subservient** person grovels. Keep those apart: rank vs. temperament.

The verb is where the GRE lives, and it almost always takes **to**: you *subordinate X to Y*, meaning you deliberately let X lose so Y can win. There's a cost implied — something real gets sacrificed to the thing ranked above it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **-AYT does it; -uht *is* it.** Same split as *sepa-RAYT / SEP-ruht*, *dele-GAYT / DEL-uh-guht*, *moder-AYT / MOD-uh-ruht*.

The full **-AYT** ending is always the action you perform, while the mumbled **-uht** is the person or the state that results.

### In sentences
1. The treaty **subordinated** every commercial interest to the single goal of preventing another war.
2. She's technically my **subordinate**, but she's been here twenty years and everyone asks her first.

### In the wild
Every grammar lesson you've ever sat through: a **subordinate clause** (*because it was raining*) can't stand on its own — it has to hang off a main clause. The name tells you its rank.

### Where it comes from
Latin **sub-** (*under*) + **ordinare** (*to arrange in rank*), from **ordo, ordinis** = *order, row, rank*. Literally: put in the row beneath.

That root pays out across the test: **ordain**, **ordinary**, **coordinate** (*co-* = arranged at equal rank), **insubordinate** (refusing to sit below), and best of all **inordinate** — *outside the order*, hence excessive.

# **unseemly** *(adj.)* — **un-SEEM-lee**

### Means
Not proper, not fitting, offending against good taste or dignity — especially given the occasion, the setting, or the person's position.

The offence is against **decorum, not morality**. An unseemly act doesn't harm anyone; it *looks bad* — grabbing at an inheritance before the funeral, a judge shouting in court, two ministers bickering on camera. That's why the word is a favourite of polite disapproval: it's genteel, slightly old-fashioned, and used by someone who is embarrassed for you rather than angry at you. Compare **disgraceful** (moral condemnation) and **rude** (blunt, personal): *unseemly* is cooler and more institutional — a raised eyebrow, not a raised voice.

**unseemly ≠ unsightly.** *Unsightly* = ugly to look at (a scar, a heap of rubbish). *Unseemly* = improper to be seen doing. An unsightly building; an unseemly argument.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Un-SEEM-ly** — it doesn't *seem* right. Not wrong in the soul, wrong in the room.

The hidden **seem** is the whole point: the word judges how a thing appears to onlookers, so it condemns bad taste rather than sin.

### In sentences
1. The heirs began dividing the estate with an **unseemly** haste that the family solicitor found difficult to watch.
2. Maybe don't text your ex at your brother's wedding — it's a bit **unseemly**.

### In the wild
Political and legal reporting runs on two set phrases: **"unseemly haste"** and **"an unseemly row"** — the press's polite way of saying *this looked awful* without alleging anything actionable.

### Where it comes from
From Old Norse **sœmiligr**, "becoming, fitting," from **sœma** = *to befit*. That's the same root as **seem**, which originally meant "to be fitting" — the modern sense "appear to be" came later.

So the spelling isn't a coincidence you have to memorise: *seemly* = as it should appear, *unseemly* = not as it should appear.

# **veritable** *(adj.)* — **VER-i-tuh-buhl**

### Means
Used before a noun to insist that a strong description — usually a metaphor or an exaggeration — is **fully deserved**: *a veritable army of lawyers*, *a veritable goldmine*.

It is almost never a plain synonym for *true* or *real*. Its job is licensing: the speaker reaches for an over-the-top comparison and then plants *veritable* in front of it to say "and I stand by that." Drop it and the sentence still works — *an army of lawyers* — so what it adds is stance, not information. Tone is formal-genteel and faintly theatrical; it's the voice of the restaurant review and the auction catalogue, which is why overuse reads as purple prose.

⚠️ **veritable ≠ venerable.**
- **veritable** = *genuinely deserving the name* (a veritable institution = really is one)
- **venerable** = *old and revered* (a venerable institution = ancient and respected)

One letter apart in the middle, and both love the word *institution*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Veri**table = ***very*-table** — it doesn't say *true*, it says *take my exaggeration as true*.

The "veri" is the truth root, but in practice the word works as an intensifier bolted onto a metaphor.

### In sentences
1. The archive proved a **veritable** goldmine: three unpublished letters surfaced in the first afternoon.
2. By eight o'clock her desk was a **veritable** graveyard of coffee cups.

### In the wild
It's the house style of food and travel writing — "a **veritable** feast," "a **veritable** paradise." If you've read a menu blurb or a Sunday supplement, you've met it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vērus** = *"true"*, via **vēritās** = *"truth."* That one root pays out across the exam: **verify**, **verity**, **veracious** (truthful), **aver** (to assert as true), **verdict** (*vērē dictum*, "truly said"), and **verisimilitude** (the look of truth).

Harvard's crest reads **VERITAS** — same root, same three letters at the front.
