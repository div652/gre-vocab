# **abstruse** *(adj.)* — **ab-STROOS**

*Rhymes with loose, not with lose.*

### Means
Hard to understand because the subject matter is deep, technical, or specialised — the kind of thing you can't follow without the right training.

*Obscure* just means little-known; **abstruse** means the difficulty is in the content itself. A footnote on medieval tax law is obscure; a proof in category theory is abstruse. It nearly always carries a faint complaint — the speaker suspects the difficulty could have been avoided, that the writer went abstruse when plain would have done.

**abstruse ≠ obtuse.** *Abstruse* describes the **material** (too deep to follow); *obtuse* describes the **person** (too dull to follow it). "An abstruse lecture" is a compliment to the topic; "an obtuse student" is an insult.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Abstruse** = a **recluse** of an idea — it lives shut away where only specialists visit.

The rhyme fixes both the sound (-*STROOS* / -*CLOOS*) and the nuance: the meaning isn't lost, it's holed up behind jargon.

### In sentences
1. His later papers grew so **abstruse** that even colleagues in the field read them with a pencil in hand.
2. Skip chapter four — it gets **abstruse** fast and you don't need any of it for the exam.

### Where it comes from
Latin **abs-** (*away*) + **trūdere** (*to thrust, push*) — literally *pushed out of sight*, hidden away.

That verb is quietly everywhere: **intrude** (thrust in), **protrude** (thrust forward), **extrude** (thrust out), **obtrude** (thrust in someone's face) — and **unobtrusive**, which doesn't push itself on you at all.

# **auxiliary** *(adj. / n.)* — **og-ZIL-yuh-ree**

*The x is a "gz" sound; note the single L — auxiliary, not "auxillary".*

### Means
**Adj.:** giving supplementary support; secondary, held in reserve. **Noun:** the helper itself — a reserve force, a supporting organisation, or (in grammar) a helping verb.

An auxiliary thing is subordinate in *rank*, not in *quality*: it's fully capable, just not the main event — the second engine, the backup generator, the volunteer battalion. That's what separates it from **ancillary**, which is peripheral, off to the side, doing the menial support work. Auxiliary power can run the ship; ancillary staff mop the deck. The word is institutional and faintly technical — you meet it in engineering, the military, and grammar rather than in conversation.

### Trick to lock it in
> **AUX** is sitting right at the front of it — the aux input, the spare way in when the main source can't do the job.

Nobody plugs into aux first, but it's there, wired up and ready — exactly the standing-by-in-support sense of the word.

### In sentences
1. When the grid failed, the hospital's **auxiliary** generators carried the surgical wing through the night.
2. The fire service runs on an **auxiliary** crew of volunteers who show up when the sirens go.

### In the wild
Grammar hands you the word daily: *be, do, have, will* are the **auxiliary verbs** — they carry almost no meaning themselves, they just let the main verb work ("she **has** finished", "I **do** know").

### Where it comes from
Latin **auxilium** = *"help, aid"*, from **augēre** = *"to increase"* — an auxiliary force is one that *adds to* your strength.

That root pays off elsewhere: **augment** (increase), **auction** (bids going up), **author** and **authority** (from *auctor*, one who makes things grow), **august** (grand, enlarged in dignity).

# **caricature** *(n. / v.)* — **KA-ri-kuh-cher**

*Four syllables — the -ca- in the middle doesn't disappear.*

### Means
**Noun:** a portrait or description that **exaggerates distinctive features** for comic or satirical effect. **Verb:** to depict someone that way.

The key is that a caricature never *invents* — it **amplifies**. The nose was always big; the cartoonist just gave it three more inches. That's why it stings: you can't fully deny it.

Used figuratively, it's an **accusation**. "That's a caricature of my position" means: you took something real, blew one part out of proportion, and attacked the result. Compare **parody**, which mimics a *style*, and **satire**, which has a target and a moral point — caricature is narrower and more personal, aimed at a face, a manner, a person.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A caricature can't make it up — it can only turn it up.**

Every feature in a caricature is genuinely there; only the volume is dishonest.

### In sentences
1. The paper's cartoonist **caricatured** the chancellor as a stooped clerk buried under his own ledgers.
2. Come on — that's a **caricature** of what I said, and you know it.

### In the wild
The seaside-pier or theme-park artist who'll draw you in ten minutes with a giant head, tiny body and one enormous grin — that's the trade name for exactly this: a caricature.

### Where it comes from
Italian **caricare** = *"to load, to pile on"* (from Latin **carrus**, a wagon). A caricature is a face with too much freight on it.

Same root, quietly everywhere: **cargo**, **charge**, **carriage**, **car**, **chariot**, **discharge**, **surcharge** — all about loading and hauling. A *surcharge* is an extra load of money; a *caricature* is an extra load of nose.

# **depravity** *(n.)* — **dih-PRAV-ih-tee**

*Second syllable rhymes with *have*, not *brave*.*

### Means
Deep, thoroughgoing **moral corruption** — wickedness that has settled into the character rather than flared up once.

The word is heavy and judgemental, with a pulpit or courtroom behind it. *Immorality* is a category, *vice* is a habit, but **depravity** says the rot goes all the way down: this person is not tempted, they are spoiled. It's the term reached for in cases of cruelty, perversion, and appetite indulged past any restraint — never for a small lapse. Calling someone's conduct depraved marks the speaker as morally appalled, not merely disapproving.

⚠️ **depravity ≠ deprivation.**
- **depravity** = you are morally *rotten* (Latin *pravus*, crooked)
- **deprivation** = you have been *denied* something (Latin *privare*, to strip)

Slum children suffer deprivation; the men who exploit them show depravity.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Depravity rhymes with gravity — a constant moral falling, not one stumble.**

Gravity pulls always and everywhere, which is exactly the claim the word makes: the corruption is permanent and total, not an isolated slip.

### In sentences
1. The report catalogued a **depravity** the committee's members had not believed possible in a licensed institution.
2. He watched two hours of the show and declared the whole thing a monument to human **depravity** — then queued up episode three.

### In the wild
**"Depraved indifference"** — the US homicide standard you've heard every prosecutor say on *Law & Order*: killing not by intent but by a recklessness so total it shows contempt for human life.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pravus** = *"crooked, bent"*, with **de-** as an intensifier: utterly bent. English morality runs on the straight/crooked metaphor — **upright**, **rectitude**, **straight-laced**, **correct** — and *depravity* is that line warped out of true.

# **dilettante** *(n. / adj.)* — **DIL-uh-tahnt**

*Also heard dil-uh-TAHNT, stress on the last syllable. Plural often dilettanti (dil-uh-TAHN-tee).*

### Means
Someone who dabbles in an art or subject **for pleasure and without serious commitment** — enough knowledge to talk at a party, not enough to do the work.

The sting is not incompetence but **pretension**: an *amateur* simply isn't paid, and can be devoted and excellent; a **dilettante** performs expertise he hasn't earned. Calling someone a dilettante says they're playing at it. As an adjective — *a dilettante interest in physics* — it means shallow, easily dropped.

**dilettante ≠ dilatory.** *Dilatory* (**DIL-uh-tor-ee**) means **slow, delaying, dragging your feet** — a dilatory tenant hasn't paid rent. Same opening syllable, unrelated roots. The dilettante does it badly; the dilatory man hasn't got round to doing it at all.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **dilettante** is a **debutante** of the arts — same *-tante* ending, same instinct: turn up for the party, leave before the work.

The rhyme carries the contempt, which is the part the definition alone misses.

### In sentences
1. Critics dismissed him as a **dilettante** — three exhibitions, four abandoned novels, and no discernible craft behind any of them.
2. Don't ask me, I'm a total **dilettante** — I've watched two YouTube videos on fermentation and now I own a crock.

### Where it comes from
Italian **dilettante** = *"one who delights (in something),"* from Latin **delectāre**, *to delight*. It began as a compliment — an eighteenth-century gentleman who loved painting or music without earning from it — and curdled into an insult once *professional* became the word of praise.

That root is still visible in **delight, delectable, delectation** — which is the memory key: a dilettante is in it purely for the **delight**, and quits the moment it stops being fun.

# **effrontery** *(n.)* — **ih-FRUN-tuh-ree**

*Not "EFF-ron-tree" — the first syllable is a mumbled uh-, and the stress lands on FRUN.*

### Means
Shameless, insolent boldness — nerve of a kind that ought to have produced a blush and didn't.

Audacity can be admiring; effrontery never is. It always carries the speaker's outrage at someone's gall — the offence isn't the act alone but the barefaced confidence with which it was done. Note the collocation: it lives almost entirely in **"had the effrontery to —"**, and whatever follows that phrase is something the speaker considers indefensible. Formal, faintly indignant, the register of a judge or an affronted letter to the editor.

**effrontery ≠ affront.** Same root, one letter apart. An **affront** is the insult itself (*he took it as an affront*); **effrontery** is the shamelessness of the person delivering it. You *offer* an affront; you *have* the effrontery.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Effrontery** is in-your-**FRONT**-ery — done to your face, without flinching.

The word wears **front** in the middle of it, which is exactly where the nerve is: brazen, frontal, no shame and no retreat.

### In sentences
1. Having lost the case, the defendant had the **effrontery** to demand that the court cover his legal fees.
2. He turned up two hours late and then had the **effrontery** to complain the food was cold.

### Where it comes from
French **effronté** = *shameless*, from Latin **frons, frontis** = *forehead, brow* — literally "without a brow," someone whose face cannot blush.

That root is a workhorse: **front**, **frontal**, **confront** (brow to brow), **frontier** (the facing edge), **affront** (a slap to the face), **effrontery** (a face that won't redden).

# **encroach** *(v.)* — **en-KROHCH**

### Means
To intrude **little by little** on what belongs to someone else — their land, rights, authority, or time.

An invasion is announced; an encroachment is noticed late. The word carries **gradualness and creep**: a hedge that grows six inches a year, a department quietly absorbing another's work, the sea taking the coast. Because the advance is incremental, no single step looks outrageous — which is exactly why the word implies a slow theft that should have been stopped earlier. Almost always used by the side being pushed back, and almost always with resentment.

Standard collocation: you encroach **on** (or **upon**) something.

### Trick to lock it in
> Hidden inside en**croach** is **roach** — a roach never bursts in, it just gets a little further in each night.

Encroaching is pest-like advance: unauthorised, incremental, and noticed only once it is well inside.

### In sentences
1. The court held that the boundary wall **encroached** upon a metre of the adjoining plot and ordered it demolished.
2. Work's started **encroaching** on my weekends and I'm putting a stop to it.

### In the wild
Municipal **anti-encroachment drives** — the bulldozers that clear illegal structures and stalls off public land — are a permanent fixture of local news headlines.

### Where it comes from
Old French **encrochier** = *"to hook in, seize"*, from **croc**, a **hook**. You don't march in — you hook a bit of the neighbour's land and drag it your way.

Same hook gives you **crochet** (a little hook), **crook** (a hooked staff — and the man who hooks what isn't his), and **croquet** (hooked mallet).

# **endow** *(v.)* — **en-DOW**

### Means
Two linked senses:

**1.** To give a large sum of money or property to an institution, so that it produces income permanently — *to endow a hospital, a scholarship, a professorship.*
**2.** To furnish someone with a quality or ability from birth — *endowed with perfect pitch.*

Both senses are grand and one-directional. You *give* someone a fiver; you **endow** a university — the word implies size, permanence, and a giver with means. In sense 2 the gift comes from nature, God, or luck, never from effort: being endowed with a talent is the opposite of having earned it, which is why the word so often shades into "born lucky."

### Trick to lock it in
> An **endow**ment is built never to **end** — you live off the interest and never touch the gift.

The "end" sitting inside the word flags the key nuance: this isn't a donation that gets spent, it's a permanent capital gift.

### In sentences
1. The alumna **endowed** a chair in medieval history, stipulating that the post could never be abolished.
2. He's **endowed** with the kind of memory that makes revision look easy — the rest of us have to work.

### In the wild
The US Declaration of Independence: *"...that they are **endowed** by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights."* Sense 2 exactly — gifts you're born holding, not ones you're granted.

### Where it comes from
Old French *endouer*, from Latin **dos, dotis** = *"dowry"* — the property that came with a bride. That root gives you **dowry**, **dower**, and **dowager** (the widow living on her dower). Hear the *dow* in **en-dow** and the money is right there in the word.

# **entreat** *(v.)* — **en-TREET**

### Means
To **ask someone earnestly and humbly** for something — to plead, to beg with feeling.

The word is dignified begging. *Beg* can be abject and *implore* is nearly frantic, but **entreat** keeps its manners: it's the plea of someone who has no power in the situation but has not lost their composure — a petitioner, a wife, a subject addressing a king. It carries urgency without hysteria, and it usually rides on affection or respect rather than desperation. Faintly archaic now, so in modern prose it sounds deliberately elevated.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Entreat** = beg so politely you're practically asking to be **treated** kindly.

The word *treat* sits right inside it, and that's the tone — pleading with courtesy, not grovelling.

### In sentences
1. The ambassador **entreated** the council to delay the vote until the survivors could be heard.
2. She **entreated** me not to tell her mother, and honestly, I wasn't going to anyway.

### In the wild
One of the most quoted lines in the King James Bible, Ruth to Naomi: *"**Entreat** me not to leave thee, or to return from following after thee."* Still read at weddings and funerals.

### Where it comes from
Old French **entraitier** = *"to deal with, negotiate"* — from Latin **tractare**, *"to handle,"* and behind it **trahere**, *"to drag, draw."*

To entreat was originally to *treat with* someone — to negotiate — and only later narrowed to the pleading half of a negotiation. That root pays off everywhere: **treat, treaty, treatise, tract, traction, extract, retract, protract**.

# **gregarious** *(adj.)* — **gruh-GAIR-ee-uhs**

### Means
Fond of company; sociable by nature — happiest in a crowd. In biology, it also means *living in flocks or herds* (gregarious geese, gregarious ants).

This describes appetite, not manners. **Affable** means pleasant when approached; **gregarious** means going out to do the approaching — the person who takes an empty evening as a problem to be solved. It's a temperament word, warm and faintly bookish, and it says nothing about whether someone is charming, only that people are what recharges them.

⚠️ **gregarious ≠ egregious.** Same flock-root, opposite lives: *e-gregious* is *out of* the flock (**e-** = out), and now means conspicuously, shockingly **bad** — an egregious error. *Gregarious* is squarely in the flock, and is a compliment.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Gregarious** = *gathers-various* — various people, gathered, on purpose.

The gregarious one doesn't merely tolerate the crowd, they go and assemble it.

### In sentences
1. Beneath the diplomat's **gregarious** manner lay a professional's instinct for who in the room was worth knowing.
2. He's so **gregarious** he'll come back from a five-minute coffee run with two new friends.

### In the wild
Nature documentaries run on this word — flamingos, starlings and wildebeest are all described as **gregarious species**, meaning they simply don't live alone.

### Where it comes from
Latin **grex, gregis** = *"flock, herd."* The flock root pays for itself across the exam: **congregate** (flock together), **segregate** (flock apart, *se-* = aside), **aggregate** (add to the flock), **egregious** (out of the flock).

# **indictment** *(n.)* — **in-DYTE-muhnt**

*The c is completely silent — never "in-DIKT-ment." Rhymes with excitement.*

### Means
**Legal:** a formal written charge accusing someone of a serious crime, handed down by a grand jury.
**General:** a fact, result, or statement that stands as **damning evidence** against something — *"the report is an indictment of the whole system."*

An indictment is an accusation, not a conviction: it opens the case, it doesn't close it. The general sense keeps that courtroom weight — calling something an indictment doesn't merely criticise it, it presents it as proof of guilt. So *criticism* is an opinion offered; an *indictment* is a charge that the evidence itself makes. Almost always preceded by **damning**, **stinging**, or **searing**.

### Trick to lock it in
> **In**dictment opens the trial; **ver**dict closes it — same *dict*, opposite ends.

Both are things spoken (*dict*) by the court, but the indictment is only the charge going in, while the verdict is the truth (*ver*) coming out.

### In sentences
1. A grand jury returned a twelve-count **indictment** against the former treasurer, though he was later acquitted on every charge.
2. That the hospital ran out of beds in week one is a pretty damning **indictment** of the planning, isn't it?

### In the wild
Journalism runs on the phrase **"a damning indictment of..."** — you'll see it in a news headline or op-ed most weeks, always about a failing system rather than a person in the dock.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dicere / dictāre** = *"to say, to proclaim"*; *in-dictāre* = to proclaim against. The silent **c** was stuffed back in by medieval scribes to make the word look properly Latin — same vanity that gave **debt** its silent *b*.

That root is a GRE workhorse: **verdict** (truth-said), **edict**, **dictate**, **contradict**, **jurisdiction**, **benediction**, **malediction**, **dictum**.

# **indignant** *(adj.)* — **in-DIG-nunt**

### Means
Angry — but specifically **angry at something you consider unjust, unfair, or insulting to you**. Noun: *indignation*.

*Furious* is about heat; *indignant* is about **standing on your rights**. The indignant person isn't just upset, they're claiming the moral high ground: *how dare you*. It carries a whiff of wounded dignity, which is why it works both ways — sympathetic when the grievance is real, faintly comic when it isn't (the indignant customer who was in fact in the wrong queue).

⚠️ **indignant ≠ indigent.**
- **indignant** = *offended, morally angry*
- **indigent** = *desperately poor*

One letter, two unrelated words. An indigent man may well be indignant, but they are not the same claim.

### Trick to lock it in
> **in-DIGN-ant** — someone stepped on your **DIGNITY**.

The word wears its own explanation: this is anger on behalf of your worth, not just bad temper.

### In sentences
1. The minister was **indignant** at the suggestion that the report had been altered before publication.
2. She sent back an **indignant** three-line email: she had paid, and she had the receipt.

### Where it comes from
Latin **in-** (*not*) + **dignus** (*worthy*) → *unworthy* — an indignity is treatment beneath you, and indignation is the reaction to it.

*Dignus* is a generous root: **dignity, dignify, deign** (*condescend to think someone worthy*), **disdain** (*think them un-worthy*), **indignity**.

🚫 **Not *indigent*** — that's Latin *indigere*, "to lack." Different root entirely.

# **ineluctable** *(adj.)* — **in-ih-LUK-tuh-buhl**

### Means
Impossible to escape or avoid; certain to happen however hard you fight it.

*Inevitable* is the everyday word and can be cheerful — an inevitable promotion, an inevitable sequel. **Ineluctable** is the high-literary cousin, and it carries the sense of a **struggle already lost**: the thing is closing in, you've tried, it's coming anyway. Death, decay, consequences, fate. Use it about tax deadlines and you sound like you're being funny — which is one way people do use it.

⚠️ **ineluctable ≠ ineffable.** Same shape, different word: *ineffable* = **too great to be put into words** (ineffable joy). *Ineluctable* = **impossible to escape** (ineluctable decline).

### Trick to lock it in
> **in-e-LUCK-table** — no amount of **luck** gets you off this table.

The stressed syllable literally sounds like *luck*, and luck is exactly what won't save you.

### In sentences
1. The report traced the **ineluctable** decline of the empire from its first unpaid legion to its last.
2. Stay up till 3 a.m. and the **ineluctable** result is a wasted Tuesday.

### In the wild
Joyce opens the Proteus chapter of *Ulysses* with **"Ineluctable modality of the visible"** — Stephen shutting his eyes on the beach and finding the world still there. It's the most quoted appearance the word has.

### Where it comes from
Latin **luctārī** = *"to wrestle."* *Ē-luctārī* is to wrestle your way **out** of something; put *in-* on the front and you can't.

That root pays off elsewhere: **reluctant** is *re-* + *luctārī* — someone **wrestling back against** being made to do it. Not shy, not lazy: resisting.

# **inquisitive** *(adj.)* — **in-KWIZ-uh-tiv**

### Means
Curious — specifically, curious in a way that **asks**. Where *curious* is a state of mind, **inquisitive** is a behaviour: the questions actually come out of your mouth, one after another.

That's what tips it. A child, a scientist and a nosy neighbour can all be inquisitive, and the word leans warm or cold depending on whether the questions are welcome. Praise on a CV ("an inquisitive mind"), mild complaint at a dinner party ("she was rather inquisitive about my salary"). It never reaches the flat rudeness of *nosy*, but it's the polite word you reach for when *nosy* is what you mean.

**inquisitive ≠ inquisitorial.** *Inquisitive* asks out of interest; **inquisitorial** interrogates out of suspicion — the tone of the Inquisition, harsh and accusing. A toddler is inquisitive; a hostile cross-examination is inquisitorial.

### Trick to lock it in
> **inQUIZitive** — there's a **quiz** hiding in it, and you're the one being quizzed.

The buried *quiz* captures the whole nuance: not silent wondering but out-loud questioning, which is delightful for about four questions and then starts to feel like a test.

### In sentences
1. Darwin's genius was less raw brilliance than an **inquisitive** patience — he would worry at a single earthworm for years.
2. Sorry, I don't mean to be **inquisitive**, but how much did you actually pay for it?

### In the wild
Job ads and appraisals run on it — "we're looking for an **inquisitive** mind" is corporate for *asks good questions and won't just do what they're told*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **quaerere** = *"to seek, to ask."* It pays for itself many times over: **query, quest, question, inquire, require, acquire, conquer, exquisite** (literally "sought out"), and the GRE favourite **perquisite/prerequisite**.

Spot *quir / quisit / quer / ques* in a word and the idea underneath is almost always *seeking*.

# **latitude** *(n.)* — **LAT-uh-tood**

*Also LAT-uh-tyood in British speech.*

### Means
Freedom to act, choose, or interpret as you see fit — **room to move within the rules**.

Latitude is almost always **given**, not taken: a boss, a court, a coach, an editor grants it. That's what separates it from *license*, which suggests freedom already run to excess. Latitude still implies a boundary somewhere out there — you're being handed a wide field, not an open one. Common in formal and professional registers: *considerable latitude*, *allowed wide latitude in sentencing*.

⚠️ **latitude ≠ lassitude.**
- **latitude** = *room to manoeuvre*
- **lassitude** = *weariness, listless lack of energy*

One letter apart, opposite worlds — a manager grants latitude; a heatwave induces lassitude.

### Trick to lock it in
> **LAT** is **FLAT** — latitude lines run flat and wide across the globe, and latitude is the wide flat space you're allowed to roam in.

Both senses come from the same idea of *breadth*, so the map lines you already know hand you the abstract meaning for free.

### In sentences
1. Appellate courts have historically granted trial judges wide **latitude** in matters of evidentiary procedure.
2. They gave me a lot of **latitude** on how to run the team, as long as the numbers landed.

### In the wild
The line every manager eventually says — *"I'll give you some latitude on the deadline"* — meaning: bend it, don't break it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **lātus** = *"broad, wide"* → *latitudo*, breadth. Same root gives **dilate** (*spread wide*) and **latitudinarian** (*broad-minded, tolerant of doctrinal difference* — a GRE word in its own right).

# **levity** *(n.)* — **LEV-uh-tee**

### Means
**Lightness of manner** — humour, joking, or a refusal to be solemn, *especially at a moment that called for seriousness*.

The word almost never stands alone: it exists in opposition to gravity. You only reach for *levity* when there is weight in the room — a funeral, a trial, a board meeting, a crisis — and someone punctures it. That makes it two-faced. From a disapproving speaker it means **unseemly frivolity** ("this is no occasion for levity"); from a grateful one it means **welcome relief** ("a moment of levity broke the tension"). The lightness is fixed; whether it's a sin or a mercy depends entirely on who's judging.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Levity levitates** — it lifts the mood off a heavy moment.

Same **lev-** = *light*, and the image carries the nuance: something heavy was sitting there until the joke floated it away.

### In sentences
1. The chairman's attempt at **levity** during the layoff announcement was met with silence.
2. Ten hours into the drive, any **levity** helped — even Dad's terrible puns.

### In the wild
Harry Potter's **Wingardium Levi*osa*** — the charm that makes a feather float. Same **lev-** root: *light enough to rise*.

### Where it comes from
Latin **levis** = *"light in weight"* — the direct opposite of **gravis**, *"heavy,"* which gives you **gravity**, **grave**, and **grievous**. Learn the pair and you get both halves of the seesaw.

**lev-** also pays out across the list: **alleviate** (lighten a burden), **elevate**, **levitate**, **lever**, **relieve**.

# **malevolent** *(adj.)* — **muh-LEV-uh-luhnt**

### Means
Wishing harm on others; full of ill will.

This is about **disposition, not deed**. A malevolent person may never lift a finger — the evil is in what they *want* for you. It's a cold, formal word, heavier than *mean*, *nasty* or *spiteful*, and it attaches to a whole character rather than one bad afternoon. It also works on things that have no hands: a malevolent presence, a malevolent silence, a malevolent stare. *Malicious* labels a specific act (malicious gossip, malicious code); **malevolent labels a nature**.

**malevolent ≠ maleficent.** *Malevolent* = wishing evil; *maleficent* = **doing** evil. Disney's Maleficent didn't just resent the baby — she cast the curse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Male-VOL-ent** hides *vol*, as in **volition** and **volunteer** — it's about what someone **wills**, not what they've done yet.

Lock onto that middle syllable and you'll always place the word in the mind rather than in the crime.

### In sentences
1. The report describes not incompetence but something **malevolent**: a system designed to make appeals fail.
2. He wasn't joking — that was a genuinely **malevolent** little smile.

### In the wild
Draco **Malfoy** — *mal foi*, "bad faith." Rowling loads her villains with the same **mal-** prefix (Malfoy, malediction, the Dark Mark's whole vocabulary of ill-wishing), and Malfoy is exactly the type: mostly sneering intent, rarely the one holding the wand.

### Where it comes from
Latin **male** (*badly*) + **volens** (*wishing*, from **velle**, "to will") — literally **bad-wishing**.

Flip the first half and you get its exact opposite: **bene**volent, *well-wishing*. Keep the second half and you get **volition**, **voluntary**, **volunteer** — all the will-words. Keep the first and you get **mal**ice, **mal**ign, **mal**ady, **mal**function.

# **mediate** *(v. (also adj.))* — **MEE-dee-ayt**

### Means
To step **between two opposed parties** and help them reach agreement. Also, more abstractly, to be the **middle thing through which** something passes: *screens mediate almost every conversation we have.*

The key limit is power. A **mediator** has none — she shuttles, reframes, coaxes, and if the two sides refuse, nothing happens. An **arbitrator** hears both sides and *rules*, and the ruling binds. A **judge** does the same with the state behind her. So "the UN mediated a ceasefire" quietly tells you the parties agreed to it; nobody was made to.

The second sense is a favourite of academic prose — *mediated by*, meaning "transmitted through / brought about by way of" — and on the GRE it usually shows up in exactly that flat, technical register.

⚠️ **mediate ≠ meditate.** One letter apart. *Mediate* = go between two sides; *meditate* = sit alone with your own mind. A monk meditates; a diplomat mediates.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Media** is sitting inside **mediate** — and the media carries messages, it doesn't hand down verdicts.

A mediator is a channel between two sides, which is exactly why she can broker a deal but never impose one.

### In sentences
1. Two neighbouring states, unwilling to speak directly, asked a retired ambassador to **mediate** the dispute over water rights.
2. Mum had to **mediate** between us for about an hour before either of us would admit anything.

### In the wild
Divorce and workplace **mediation** is the everyday version: you sit in separate rooms, the mediator walks between them, and if you won't agree, the whole thing collapses and you go to court instead — the powerlessness is the point.

### Where it comes from
Latin **medius** = *"middle."* The whole family is just positions relative to the middle: **medium** (the stuff in between), **median** (the middle value), **intermediate**, **Mediterranean** (*"middle of the earth"*), and **mediocre** — literally *middling*, which is why it's an insult.

Best of the lot: **immediate** = *im-* (not) + *mediate* — **nothing in the middle**. No go-between, no delay. That's why it means *right now*.

# **occlude** *(v.)* — **uh-KLOOD**

### Means
To **block or close off a passage or opening** — or to **cut off from view** by getting in the way.

This is a physical, clinical word. Arteries occlude, sinuses occlude, a cloud occludes the sun, a pillar occludes your view of the stage. Where *block* is plain and *obstruct* suggests something lodged in the way, **occlude** is what a doctor, an engineer, or a computer-vision paper says — and it leans toward the passage being *sealed shut* rather than merely narrowed. You cannot occlude a plan or a proposal; only a channel or a line of sight.

⚠️ **occlude ≠ preclude.**
- **occlude** = *physically shut* a passage (a clot occludes the artery)
- **preclude** = *rule out in advance* (the clot precludes surgery)

One shuts a pipe; the other shuts a possibility.

### Trick to lock it in
> Every **-clude** word is a door: *ex*clude shuts you out, *in*clude shuts you in, **oc**clude shuts the *pipe*.

The *oc-* is Latin *ob-*, "in the way," so occlude is the door slammed across a channel.

### In sentences
1. Fatty deposits had **occluded** the coronary artery almost entirely, leaving a channel barely wide enough for blood to pass.
2. Can you shift left? Your head's **occluding** half the whiteboard on the call.

### In the wild
Standard hospital phrasing you'll hear on any medical drama or in a real cardiology report: *"a fully **occluded** artery."* If you've ever had a dentist check your **occlusion**, that's the same word — how your teeth shut against each other.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ob-** (*"in the way"*) + **claudere** (*"to shut"*) — the same *claudere* that gives you **close**, **exclude**, **include**, **seclude**, **preclude**, **recluse**, and **cloister**. Learn the root once and you own the set: whatever the prefix does, *-clude* means a door is shutting.

# **pacify** *(v.)* — **PAS-uh-fye**

### Means
To **calm someone's anger or agitation**; also, of a country or region, to **force into quiet submission**.

Both senses share a faint whiff of condescension. You *pacify* a screaming toddler, a furious customer, a restless crowd — the word treats the other side's feeling as noise to be switched off rather than a grievance to be answered, so a pacified person is quiet, not satisfied. In the political sense it's a euphemism doing heavy lifting: armies "pacify" territory the way a nurse quiets a ward, which is exactly why the word turns up in official reports and rarely in honest ones.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A pacifier doesn't feed the baby — it just stops the noise.**

To pacify is to quiet the complaint without addressing what caused it.

### In sentences
1. The governor's speech did little to **pacify** a city that had spent the night in the streets.
2. I bought him a coffee to **pacify** him, but he was still sulking an hour later.

### In the wild
Melanie Martinez's **"Pacify Her"** — off *Cry Baby*, the album built entirely around dummies, tantrums and being soothed rather than heard. Title match and meaning match at once.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pax, pacis** = *"peace"* + **-ficāre** = *"to make."* Literally *peace-make*.

That root pays out across the list: **pact** (a peace agreed), **appease**, **pacifist**, and the **Pacific** Ocean — named by Magellan because he crossed it in unnervingly calm weather.

# **paragon** *(n.)* — **PA-ruh-gon**

*Stress the first syllable — not puh-RAG-un.*

### Means
A **model of excellence** — a person or thing so perfect in some quality that everyone else gets measured against it.

Not merely "very good." A paragon is the **benchmark**: the phrasing is almost always *a paragon of* + a virtue (patience, integrity, restraint, efficiency). Because the praise is absolute, it tips easily into sarcasm — "he's a paragon of punctuality" said about a man forty minutes late does real damage.

⚠️ **paragon ≠ paradigm.**
- **paragon** = the *best* example (of a virtue) — praise
- **paradigm** = the *typical* example, the pattern or framework — neutral

You're a paragon of virtue; you work within a paradigm.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Par**agon — a paragon doesn't *meet* par, it **sets** par.

Golf's *par* is the score everything else is judged against, which is exactly what a paragon is for a virtue.

### In sentences
1. Her predecessor was held up as a paragon of judicial restraint, a standard no successor was expected to reach.
2. Look, I'm no paragon of healthy eating, but even I don't have cake for breakfast.

### In the wild
Hamlet on mankind: *"how noble in reason... the beauty of the world, the **paragon** of animals"* — the top specimen, the one the rest are compared to.

### Where it comes from
Italian **paragone** = a *touchstone*: the dark stone jewellers rubbed gold against, reading the streak to judge its purity. A paragon is still that — the thing you test others against.

# **patronize** *(v.)* — **PAY-truh-nyze**

*British: PAT-ruh-nyze, with the vowel of *cat*. Spelled *patronise* in the UK.*

### Means
Two senses, and the GRE tests both.

**1. To treat someone as an inferior while appearing kind** — to talk down. The sting is that it comes wrapped as help: the explanation you didn't need, the praise pitched at a child, the slow careful voice. Plain rudeness insults you openly; patronizing insults you *while doing you a favour*, which is why it's harder to object to without looking ungracious.

**2. To be a customer of a shop, or to sponsor an artist or cause.** Fully neutral, even complimentary — "she patronized the same tailor for forty years." Same word, no condescension at all.

Both senses run through the noun **patron**: the rich backer. Sense 2 is what a patron does; sense 1 is what it feels like to be on the receiving end.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **patron** funds you and thinks he owns you — so to *patronize* is to treat someone like your charity case.

The hidden word *patron* gives you both meanings at once: the sponsor's money (sense 2) and the sponsor's superior smile (sense 1).

### In sentences
1. The report **patronizes** its readers, defining "inflation" twice and pausing to reassure them that economics is not so very difficult.
2. Don't **patronize** me — I've been doing this job longer than you've been alive.

### In the wild
"Thank you for patronizing our business" on a shop receipt or door sign — the sense that has nothing to do with condescension, printed by people trying to be polite.

### Where it comes from
Latin **pater, patris** = *"father."* A **patron** was a father-figure protector; to patronize is to play the father to a grown adult — funding them, or lecturing them.

The root is worth a lot of GRE words: **paternal**, **paternalistic** (governing people "for their own good"), **patriarch**, **patrimony**, **patriot**, **expatriate**.

# **penurious** *(adj.)* — **puh-NUUR-ee-uhs**

*British speakers often say puh-NYUUR-ee-uhs.*

### Means
Two linked senses: **(1) extremely poor**, destitute — *a penurious old age*; **(2) stingy**, unwilling to part with money — *a penurious tip*.

Context decides which, and the overlap is the point: the word describes someone who *lives as though there is nothing*, whether or not there actually is. Sense (2) is where the GRE lives, and it is an insult — **frugal** and **thrifty** praise you for spending carefully, **penurious** says your meanness is a defect. It's also a bookish word: you'd write it in a novel or an essay, not hiss it across a restaurant table.

⚠️ **penurious ≠ pernicious.** Similar shape, unrelated: *pernicious* = quietly destructive, doing harm by degrees (*a pernicious rumour*). Penury is about money; pernicious is about damage.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Penurious** = down to the last **penny** — either you have one, or you act like it.

The false penny-echo carries both senses at once, which is exactly how the word behaves.

### In sentences
1. Widowed at thirty, she passed the remaining decades in **penurious** retirement, heating one room of the house.
2. He's not broke — he's just **penurious**; the man split a birthday dinner bill by the item.

### Where it comes from
Latin **penuria** = *"want, scarcity"* — straight into English as **penury**, meaning grinding poverty.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *poena* (punishment)** — that root gives *penal, penalty, penance, penitent, repent*. Two different "pen-" families; don't let *penitentiary* pull *penurious* toward guilt.

# **piquant** *(adj.)* — **PEE-kuhnt**

*Also heard as PEE-kwahnt or pee-KAHNT; the one thing it is not is "PICK-want."*

### Means
Sharp in a way you enjoy. Of food: **agreeably pungent, tangy, zesty** — a bite that wakes the tongue up. Of a remark, a face, a detail, a scandal: **pleasantly stimulating, intriguing, a little provocative**.

The word carries a built-in verdict: praise. *Pungent* and *acrid* describe a sharpness that may well be unpleasant; **piquant** insists the sharpness is a pleasure. It also implies the sharpness is *small and well-judged* — a piquant sauce is not a mouthful of chilli, and a piquant remark is not a savaging. Register is a touch refined: food writers, critics and novelists use it, and it flatters both the thing described and the speaker's palate.

⚠️ **piquant ≠ poignant.**
- **piquant** *(PEE-kuhnt)* = sharp and **delightful**
- **poignant** *(POYN-yuhnt)* = sharp and **sad** — it hurts in a moving way

A piquant detail makes you lean in; a poignant one makes your throat tighten.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Piquant = "peek" + "want" — a sharpness that makes you peek, then want more.**

The sound spells out the nuance: this kind of sharpness attracts rather than repels.

### In sentences
1. Her memoir is at its best in the **piquant** margins — the small, faintly scandalous details she clearly enjoyed committing to print.
2. Add a squeeze of lime at the end; it makes the whole thing **piquant** instead of just heavy.

### In the wild
It lives on menus and in food writing — Creole **sauce piquante** is a standard dish name, and any restaurant review praising a "piquant" dressing means sharp-and-lively, never harsh.

### Where it comes from
French **piquer** = *"to prick, to sting."*

Same prick gives you **pique**: *piqued my curiosity* (something pricked your interest) and *a fit of pique* (your pride got pricked). Hold the image of a small, sharp jab — pleasant on the tongue, provoking to the mind.

# **rampant** *(adj.)* — **RAM-puhnt**

### Means
Spreading or flourishing **without restraint** — unchecked, out of control.

*Widespread* just counts heads; **rampant** says nothing is stopping it and it's still growing. That's why it collocates almost exclusively with things you'd want stopped: rampant corruption, rampant inflation, rampant speculation, rampant weeds. Calling growth "rampant" is a complaint about the *absence of a brake*, not a measurement of size.

There's a second, technical sense in heraldry: an animal **rearing up on its hind legs**, forepaws raised — *a lion rampant*.

⚠️ **rampant ≠ rampart.** A **rampart** is a defensive earth wall — the thing built to *stop* an assault. *Rampant* is the assault nobody stopped. Unrelated roots, one letter apart.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Rampant** things **ramp** up — and no one's found the brake.

The hidden *ramp* is real, not a coincidence — same root — and it carries the nuance: not merely common, but climbing unchecked.

### In sentences
1. Absent any independent auditor, embezzlement became **rampant** within the ministry.
2. Don't leave the raised bed a month — the mint goes **rampant** and takes the whole thing.

### In the wild
Scotland's royal banner is literally called the **Lion Rampant** — a red lion reared up on its hind legs, claws out. Same beast on the **Peugeot** badge. That's the word's original picture: a creature standing up out of control.

### Where it comes from
Old French **ramper** = *"to climb, to rear up."* The heraldic lion climbs; so does the corruption. Same root gives you **ramp** (the thing you climb) and **rampage**.

# **remote** *(adj.)* — **ri-MOHT**

### Means
**1.** Far off in space or time — a *remote* village, the *remote* past.
**2.** **Slight, faint, barely there** — a *remote* possibility, a *remote* resemblance.
**3.** Of a person: **coolly detached**, hard to reach emotionally.

Sense 1 is the one you already know and the one the GRE almost never asks about. Senses 2 and 3 are the test: both are just distance re-applied. A *remote* chance is a chance so far off you can hardly make it out — not impossible, but not worth planning around. A *remote* manner isn't coldness or hostility; the person is perfectly polite and simply unreachable, answering from somewhere further back than the room. That's what separates it from *aloof* (which implies choosing to stand apart, often with a hint of superiority): *remote* carries no judgement of the person, only the sense that you can't get to them.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **remote** chance is a mere **mote** of a chance — one speck, seen from far away.

*Mote* isn't the real root, but the sound hook pins the exam sense: remote = faint, slight, barely registering.

### In sentences
1. Grief had left her **remote** — present at every dinner, courteous throughout, and somewhere far behind her own eyes.
2. There's a **remote** chance he texts back tonight, but honestly, don't wait up.

### In the wild
The thing on your sofa. A **remote control** is control exercised from a distance — and that same distance, stretched to its limit, is what gives you *a remote chance*: so far off you can barely see it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **removēre** = *"to move back, move away"* (**re-** back + **movēre** to move); *remotus* = "moved away." Everything the word does is that one gesture repeated — moved away in space, in likelihood, in feeling.

*Movēre* is a workhorse root: **remove, motion, motive, mobile, momentum, promote, demote, commotion, emotion** (literally "a moving out" — feeling that stirs you).

# **reprobate** *(n. / adj. / v.)* — **REP-ruh-bayt**

### Means
**Noun:** a morally unprincipled person — a scoundrel, a degenerate. **Adjective:** depraved, unrepentant. **Verb (rare, formal):** to condemn or reject as worthless.

The word's flavour comes from its theological past: a reprobate isn't just badly behaved, he's been *written off* — tested, judged, and abandoned as past reforming. Which is exactly why it has drifted half-affectionate. Calling someone a *degenerate* is an attack; calling him an **old reprobate** is usually said with a smile, about a boozy uncle who will never change and doesn't intend to. Real venom needs the tone to supply it — the word alone comes with a shrug.

⚠️ **reprobate ≠ reprove.**
- **reprobate** = the *person* who is past correcting
- **reprove** = to *mildly correct* someone (a teacher reproves a chatty pupil)

You reprove someone you still have hope for.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Re-probate = failed his probation.** Tested, rejected, and nobody's expecting reform.

The *prob-* is the same "test" root as **probation** and **probe**, and *re-* flips it to rejection — so the reprobate is the one already weighed and written off, which is why the word lands as resignation more than outrage.

### In sentences
1. Victorian novelists loved the figure of the **reprobate** heir — drinking, gambling, and dismantling an estate his father spent forty years assembling.
2. He's a **reprobate** and everyone knows it, but he tells the best stories at the table, so he keeps getting invited.

### In the wild
The King James Bible gives the phrase **"a reprobate mind"** (Romans 1:28), and Calvinist doctrine split humanity into the *elect* and the *reprobate* — the damned. That's where the "beyond saving" charge in the word comes from.

### Where it comes from
Latin **probāre** = *"to test, to prove good"*, from **probus** = *"upright, honest."* Add *re-* in its reversing sense and you get **reprobāre**, "to reject after testing."

One root, a whole GRE shelf: **probe**, **probation**, **probity** (moral uprightness), **approve**, **prove**, **reprove**. Note that **probity** and **reprobate** are exact opposites built from the same word.

# **turbid** *(adj.)* — **TER-bid**

### Means
Cloudy, thick, opaque — water (or any liquid) you can't see through because sediment is churned up in it. Figuratively: **muddled, confused** — *turbid prose*, *turbid emotions*.

The murk is **suspended, not dyed**: something stirred the bottom and it hasn't settled. That's the difference from *murky*, which is the everyday word and drifts into moral vagueness (*murky dealings*); *turbid* stays physical and precise, which is why lab reports and river surveys use it. Applied to writing or thought, it accuses you of clouding a thing that could have been clear.

⚠️ **turbid ≠ turgid.**
- **turbid** = *cloudy*, full of stirred-up silt
- **turgid** = *swollen* — and of prose, bloated and pompous

Turgid writing is overblown; turbid writing is unclear. Both are insults, different ones.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Turbid** water has been **dis-TURB-ed** — the mud is up off the bottom.

The word hides *disturb*, which pins the exact nuance: the cloudiness comes from stuff stirred into suspension, not from dirt staining the water.

### In sentences
1. After the storm the river ran **turbid** and brown, carrying half the hillside toward the sea.
2. His explanation got more **turbid** the longer he talked, until nobody knew what he was actually asking for.

### In the wild
**Turbidity** is a number people actually measure — your city's water-quality report, a pool test kit, an aquarium filter spec, all rated in NTU (nephelometric turbidity units). Low turbidity = you can see through it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **turba** = *"crowd, uproar"* → **turbidus**, "stirred up." A milling crowd and clouded water are the same idea: everything churning at once.

Same root: **disturb, perturb, turbulent, trouble, turbine** — all things that stir.

# **turgid** *(adj.)* — **TUR-jid**

### Means
**Literally:** swollen, distended, bloated with fluid — a *turgid* river, *turgid* tissue.

**On the GRE, almost always figurative:** writing or speech that is inflated, ponderous, and exhausting to get through.

Where *bombastic* is loud and *grandiloquent* is showy, **turgid is heavy**. A bombastic speaker at least entertains you; turgid prose puffs itself up and puts you to sleep at the same time. The word passes judgement on the *effect* — you sank in it. That double charge, pompous *and* tedious, is why it's the reviewer's favourite insult for a bad novel or a bad report.

⚠️ **turgid ≠ turbid.**
- **turgid** = *swollen* (prose, rivers, egos)
- **turbid** = *cloudy, muddy, murky* (water, thinking)

Turbid water is unclear; turgid writing is unbearable.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Turgid prose is gorged** — stuffed with words, not ideas.

The *g* in *turgid* pulls in *gorged*: fat, full, and slow-moving, which is exactly what the word accuses a piece of writing of being.

### In sentences
1. The commission's **turgid** report buried its one useful recommendation under forty pages of throat-clearing.
2. I quit his novel by page thirty — "turgid" doesn't begin to cover it.

### In the wild
British football commentary lives on this word — a dire goalless match is *"a turgid 0-0"* in every match report on the Monday. Same idea as the prose sense: bloated, slow, nothing happening.

### Where it comes from
Latin **turgēre** = *"to swell."* Biology kept it straight: **turgor pressure** is the water pressure that keeps a plant cell swollen and a leaf standing up — let it drop and the plant wilts.

🚫 **Not *turbid*.** That's Latin **turba**, *"crowd, uproar"* — which also gives you **disturb, turbulent, perturb**. Stirred-up, hence murky. Different root, different word.

# **vacuous** *(adj.)* — **VAK-yoo-uhs**

### Means
Empty — literally void of contents, but almost always used of a remark, expression, or mind that **contains no thought at all**.

The word is a sneer, and it lands hardest when something *should* have had substance: a vacuous speech had a podium, an audience and twenty minutes, and delivered nothing. *Vague* means you can't tell what was meant; *vacuous* means nothing was meant. Reach for it about the smile that registers no one home, or the comment that sounds like an idea until you try to hold it.

⚠️ **vacuous ≠ fatuous.** They rhyme and both insult, but *vacuous* = **empty** (no content), *fatuous* = **smugly silly** (plenty of content, all of it foolish). A blank stare is vacuous; a pleased-with-itself joke is fatuous.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Vacuous = vacuum + -ous.** A vacuum is empty *space*; a vacuous remark is empty *where the meaning should be*.

The vacuum cleaner in the word tells you the contents have been sucked out of something built to hold them.

### In sentences
1. The minister's statement was a masterpiece of **vacuous** reassurance: three hundred words, not one commitment.
2. He just gave me this **vacuous** grin, like the question had gone straight through him.

### In the wild
In logic and CS, a statement like "every file in this empty folder is corrupted" is **vacuously true** — true only because there's nothing there to check. The technical sense preserves the ordinary one exactly: true, but empty.

### Where it comes from
Latin **vacuus** = *"empty,"* from **vacāre**, *"to be empty, to be free."* That root is everywhere: **vacuum, vacant, vacate, vacation, evacuate, vacancy** — and *vacation* is literally the time your desk stands empty.
