# **abhor** *(v.)* — **ab-HOR**

### Means
To hate something so deeply that it disgusts you — to recoil from it.

This is the top of the hatred scale, and it's **moral, not personal**. You *dislike* a colleague, you *hate* Mondays, but you **abhor** cruelty, torture, hypocrisy — practices and principles rather than people. It's formal, a little lofty, and it says as much about the speaker's values as about the thing: whoever abhors something is announcing where their line is.

⚠️ **abhorrent ≠ aberrant.** *Abhorrent* = loathsome, disgusting. *Aberrant* = straying from the normal type. An aberrant result is just odd; an abhorrent one is repellent.

### Trick to lock it in
> **ab-HOR** — you back **away** (*ab-*) in **horror**.

The word literally has *horror* sitting inside it, which is exactly the intensity it carries — not mild distaste but a physical flinch.

### In sentences
1. The treaty's framers **abhorred** the very idea of a standing army, seeing in it the seed of tyranny.
2. I **abhor** group projects — three people vanish and one of us does the whole thing at 2am.

### In the wild
**"Nature abhors a vacuum."** The old Aristotelian maxim you've heard used about everything from politics to office gossip — nature doesn't merely dislike empty space, it rushes in to destroy it.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ab-** (*away from*) + **horrēre** (*to bristle, to shudder*) — the hair standing up on your arms. Same root gives you **horror, horrid, horrendous, horrific**, and **abhorrent**.

# **boisterous** *(adj.)* — **BOY-stuh-ruhs**

### Means
Noisily energetic and unruly — loud, physical, full of high spirits. Also, of wind or sea: **rough, wild, turbulent**.

The warmth is what sets it apart. **Rowdy** hints at trouble brewing; **raucous** is about the harshness of the sound itself; **boisterous** keeps its good humour — the noise comes from people enjoying themselves too much, not from malice. You'd call a family dinner or a pub full of fans boisterous, and only half be complaining.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Boisterous** starts with **boys** — a houseful of them: deafening, exhausting, harmless.

The "boys" buried in the spelling carries the exact tone — chaos you tolerate rather than chaos you call the police about.

### In sentences
1. Three days of **boisterous** seas kept the fleet at anchor.
2. The party got **boisterous** around midnight — someone was singing, badly, standing on a chair.

### In the wild
It's the standard euphemism on a child's school report: **"boisterous"** is what a teacher writes when she means *this child never stops shouting* but wants to sound fond about it.

# **chivalrous** *(adj.)* — **SHIV-uhl-ruhs**

*The ch is sh, and the stress lands on the first syllable — never shiv-AL-rus.*

### Means
Courteous, gallant and honourable — behaving with the courage, generosity and protectiveness of an ideal knight, especially toward someone weaker or toward women.

*Polite* is about manners; **chivalrous** is about a code. It carries self-sacrifice — giving up the seat, the coat, the advantage — and it always looks slightly backdated, which is why it's so often used with a wink ("very chivalrous of you"). Praise, but praise with a period costume on.

⚠️ **chivalrous ≠ cavalier.** Both grew out of the word for *horse*, and they ended up opposites: **chivalrous** = gallant and considerate; **cavalier** = airily dismissive, treating serious things as trifles. The knight on the horse became a gentleman in one word and a snob in the other.

### Trick to lock it in
> It starts with **shiv** — a chivalrous man would take the shiv for you.

The sound hook fixes the key part of the meaning: not mere politeness, but taking the hit on someone else's behalf.

### In sentences
1. Their surrender was met with **chivalrous** treatment: the officers kept their swords, and the wounded were carried out first.
2. He walked her to her car at 2am, which was **chivalrous**, though mostly he just wanted to keep talking.

### In the wild
The stock complaint **"chivalry is dead"** — muttered whenever nobody holds a door or gives up a seat — is this word's everyday afterlife.

### Where it comes from
French **cheval** = *horse* → **chevalier**, a horseman, i.e. a knight → **chivalry**, the knight's code. Only rich men had horses, so the horse-word became the class-word became the manners-word.

Same root, whole family: **cavalry** (horse soldiers), **cavalier**, **chevalier**, Spanish **caballero**.

# **churlish** *(adj.)* — **CHUR-lish**

### Means
Rude, surly and **ungracious** — especially in response to someone else's kindness or generosity.

Churlish isn't loud rudeness or aggression; it's *meanness of spirit* on a small scale — the grudging thanks, the sulky refusal, the petty complaint about a free gift. Where **rude** describes an act and **surly** describes a mood, churlish passes judgement on your character: you were given something and you couldn't rise to it. Note also where it lives — the word is most at home in the negative, conceding a point: *it would be churlish not to admit she was right.*

### Trick to lock it in
> Churlish is a **churl**'s answer to a kindness — small, sour and ungrateful, never grand.

Listen for its favourite frame, *"it would be churlish to refuse"* — the word exists to name bad grace in the face of generosity, not open hostility.

### In sentences
1. Given how much the committee had conceded already, it would be **churlish** to press them further.
2. He mumbled a **churlish** thanks and shoved the present under the sofa without opening it.

### In the wild
Shakespeare, *As You Like It* — the exiled Duke endures "the icy fang / And **churlish** chiding of the winter's wind." The wind isn't violent, just meanly ungenerous; that's the exact temperature of the word.

### Where it comes from
Old English **ceorl** = a *churl*, the lowest rank of free peasant. The aristocracy did this constantly: take a class label, turn it into a character flaw. **Villain** ← *villein* (a farm serf), **boor** ← a farmer, **vulgar** ← the common crowd, **ignoble** ← not of noble birth. Learn the pattern and four GRE words come free.

Same Germanic root as **Karl / Charles**, which simply meant "free man."

# **clandestine** *(adj.)* — **klan-DES-tin**

*Also heard as klan-DES-teen; stress never falls on "clan".*

### Means
Kept secret, hidden, concealed — especially something **organised and deliberately hidden because it isn't allowed**.

The word almost always carries a whiff of illegitimacy: clandestine *meetings*, *affairs*, *labs*, *cells*, *operations*. It describes a **planned activity**, not a personality — which is what separates it from **furtive**, which describes *manner* (a furtive glance, shifty and nervous). **Covert** is the neutral, official term an agency would use about itself; **clandestine** is what the newspaper calls it afterwards. Nobody runs a clandestine bake sale.

### Trick to lock it in
> **CLAN**destine — a **clan** meeting after dark, doors bolted, nobody told.

The hidden word gives you the key nuance: this is *group* secrecy, planned and conspiratorial, not one person looking shifty.

### In sentences
1. The pamphlets were printed at a **clandestine** press in the cellar of a bookshop, three streets from the censor's office.
2. They'd been dating for months — very **clandestine**, separate cars, separate entrances, the whole thing.

### In the wild
The CIA's spy branch was literally called the **National Clandestine Service** — the officers who run deniable operations abroad, as opposed to the analysts at desks.

### Where it comes from
Latin **clam** = *"secretly"*, kin to **celāre**, *"to hide"* — the same root under **conceal** and (via *cella*, a hidden room) **cell** and **cellar**.

# **complacent** *(adj.)* — **kuhm-PLAY-suhnt**

### Means
Smugly satisfied with how things are — so pleased with yourself or your situation that you stop checking, stop improving, stop worrying.

The word always carries a warning inside it: the danger you've stopped noticing is still there. *Content* is a compliment and *satisfied* is neutral, but **complacent** is a rebuke — it says your comfort is unearned, or at least unwise. Note the two flavours: complacent *about* a threat (the ignored risk) and complacent *in* success (the champion who stops training).

⚠️ **complacent ≠ complaisant.**
- **complacent** = pleased with **yourself**
- **complaisant** (kuhm-PLAY-zuhnt) = eager to please **others**; obliging to a fault

A complacent host thinks the party is going brilliantly; a complaisant host will fetch you anything you ask for.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Com-PLEASE-ant** — so pleased with yourself you've gone **placid**.

The middle of the word is *pleased*, and its cousin *placid* supplies the rest of the meaning: pleased into stillness, which is exactly when things go wrong.

### In sentences
1. Two decades of unchallenged dominance had made the firm **complacent**, and it never saw the smaller rival coming.
2. We were 3–0 up at half time and got **complacent** — ended up drawing.

### In the wild
Corporate and sporting cliché you've heard a hundred times: *"there's no room for complacency."* Nobody ever says it about a team that's losing — it's the thing you warn winners about.

### Where it comes from
Latin **complacēre** = *"to please greatly"* (*com-* intensive + *placēre*, "to please").

That root is worth banking: **placid** (calm), **placate** and **implacable** (impossible to soothe), **placebo** (literally "I shall please"), **complaisant**, and plain **please**.

# **cumbersome** *(adj.)* — **KUM-ber-suhm**

### Means
**Hard to handle** because it's too large, heavy, or awkwardly built — and by extension, any system so clunky and over-complicated that it slows everything down.

The complaint is about **friction, not pain**. Something *onerous* imposes real hardship; something **cumbersome** merely makes you struggle with it — the suitcase that won't fit through the door, the eight-step approval form that eventually works. It's mild, practical, faintly exasperated: the word of someone who got there in the end, but wishes they hadn't had to.

On the GRE it usually shows up in the figurative sense — cumbersome *machinery*, *bureaucracy*, *prose*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **burdensome, troublesome, tiresome, cumbersome** — the *-some* family all name a nuisance; cumbersome's nuisance is **bulk**.

Park it beside its rhymes and keep the distinguishing bit: the trouble is that the thing is too big or clunky to manage smoothly, not that it's painful or wrong.

### In sentences
1. The committee's **cumbersome** review procedure required four signatures before a single sentence could be published.
2. This laptop's fine, but the charger's so **cumbersome** I just leave it at home.

### In the wild
This is a workplace staple: *a cumbersome approval process*, *a cumbersome UI*, *cumbersome paperwork*. It's the standard polite complaint for something that technically works but makes you work for it.

### Where it comes from
From **cumber**, "to hinder, block up" (Old French *combrer*, from Late Latin *cumbrus*, a barrier or heap of rubble) + **-some**, "full of."

The useful payoff is the family: **encumber** (to weigh down), **encumbrance** (a burden — in law, a claim against property), and the GRE favourite **unencumbered** — free of baggage, financial or otherwise.

# **debilitating** *(adj. (from v. debilitate))* — **dih-BIL-ih-tay-ting**

### Means
Draining someone's strength so badly that they can no longer function normally — used of illnesses, injuries, pain, anxiety, strikes, sanctions.

The word marks a threshold, not a degree of unpleasantness. A migraine can be *painful* without being debilitating; it becomes debilitating the moment you can't work, drive, or stand light. *Damaging* means harm was done; **debilitating** means capacity was taken away, and usually taken away for a stretch of time rather than in one blow. It's the standard word of doctors, insurers and journalists, so it carries a clinical, matter-of-fact tone even when describing something awful.

### Trick to lock it in
> De-**BIL**-itating quietly deletes your a-**bil**-ity.

Use it only when the thing takes function away, not when it is merely painful or inconvenient.

### In sentences
1. A **debilitating** tremor ended a surgical career that had lasted thirty years.
2. Three hours of sleep and back-to-back meetings — by Friday the tiredness was genuinely **debilitating**.

### In the wild
It's the gatekeeping word on disability and insurance paperwork: the form never asks whether your condition is *bad*, it asks whether it is **debilitating** — i.e. whether it stops you doing the job.

# **deliberate** *(adj. / v.)* — **dih-LIB-uh-rut**

*The verb moves: dih-LIB-uh-RAYT. Same spelling, different last syllable — the adjective ends in a mumble, the verb ends in "-ate."*

### Means
**Adjective, sense 1:** done **on purpose**, with the consequences already weighed — not accidental, not impulsive.
**Adjective, sense 2:** **unhurried and measured** — "a deliberate pace," "deliberate movements."
**Verb:** to **think or discuss something over carefully** before deciding.

*Intentional* just means you meant it. **Deliberate** says you thought about it first — which is why it's the word prosecutors and critics reach for: *deliberate cruelty*, *a deliberate snub*, *a deliberate falsehood*. The forethought is what makes it damning. Turned the other way, the same forethought is a compliment: a deliberate craftsman, a deliberate decision. Sense 2 is the same idea applied to speed — deliberate slowness isn't sluggishness, it's someone taking the time the thing deserves.

### Trick to lock it in
> **De-LIBRA-te** — the scales are sitting in the middle of the word. You weigh first, *then* act.

Weighing takes time, which gives you both senses at once: the slow measured pace, and the fact that whatever follows was decided, not stumbled into.

### In sentences
1. The committee **deliberated** for six hours before conceding that no version of the proposal could survive a vote.
2. That wasn't a slip of the tongue — she left his name off the list, and it was 100% **deliberate**.

### In the wild
"The jury is still **deliberating**" — the line every courtroom drama and news bulletin uses for the hours the verdict takes. Twelve people in a room, weighing.

### Where it comes from
Latin **dē- + lībrāre** = *"to weigh out,"* from **lībra**, a pair of **scales**. Same *libra* as the zodiac sign **Libra** (the scales), as **equilibrium** (equal weights), and as the **lb** abbreviation for pound.

🚫 **Not *liber*, "free."** That's *liberate, liberty, liberal* — a different root that happens to look identical. Deliberating has nothing to do with freeing anything.

# **droll** *(adj.)* — **DROHL**

*Rhymes with roll, not with doll.*

### Means
Amusing in an **odd, dry, understated** way — funny because it's peculiar, not because it's uproarious.

Scale matters: a *hilarious* joke makes you laugh out loud, a **droll** one gets a slow half-smile and a raised eyebrow. The humour is deadpan, delivered flat, often faintly old-fashioned or whimsical — the sort of wit that never signals it's joking. Said sincerely it's a compliment (a droll storyteller is good company); said flatly to someone's face — *"How droll."* — it's pure sarcasm, meaning the opposite.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Droll** has **roll** in it — but nobody's *rolling* on the floor laughing.

The buried word is exactly what droll humour never produces: it's the quiet smile, not the belly laugh.

### In sentences
1. His memoirs are **droll** rather than funny — a lifetime of disasters recounted in the tone of a man reading a weather report.
2. She's got this **droll** way of complaining where you're not sure for about three seconds whether she's serious.

### In the wild
It's a stock word in British film and theatre reviews — the standing critic's label for deadpan delivery, the comic who never cracks a smile at his own line.

# **eccentric** *(adj. / n.)* — **ik-SEN-trik**

### Means
**Adj.:** deviating from the conventional in a harmless, distinctive way. **Noun:** a person who does exactly that.

The word is *tolerant*. **Weird** and **bizarre** push the odd person away; **eccentric** keeps them, with a smile. It implies the oddity is stable and self-contained — a man who wears the same purple suit daily and keeps forty clocks is eccentric; a man who frightens strangers is not. There's also a quiet class edge to it: the rich and the tenured get called eccentric, everyone else gets called strange.

⚠️ **eccentric ≠ eclectic.**
- **eccentric** = *off-centre* — odd, unconventional
- **eclectic** = *selected from many sources* — a wide-ranging mix

An eclectic record collection is broad. An eccentric one is all kazoo music.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Off-centre, not off the rails.**

The eccentric has merely drifted from the middle of normal — still functioning, still likeable, just not standing where everyone else is.

### In sentences
1. The college indulged its most **eccentric** don, who lectured barefoot and addressed his students exclusively in the third person.
2. My aunt's a bit **eccentric** — she's named all the garden gnomes after prime ministers.

### In the wild
The press can't resist **"eccentric billionaire"** — Howard Hughes, Musk, any tech founder mid-scandal. Watch what the phrase does: the money is what converts *unhinged* into *eccentric*.

### Where it comes from
Greek **ek** (*out of*) + **kentron** (*centre*) — literally *out of centre*. It started as astronomy: an **eccentric** orbit is one whose centre isn't where it ought to be.

Same **centr-** family: **concentric** (sharing a centre), **egocentric** (self at the centre), **epicentre**, **centrifugal**.

# **fractious** *(adj.)* — **FRAK-shuhs**

### Means
**Irritable and quarrelsome; unruly, hard to keep in line.**

Two habitats. Of a person — usually a child, a patient, a passenger — it means cranky and difficult, complaining and resisting. Of a group — a coalition, a union, a parliament, a family — it means the members squabble among themselves and could split apart at any moment.

Milder and more detached than *belligerent* or *truculent*: a fractious person is a nuisance, not a threat. It's a word an observer uses about someone else's bad temper, often with a faint weariness — the tone of a nanny or a political correspondent, not of someone spoiling for a fight.

⚠️ **fractious ≠ factious.**
- **fractious** = *bad-tempered, unruly*
- **factious** = *creating factions, stirring up dissent*

A fractious toddler is merely difficult; a factious minister is deliberately splitting the party.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Fract**ious = one **fract**ure away from falling apart.

The word is visibly *fracture* plus an adjective ending, which catches both senses at once — the temper that snaps and the alliance that cracks.

### In sentences
1. The armistice was administered by a **fractious** committee whose members agreed on nothing but their contempt for one another.
2. He'd been stuck on a delayed flight for six hours with two **fractious** kids and no snacks left.

### In the wild
Political journalism runs on this word — "a **fractious** coalition," "**fractious** talks," "the party's **fractious** right wing." If you read any election coverage, you've met it a dozen times.

### Where it comes from
Latin **frangere / fractus** = *"to break."* English *fraction* once meant not a number but a **breaking apart — discord, dissension**; *fractious* is the adjective from that lost sense.

Same root, huge family: **fracture, fragment, fraction, fragile, frail, infringe, refract**.

# **limpid** *(adj.)* — **LIM-pid**

### Means
Perfectly clear and transparent — water, air, eyes you can see straight through. Extended to prose, music or reasoning: so clear it costs the reader no effort.

This is a compliment, and a slightly literary one. *Transparent* is a physical fact; *lucid* praises the mind behind the words; **limpid** praises the surface — it adds calm and beauty, the stillness of an untroubled pool. Water churning white over rocks is clear, but it isn't limpid. Nobody says it in conversation about a glass of water; it turns up in reviews, poetry, and obituaries of good writers.

⚠️ **limpid ≠ limp.** The first four letters are a decoy — *limpid* has nothing to do with floppiness or weakness. A limpid style is clean and strong, not feeble.

### Trick to lock it in
> **LIMP-ID** — so clear you can see all the way down to the **id**.

The hidden **id** at the end reminds you that limpid clarity goes right to the bottom, nothing hiding in the depths — and that the *limp* at the front is a trap.

### In sentences
1. Her late essays are **limpid** — not a sentence you have to read twice.
2. The lake was so **limpid** you could count the stones eight feet down.

### Where it comes from
Latin **limpidus** = *"clear"*, tied to **lympha**, *"clear spring water"* — the same root that gives English **lymph** and **lymphatic**, the clear fluid in your body. Lymph and limpid: both mean *water you can see through*.

# **mawkish** *(adj.)* — **MAW-kish**

*Rhymes with hawkish.*

### Means
Sickly sentimental — emotional in a way that's laid on so thick it stops being moving and starts being nauseating.

Two things do the work here. It is **always** an insult, and the insult is about *excess*, not about feeling. A film can be sad, tender, even sappy and survive; call it **mawkish** and you're saying the emotion was manufactured, over-sugared, and it turned your stomach. It's a critic's word — cool, faintly superior, spoken by someone who is refusing to be moved.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Mawkish** = a **maw**-ful of **kiss** — sweetness pushed down your gullet until you gag.

The word never means *emotional*; it means emotion so overdone that the body rejects it.

### In sentences
1. The eulogy slid from moving into **mawkish** the instant he began reciting song lyrics.
2. I wanted to like the ending, but it got so **mawkish** I had to look at my phone.

### In the wild
A house word of film and book reviewers: when a critic wants to say a tearjerker overplayed its hand, **mawkish** is the verdict — it carries a sneer that plain *sentimental* doesn't.

### Where it comes from
From Middle English **mawke** = *"maggot"* (via Old Norse *mathkr*). **Mawkish** first meant simply *sickening, nauseating* — maggot-ridden — and only later narrowed to one specific thing that makes you queasy: too much sugar in the feelings.

That buried disgust is the whole point of the word. It isn't "a bit soppy"; it's *revolting*.

# **obeisance** *(n.)* — **oh-BAY-suhns**

*Also heard as oh-BEE-suhns; the "s" is soft, never "obese-ance".*

### Means
A **bow, curtsy, or kneeling** — a bodily gesture of respect to someone above you; by extension, **deferential homage** itself ("paying obeisance to tradition").

Where *respect* is an attitude and *obedience* is doing what you're told, obeisance is the **performance** — submission made visible, in front of witnesses. It belongs to courts, shrines and thrones, so in modern prose it carries either genuine ceremony or a dry sneer: when a columnist writes that tech CEOs *paid obeisance* to the new administration, the word is doing the mocking.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Obei**sance has **obey** sitting right at the front — bend the "y" into a bow and you get the word.

Obedience is obeying; obeisance is the visible bending that shows it.

### In sentences
1. The courtiers made their **obeisance** as the queen passed, foreheads nearly touching the marble.
2. He hates the boss but still does that little **obeisance** — the nod, the laugh at the bad joke — every single morning.

### In the wild
Indian news copy runs on this word: **"the Prime Minister paid obeisance at the temple"** appears in headlines almost every week — always the bow, the offering, the cameras.

### Where it comes from
Old French **obéissance** ("obedience"), from Latin **oboedīre** = *ob-* (toward) + **audīre** (to hear) — to obey is literally to *listen toward* someone.

That gives you a free family: **obey, obedient, audience, audible, audition, auditorium**.

# **ostentatious** *(adj.)* — **os-ten-TAY-shus**

### Means
Showy and extravagant **in a way designed to be noticed** — wealth, taste, learning or piety put on display for an audience.

The judgement is on the *motive*, not the price tag. **Lavish** describes scale; **opulent** describes richness; **ostentatious** says the point of the thing was to be seen. A quietly expensive watch is opulent; the same watch worn sleeve-up all evening is ostentatious. It also works outside money — ostentatious grief, ostentatious humility, an ostentatious display of concern — anywhere sincerity is being performed.

**ostentatious ≠ ostensible.** Same Latin root, opposite jobs: **ostentatious** = showing off *loudly*; **ostensible** = the reason *shown* on the surface, hinting the real one is hidden ("his ostensible motive was charity").

### Trick to lock it in
> **-tatious like flirtatious** — both are performances aimed at somebody watching.

You can't flirt alone and you can't be ostentatious alone; the word always implies an audience the display is aimed at.

### In sentences
1. The new headquarters, all marble atrium and no meeting rooms, struck the board as **ostentatious** at a moment when staff were being let go.
2. He didn't need a gold phone case — that's just **ostentatious**.

### In the wild
Gatsby's parties in *The Great Gatsby* — the shirts, the orchestra, the fireworks. All of it staged so one person across the bay would look. That's ostentation with a purpose.

### Where it comes from
Latin **ostendere** = *"to stretch out in front of, to show"* (**ob-** "before" + **tendere** "to stretch"). Ostentation is holding something out for people to look at.

*tendere* is a workhorse root: **extend, tension, contend, portend, tendency** — all stretching in some direction.

# **panacea** *(n.)* — **pan-uh-SEE-uh**

*Not puh-NAY-see-uh — the stress lands late, on the SEE.*

### Means
A single remedy that cures **all** diseases, fixes **all** problems — a universal solution.

The catch is how it's actually used: nine times out of ten the word arrives with a **negative attached**. Writers reach for *panacea* precisely to deny one exists — "microfinance is no panacea," "AI is not a panacea for education." Calling something a panacea straight-faced is almost always a setup for deflating it. So it carries a faint whiff of **overclaiming**: a *remedy* is modest and real, a *panacea* is the thing credulous people believe in.

### Trick to lock it in
> The word almost always comes wearing the word **"no"** in front of it: *no panacea.*

Learn the collocation, not just the definition — if you can hear "it's no panacea," you've got both the meaning (cure-all) and the sceptical tone in one move.

### In sentences
1. Land reform was presented to the villagers as a **panacea**, though it addressed neither drought nor debt.
2. Sure, a standing desk helps your back — but it's not some **panacea** for sitting on your laptop all day.

### In the wild
The Hippocratic Oath opens by swearing "by Apollo Physician, by Asclepius, by **Hygieia**, by **Panacea**" — two Greek healing goddesses whose names became English words: *hygiene* and *panacea*.

### Where it comes from
Greek **pan** (*all*) + **akos** (*cure*) — literally "all-cure."

**pan-** is a workhorse prefix worth owning: **pandemic** (all the people), **panorama** (all the view), **pantheon** (all the gods), **panoply** (a full suit of armour → a complete array).

# **perfunctory** *(adj.)* — **per-FUNK-tuh-ree**

### Means
Done merely as a duty — mechanically, with the minimum effort, and no real interest.

The key is that the thing **does get done**. A perfunctory inspection happens; a perfunctory apology is offered. What's missing is care. That makes it a quiet insult: you're not accusing someone of failing, you're accusing them of not bothering. *Careless* implies sloppiness; *perfunctory* implies indifference wearing the costume of compliance.

**perfunctory ≠ peremptory.** *Perfunctory* is a limp handshake — going through the motions. *Peremptory* (puh-REMP-tuh-ree) is a slammed door — commanding, allowing no refusal. Low energy vs. overbearing energy.

### Trick to lock it in
> **There's no *funk* in per-FUNK-tory** — the motions happen, the soul doesn't.

The stressed syllable is literally *funk*, so hear it as a performance with all the groove drained out — technically played, emotionally absent.

### In sentences
1. The board gave the whistleblower's report a **perfunctory** review and voted, as everyone had expected, to do nothing.
2. He gave the fridge a **perfunctory** wipe, decided that counted as cleaning, and sat back down.

### In the wild
The cashier's **"How are you today?"** — asked, answered, and heard by nobody. That's the most perfunctory sentence in daily life.

### Where it comes from
Latin **perfungī** = *"to get through with, discharge a duty"* — *per-* (through) + *fungī* (to perform). Baked into the word is the idea of getting something **over with**.

That root *fungī* also gives you **function** (a thing's performance), **defunct** (no longer performing at all), and **fungible** (one unit performs the same job as another).

# **perilous** *(adj.)* — **PERR-uh-luhs**

*Three syllables — not "PERR-luhs."*

### Means
Full of danger; likely to get you hurt, ruined, or killed.

*Dangerous* is the flat, everyday word — a dangerous road, a dangerous chemical. **Perilous** is its elevated cousin: it carries weight and a whiff of narrative, and it attaches to journeys, crossings, voyages, missions, and *times*. It also stretches beyond physical harm to precarious situations — a company in a perilous financial position isn't about to explode, it's about to collapse. Reach for it when the danger is grave and the moment feels consequential; use it for a wobbly stepladder and you sound like you're narrating a trailer.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Peril** is sitting right inside it — and peril is what heroes face, not what you find in the kitchen.

The hidden word gives you the meaning free; the company peril keeps (quests, voyages, crossings) gives you the grand register.

### In sentences
1. The expedition's final ascent was the most **perilous** stage, undertaken in darkness and thinning air.
2. Lending him the car with no insurance? Mate, that's a **perilous** move.

### In the wild
The US national anthem: *"through the **perilous** fight, o'er the ramparts we watched..."* — a battle at night, exactly the grand-danger register the word lives in.

### Where it comes from
Latin **perīculum** = *"a trial, a risk"* — from a root meaning *to try, to attempt*.

That root pays out elsewhere: **experīrī** (*"to try out"*) gives you **experience**, **experiment**, and **expert** — all of them people or things that have been *tested*. Peril is simply the test that can kill you.

---

# **pervasive** *(adj.)* — **per-VAY-siv**

### Means
Spread throughout every part of something; present everywhere in a place, a system, or a mood.

*Widespread* is about **coverage** — how much of the map it reaches. **Pervasive** is about **saturation** — it has soaked into the thing, seeped into corners you wouldn't check. Damp, smoke, propaganda, anxiety: things that get *into* the material rather than sitting on top of it. It also carries a hint of the unnoticed — pervasive things are so evenly distributed that people stop registering them, which is why writers reach for it when describing corruption, bias, or dread.

⚠️ **pervasive ≠ persuasive.** One letter apart, and both turn up in GRE sentences about influence. **Pervasive** = *everywhere* (it spread). **Persuasive** = *convincing* (it argued). An ad campaign can be pervasive without being remotely persuasive.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Widespread** covers the map; **pervasive** soaks the sponge.

Reach for *pervasive* when the thing has got *into* everything rather than merely spread *across* a lot of it.

### In sentences
1. A pervasive distrust of the courts had settled over the province long before the first protest.
2. There's a pervasive smell of burnt coffee in this office and nobody will admit whose fault it is.

### In the wild
**Pervasive computing** — the industry name for the idea that chips end up in the thermostat, the doorbell, the watch, the car, until computing is *in* everything rather than parked on a desk. The term was chosen for exactly the saturation sense.

### Where it comes from
Latin **per-** (*through*) + **vādere** (*to go*) → *pervade*, "to go through." The same **vādere** builds a tidy GRE set: **invade** (go *in*), **evade** (go *out* of), and their adjectives **invasive** and **evasive** — so *pervasive* is simply the "goes right through it" member of the family.

---

# **preclude** *(v.)* — **prih-KLOOD**

### Means
To **make something impossible in advance** — to rule it out before it can happen.

The force is structural, not personal. A person *forbids*; a fact, rule, clause, or condition **precludes**. Nobody has to refuse you — the possibility simply never exists. That's why it lives in formal registers: contracts, medicine, policy. Two shapes to know: *X precludes Y* ("the injury precluded a comeback") and *X precludes someone from doing Y*. The stock GRE collocation is **preclude the possibility of**.

⚠️ **preclude ≠ exclude.**
- **exclude** = shut out something that exists (you're excluded *from* the party)
- **preclude** = shut out the option before it arises (the schedule precluded a party at all)

### Trick to lock it in
> **pre- + -clude** = *closed beforehand* — the door was locked before you ever walked up to it.

Because the closing happened in advance, no one ever had to turn you away — which is why *preclude* sounds impersonal rather than hostile.

### In sentences
1. Her prior work for the regulator **precluded** her from advising the firm on the merger.
2. Sorry — the 6 a.m. flight kind of **precludes** any chance of me coming out tonight.

### In the wild
Contract and policy boilerplate runs on it: *"Nothing in this Agreement shall **preclude** either party from…"* Once you notice it, you'll see it in every terms-of-service page and insurance clause you skim past.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae-** (*before*) + **claudere** (*to shut*) — literally "to shut in advance."

*Claudere* is a workhorse root: **include** (shut in), **exclude** (shut out), **seclude** (shut apart), **conclude** (shut off the argument), **recluse** (one shut away), **closure**, **cloister**.

# **predilection** *(n.)* — **pred-uh-LEK-shuhn**

*Also pree-duh-LEK-shuhn; both are standard.*

### Means
A **standing preference** — a liking for one kind of thing that you bring with you, already formed, before the particular choice comes up.

Weight matters: a predilection is **mild and habitual**, not a passion. You don't have a predilection for your spouse; you have one for corner tables, Belgian beer, or nineteenth-century novels. It's the most **formal** member of its family, and it often arrives with a raised eyebrow — writers use it for tastes that are slightly telling ("*a predilection for lost causes*", "*a predilection for younger men*"), so it can quietly editorialise while sounding perfectly polite. Being *pre*-formed is the point: it colours your judgement before the evidence is in.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Pre-di-LECT-ion** — you **elected** it before the vote was held.

The *-lect-* is the same "choose" as in *elect* and *select*, and the *pre-* tells you the choosing was already done — the leaning exists before the options are on the table.

### In sentences
1. The judge's **predilection** for precedent over principle was well known to everyone who argued before her.
2. He'll order the spiciest thing on any menu — a lifelong **predilection** for punishing his own tongue.

### In the wild
Doctors use it flatly, with no glamour at all: a disease "has a **predilection** for" a certain site — melanoma for the back, TB for the lungs. It just means *that's where it tends to turn up* — which is exactly the everyday sense, stripped of taste.

### Where it comes from
Latin **prae-** ("before") + **dīligere** ("to choose out, to prize"), itself *dis-* + **legere**, "to pick, to gather, to read."

That *legere* is a workhorse: **select**, **elect**, **eligible**, **collect**, **lecture**, **legible**. And *dīligere* — "to pick out lovingly" — is where **diligent** comes from; a diligent worker was originally a *caring* one.

# **rapacious** *(adj.)* — **ruh-PAY-shus**

### Means
Aggressively, insatiably greedy — seizing wealth, land, or resources, and taking what belongs to others.

*Greedy* is a private failing; **rapacious** is greed with power and teeth. The word implies a victim who has been stripped bare: rapacious landlords, rapacious conquerors, rapacious creditors. It sits at the top of the greed scale, carries a whiff of the predator, and is purely condemnatory — nobody has ever described themselves as rapacious.

⚠️ **rapacious ≠ voracious.** They rhyme and both mean "never satisfied," but:
- **voracious** = devours — food, novels, information (can be admiring: *a voracious reader*)
- **rapacious** = seizes — money, land, other people's things (never admiring)

### Trick to lock it in
> There's a **raptor** hiding in **rapacious** — it doesn't just want it, it takes it with talons.

Same root, same behaviour: the rapacious party comes down on something and carries it off.

### In sentences
1. The company's **rapacious** expansion emptied the delta of fish within a decade, and the villages with it.
2. Those resale sites are **rapacious** — they hoovered up the whole stadium in nine seconds and tripled the price.

### In the wild
**Velociraptor** — *Jurassic Park*'s star predator — literally means "swift seizer," same *rap-* root. A raptor snatches; so does a rapacious banker.

### Where it comes from
Latin **rapere** = *"to seize, snatch, carry off."*

That one root is a GRE goldmine: **raptor** (the snatcher), **rapture** (being carried off — by joy), **rapid** (originally *swept away* by a current), **ravenous**, and **surreptitious** (*sub-* + *rapere* = snatched from under you).

# **relish** *(v. / n.)* — **REL-ish**

### Means
**Verb:** to enjoy something greatly — with appetite, savouring it.
**Noun:** keen enjoyment, zest; also the pickled condiment.

*Enjoy* is quiet and private; **relish** shows on your face. It carries the sense of tasting the pleasure slowly, and it very often attaches to things other people would find unpleasant — a fight, a challenge, an enemy's discomfort. "He relished the argument" hints he was slightly enjoying himself at someone's expense.

Watch the negative form too: **"I don't relish the prospect"** is standard English understatement for *I'm dreading it*.

### Trick to lock it in
> **relish** rhymes with **delish** — and you relish what you find *delicious*, including a fight.

The rhyme keeps the *taste* metaphor front and centre: relishing isn't just liking something, it's savouring it like food.

### In sentences
1. He **relished** the irony of being lectured on modesty by the vainest man in the department.
2. Honestly, I don't **relish** telling her the flight's been cancelled.

### In the wild
The jar of **relish** you squeeze onto a hot dog is the same word — a thing added purely for savour. Every hot dog is a reminder that relish means *taste*, not just *like*.

### Where it comes from
Old French **reles** = *"aftertaste, what's left behind,"* from Latin **relaxare**, *"to loosen, let go."* Relish is the flavour that lingers after the food is gone.

That root also gives you **release**, **relax**, and **relay** — all about letting go.

# **satirical** *(adj.)* — **suh-TIR-i-kuhl**

*Also written satiric — same meaning, same stress: suh-TIR-ik.*

### Means
Using irony, exaggeration or ridicule to **expose folly, vice or stupidity** — usually in politics, institutions, or fashionable opinion.

Satire aims at a *target*, and it wants that target fixed or shamed: there's a moral engine under the joke. Sarcasm, by contrast, is personal and spoken — a jab at *you*, in the room, right now. A sarcastic remark makes the speaker sound sour; a satirical piece makes the speaker sound clever and a little dangerous. Note too that satirical describes the *work* far more than the person — an essay, a cartoon, a sketch show, a novel.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sat-IRICAL** hides **IRONICAL** — but irony with a target and a grudge.

Plain irony just says the opposite of what it means; satire points that irony at somebody who deserves it.

### In sentences
1. Swift's **satirical** proposal that the Irish poor sell their children as food was read, by a few humourless readers, as sincere policy advice.
2. The whole sketch was a **satirical** take on wellness influencers, and half the audience didn't realise they were the joke.

### In the wild
**The Onion** — headlines written straight-faced as real news precisely so the target is the thing being reported on. Every time someone shares an Onion piece and a relative believes it, that's satire working exactly as designed.

### Where it comes from
Latin **satura** — from *lanx satura*, "a full plate," a **mixed dish** of assorted food. Roman satire was a medley: a bit of everything, all of it skewering someone. Gives you **satire, satirist, satirize**.

🚫 **Not from *satyr*.** The Greek goat-man got attached to the word later by mistaken association; the two are unrelated, which is why the spelling is *sati-r-e*, not *saty-r-e*.

# **sham** *(n. / adj. / v.)* — **SHAM**

### Means
**Noun:** something passed off as genuine that is hollow inside — a fraud, an imitation.
**Adjective:** fake but dressed in the trappings of the real thing — *a sham trial, a sham marriage*.
**Verb:** to feign — *he shammed a limp to get out of drill*.

Where *fake* describes an object and *scam* describes an operation, **sham** attacks the **legitimacy of a form**: it fastens onto ceremonies, institutions, and procedures that go through every correct motion while meaning nothing. A sham election has ballots, observers, a result — and no choice. The word is always a verdict, never a description; calling something a sham is a small act of contempt, and the speaker is claiming to have seen through it.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Sham** rhymes with **scam** — the scam is the con being run, the sham is the hollow thing it hands you.

One letter apart, one step apart: the scam is the act, the sham is the empty object or ritual it produces.

### In sentences
1. The tribunal was a **sham**: the verdict had been drafted a week before the first witness was called.
2. He **shammed** a stomach bug all Monday, then posted a photo from the beach by two.

### In the wild
Newsrooms have a fixed phrase for it — **"sham election"** is the standard wire-copy label for a vote with one plausible candidate and a pre-known result. That collocation alone will carry the word for you.

# **skirt** *(v. / n.)* — **SKERT**

### Means
**Verb:** to lie along the edge of something (*the road skirts the lake*), to pass around it rather than through it, or — the GRE sense — to **avoid a subject or a rule by going around it**: *skirt the issue, skirt the law*.

Skirting is evasion that never looks like refusal. You don't deny the question, you don't break the rule — you stay *adjacent* to it and keep moving. That's the difference from **flout** (open defiance) and from **lie** (direct falsehood): someone who skirts the law obeys every letter of it while defeating its point, and a politician who skirts a question answers at length without ever touching it. There's also a live sense of dangerous proximity — *skirting bankruptcy*, *skirting the edge of legality* — where the word means "close enough to be worrying."

⚠️ **skirt ≠ shirk.**
- **skirt** = go *around* it (an issue, a rule)
- **shirk** = *dodge the work* you owe (a duty, a shift)

You skirt the question; you shirk your responsibilities.

### Trick to lock it in
> **A skirt hangs round the outside and never touches the middle.**

That's exactly what the verb does — circle the edge of the thing, never make contact with the centre.

### In sentences
1. The treaty's drafters **skirted** the question of sovereignty entirely, burying it under two hundred pages of trade schedules.
2. Stop **skirting** it — did you tell her or not?

### In the wild
**Skirting board** — the strip of wood running along the bottom edge of a wall, and the **outskirts** of a town, its outer rim. Both are the same idea in plain sight: skirt = the border of a thing.

### Where it comes from
Old Norse **skyrta**, "shirt" — *skirt* and *shirt* are the same word, split in two. The garment sense narrowed to the *lower, hanging part*, i.e. the hem, and from "hem" came the general meaning **border, edge** — which is where the verb lives.

# **sluggish** *(adj.)* — **SLUG-ish**

### Means
Moving, working, or reacting **more slowly than it should** — and lacking energy while it does.

Slowness on its own is neutral; **sluggish** is slowness read as a *symptom*. A sluggish engine needs servicing, a sluggish market worries investors, a sluggish student worries a teacher. Nobody chooses to be sluggish, and nothing desirable is ever described that way — the word quietly diagnoses a fault, whether that's fatigue, congestion, or underperformance. It's mild, everyday criticism rather than contempt: closer to *underpowered* than to *lazy*.

### Trick to lock it in
> A **slow** Sunday sounds lovely. A **sluggish** Sunday sounds like you're coming down with something.

Swap the words in any sentence and *sluggish* always adds the sense that something is wrong.

### In sentences
1. Trade across the region remained **sluggish** for a third consecutive quarter, defying every forecast of recovery.
2. My laptop's been so **sluggish** since the update that I've started making tea while it boots.

### In the wild
Business news runs on this word — "**sluggish** growth," "**sluggish** demand," "a **sluggish** start to the quarter." It's the standard polite way to say *bad, but not yet a crisis*.

### Where it comes from
From **slug**, which in Middle English (*slugge*) meant a **lazy person** — the slimy garden mollusc was named *after* the insult, not the other way round. Same root gives you **sluggard**, an old word for a habitual idler.

# **spartan** *(adj.)* — **SPAR-tuhn**

### Means
Bare, plain, without comfort or ornament — and, of a person or regime, marked by hardiness and strict self-denial.

The key is *chosen* deprivation. **Meagre** and **destitute** mean you had no choice; **spartan** implies discipline behind the bareness — a soldier's bunk, a training camp, a monk's cell, a designer's stripped-back room. It can be quiet praise (tough, unfussy) or a dry complaint (there was nothing to eat and one thin blanket), and the same sentence often carries both.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Spar**tan = **spare** — spare by choice, not by poverty.

The first syllable hands you *spare*, and the Spartan soldier behind the word supplies the missing half: the bareness is a discipline, not a misfortune.

### In sentences
1. The retreat's accommodation was deliberately **spartan**: a cot, a washbasin, and a single high window.
2. Her fridge is **spartan** — mustard, half a lemon, and a very old soy sauce.

### In the wild
The film **300** — the Spartans who fight in nothing but a cloak and a shield are the literal source of the adjective. Same idea sells the **Spartan Race**: mud, no comfort, on purpose.

### Where it comes from
From **Sparta**, the Greek city-state that raised its boys in barracks on stolen food and black broth. The word is an eponym doing exactly what it says.

Bonus GRE word from the same place: Sparta sat in the region of **Laconia**, and the Spartans' famously clipped speech gives us **laconic** — using very few words. (Asked to surrender, they reportedly replied: *"If."*)

# **truculent** *(adj.)* — **TRUK-yuh-luhnt**

*First syllable is truck, not "true" — never TROO-kyuh-lent.*

### Means
**Aggressively defiant and hostile** — scowling, bristling, obviously spoiling for a fight. Of speech or writing: savagely harsh, out to wound.

Anger alone isn't enough. *Truculent* describes a **settled disposition**, not a flare-up: the person is sullen *and* combative at once, radiating a dare. It also carries a whiff of the subordinate resisting authority — the truculent teenager, the truculent witness, the truculent shop steward. Where **belligerent** means actively attacking you, truculent means glowering at you, waiting for an excuse.

### Trick to lock it in
> **TRUCK**-ulent — he comes at you like a truck, and he's already revving.

The hidden *truck* gives you the aggression and the sheer bulk of the mood — heavy, oncoming, daring you not to move.

### In sentences
1. The witness grew steadily more **truculent**, answering each question with a question of his own.
2. One coffee before nine and he's fine; without it he's **truculent** all morning.

### Where it comes from
Latin **trux, trucis** = *"fierce, savage, grim"* — used of wild beasts and of faces.

🚫 **Nothing to do with *truce*.** That's Old English *trēow*, "pledge, good faith" — the exact opposite mood. The lookalike is pure coincidence.
