# **censor** *(v. / n.)* — **SEN-ser**
*(Sounds exactly like* sensor *— no audible difference.)*

### Means
**Verb:** to inspect material and **cut out or suppress** whatever an authority finds objectionable — obscene, seditious, or secret.
**Noun:** the official who does the cutting.

Censoring is **suppression by power** — a state, a studio, a school board, an army. It's not editing for quality; it's removal for control. The content is *made to disappear*.

⚠️ **The GRE trap: censor ≠ censure.**
- **censor** = *delete* the message
- **censure** = *formally condemn* the person for it

A senator can be censured. A senator's speech can be censored.

### Trick to lock it in
> **Cens*or* is an agent, like edit*or* — a man who cuts. Cens*ure* is like in*jure* — it wounds a reputation.**

The censor deletes what you said; the censure is the public scolding for having said it.

### In sentences
1. Wartime officials **censored** the soldiers' letters, cutting place names out of the page before the mail went home.
2. She **censored** herself mid-sentence, remembering that her boss was standing right behind her.

### In the wild
Every time Spotify offers you the **"Clean"** version next to the *Explicit* one, you're looking at a censored track — the same song with words surgically removed.

### Where it comes from
Latin **censēre** = *"to assess, to judge."* The Roman **censor** was a magistrate with two jobs: take the **census**, and police public morals — he could strike your name off the rolls for bad conduct.

That one root gives you a whole family: **census** (counting), **censor** (suppressing), **censure** (condemning), **censorious** (fault-finding).

🚫 **Not *sensor*.** That's Latin *sentīre*, "to feel" — *sense, sentiment, sentient, consent*. Identical sound, unrelated word.

## Why this one worked

- **A ⚠️ trap block inside "Means"** when a near-identical word is the real exam risk
  (*censor / censure*). This is different from the end-of-doc synonym groups — it is a
  false-friend warning and belongs on the card.
- **Etymology that generalises**: one root (*censēre*) unlocked four words. That is what
  earns the section. Compare *calamitous*, where the etymology was transparent and boring,
  so the section was dropped entirely.
- **Trick used a suffix pattern, not etymology** — *cens-or* like *edit-or*, *cens-ure*
  like *in-jure*. Vary the mechanism across words.
- **In the wild was daily-life, not trivia** — the Spotify Clean/Explicit toggle, something
  they touch every day, rather than an obscure historical reference.
